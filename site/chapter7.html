Mental Health

Toward a Better Psychology

Chapter 7~ 55 min read

On the last day of my college economics course, my professor stood at the front of the auditorium.

“All semester, I have taught you how economics can make you better off. Today, I am going to teach you how it can make you worse off.”

That lecture changed my life.

For a full semester, my professor had taught us the power of incentives, trade-offs, comparative statics, and cost-benefit analysis. Then, on the final day, he pulled the rug out from under us. Economic models depend on simplifying assumptions, he explained, and those assumptions sometimes conceal as much as they reveal. Economics is not a religion. It can help make people materially better off, but it has much less to say about matters of the soul.

I think I heard every serious criticism of the discipline that day, and I will always be grateful.

That lecture taught me that mature disciplines do not merely advertise their discoveries. They also teach their practitioners where their models fail, what their methods cannot establish, and how their recommendations can backfire.

This chapter offers teachers a similar education about psychology.

We are regularly told what psychological research requires of us. We are told much less often where the field is uncertain, divided, or simply wrong.

Nowhere are the stakes clearer than in the youth mental-health crisis.

The generation entering classrooms now is the most therapy-aware, diagnosis-literate, and mental-health-conscious generation in American history.

It is also in the middle of a mental-health crisis.

Over the same period that stigma declined, screening expanded, treatment became more visible, and schools invested heavily in psychological support, many indicators of adolescent distress worsened. Persistent sadness and hopelessness rose. So did several measures of suicidal thinking and behavior. The young people who know the most about mental health do not appear to be the healthiest.

Psychology did not anticipate this reversal, and it has not produced a satisfactory explanation for it. The field has theories: smartphones disrupted sleep and friendship; childhood lost independence and free play; academic and economic pressures intensified; communities weakened; diagnostic categories expanded; awareness changed how young people interpret ordinary distress; the pandemic made everything worse.

Each explanation captures something. None accounts for enough of the pattern.

The crisis therefore offers a useful case through which to examine both the limitations of psychology and the knowledge it still has to offer. What has the field gotten wrong? What does it not yet understand? Where might its concepts and recommendations unintentionally cause harm? And which of its findings should teachers trust?

Those are the questions of this chapter.

The big picture
Psychology has earned neither unquestioning authority nor wholesale dismissal. The mental health crisis is but one example of how the field of inquiry is both crucial and fallible. The field has identified useful mechanisms, developed treatments that reduce suffering, and clarified how avoidance, connection, and context can shape behavior. It also failed to anticipate a population-level crisis, often individualizes social problems, allows descriptive categories to harden into identities, hides value judgments inside technical language, and permits weak or harmful interventions to travel under the label “evidence-based.” Teachers therefore need calibrated trust. They are not therapists, but neither are they bystanders. They can distinguish distress from disorder, build belonging and predictability, preserve meaningful challenge while adding support, notice important changes, act on safety concerns, refer beyond their role, and scrutinize programs before adopting them. The task is not to choose between psychology and common sense. It is to use what psychology has earned, recognize where its evidence ends, and retain responsibility for moral and educational judgment.

Major concept 1 · What the field did not see coming

The Mental Health Crisis

In 2008, the financial system failed, and in a very public way. Major firms collapsed. Boards removed executives, governments convened inquiries, and legislators rewrote financial rules. Economists were forced to ask why so few of their models had made room for the disaster. The reckoning was incomplete—many powerful people escaped meaningful accountability—but the failure became a failure of institutions and ideas, not merely a collection of distressed individuals.

When the warning lights began flashing in youth mental health, psychology never held a reckoning that was equivalent in seriousness or scope. It expanded screening, awareness, treatment, crisis response, and school-based programming. Psychology became better at finding and naming troubled young people. But fundamentally, it never developed a satisfying account of why so many more young people were becoming troubled at once.

There are many possible explanations for the youth mental-health crisis. We will examine three broad families of explanation. They are not mutually exclusive, and none is fully satisfactory on its own.

The first argues that young people are responding reasonably to an increasingly difficult social world: economic insecurity, political instability, academic competition, discrimination, climate fear, and weakened communities. Call this the social-conditions hypothesis—or, less formally, the “late-stage-capitalism hellscape” hypothesis.

The second argues that some portion of the apparent increase reflects changes in recognition and interpretation rather than an equivalent increase in underlying disorder. Stigma declined, screening expanded, diagnostic language spread, and young people became more willing to identify and report distress. We can call this the recognition hypothesis. Its strongest version does not claim that suffering is imaginary; it claims that societies partly determine which experiences are noticed, named, and counted.

The third argues that childhood itself was rewired. Smartphones and social media disrupted sleep, attention, friendship, and social comparison at the same time that independent play, unsupervised activity, manageable risk, and face-to-face interaction declined. Jonathan Haidt is the most prominent advocate of this account, which we can call the rewired-childhood hypothesis.

Each explanation identifies something the others can miss. Each becomes misleading when asked to explain the entire crisis.

What data support that there is a crisis?

CDC surveillance shows that several indicators of poor mental health and suicide risk worsened across the decade from 2013 to 2023. In 2023, about 40 percent of surveyed U.S. high-school students reported persistent sadness or hopelessness, about 20 percent reported seriously considering suicide, and about 10 percent reported a suicide attempt during the previous year. These are survey estimates, not individual diagnoses. They do not tell us that 40 percent of students have a depressive disorder. They are nevertheless too consequential to dismiss.

The trend is also not one of uniform deterioration every year. From 2021 to 2023, persistent sadness or hopelessness declined modestly among students overall, while the other overall mental-health and suicide-risk indicators tracked by CDC did not change significantly. Two truths must therefore remain together: “There is no crisis” ignores the broad pattern, while “everything is getting worse every year” ignores real variation in the data.

The Social-Conditions Hypothesis

In 2023, journalist Taylor Lorenz offered a blunt explanation for the youth mental-health crisis. Young people were not depressed because of their phones, she suggested, but because they were growing up in a “late stage capitalist hellscape”—a world marked by economic insecurity, extreme inequality, a weak social safety net, pandemic disruption, and climate change.

Behind the inflammatory language is a recognizable psychological argument. It is astructural account, often expressed through the framework of thesocial determinants of mental health. Mental health is shaped not only by thoughts, traits, relationships, and diagnoses, but also by the economic, political, and social environments in which people live. From this perspective, widespread distress may be an intelligible response to widespread insecurity. The problem is not simply that millions of individuals have failed to regulate their emotions. It may be that the conditions surrounding people are producing more emotions that require regulation.

This account provides an important corrective to psychology’s tendency to individualize suffering. Therapy can help a young person cope with economic precarity, discrimination, community violence, or fear about the future. It cannot fix the economy, provide societal justice, or prevent suffering.

Inequality, insecurity, pandemics, and environmental threats are plausibledistal risk factors: broad conditions that may influence mental health through more immediate mechanisms. Such concerns are worth considering. Listing them, however, does not establish how much each factor matters, which young people it affects, through what pathway, or why adolescent distress rose when it did. In epidemiological terms, the argument identifies possible population-level exposures. It does not yet explain the timing, magnitude, or distribution of the change.

While many politically progressive commentators emphasize the need for social justice and better economic mobility, there is a version of this story that is equally appealing to the political right: Communities have weakened in many places, and institutions that once offered stability, belonging, or a credible path into adulthood may feel less dependable. The loss of religion, the deterioration of the family, and the fading morality of public spaces (particularly schools) are all a part of this narrative.

As a scholar-practitioner, your job is to start from a stance of humility: admit what you do not know. Then, seek for data rather than vibes. Let's model just such a process, albeit in a simplified way.

Could it be the pandemic? The Coronavirus outbreak belongs here as an amplifier, not an original explanation. It disrupted schooling, relationships, routines, family stability, and access to care, but the deterioration began before 2020. The pandemic intensified a crisis already underway.

The biggest problem with these hypotheses are chronology and geography. First, to the issue of timing. The deterioration in adolescent mental health began around 2012, not at the bottom of the Great Recession but during the recovery. The U.S. unemployment rate fell from 8.1 percent in 2012 to 7.4 percent in 2013, and then to 3.7 percent in 2019. The economy expanded throughout that period. By 2019, median household income had risen substantially and the poverty rate had fallen to its lowest recorded level since 1959. Yet adolescent depression, loneliness, self-harm, and suicide-related indicators moved in the opposite direction. The economy was improving while teenagers’ mental health was deteriorating. The Great Recession fails the timing test because adolescent distress rose as unemployment fell. The pandemic fails because it arrived nearly eight years after the reversal began. Social injustice can produce real suffering without explaining the distinctive timing of this crisis.

The political right offers a parallel social-conditions hypothesis. Perhaps young people are suffering because religion, community, and trusted institutions no longer provide the meaning, moral structure, belonging, and intergenerational support they once did. There is something here worth preserving. Spiritual well-being and some forms of religious participation appear to offer modest protection against depression, although the evidence is heterogeneous and the personal importance of religion alone was not associated with lower depressive symptoms overall in a recent meta-analysis. Religion can be a protective factor without being a complete explanation for the crisis.

Once again, the timing resists the grand narrative. Religious disaffiliation accelerated in the 1990s, well before the sharp mental-health reversal. Confidence in major institutions had also been declining for decades; Gallup’s average measure was 32 percent in 2010, 32 percent in 2012, and 32 percent in 2013—hardly the sudden break needed to explain a sudden change in adolescent well-being. Nor did every conventional marker of social health worsen: the 2010s had far lower rates of violent crime and teenage pregnancy than the early 1990s, even as youth suicide returned to comparable or higher levels. Declining religion and institutional trust may have weakened protective buffers. But a slow erosion stretching across decades cannot, by itself, explain a sharp international deterioration beginning around 2012.

Geography creates one more problem for the hellscape explanation. If data were to confirm that social conditions lead to worsening mental health, we would expect countries with good conditions to fare better than those with challenging conditions. Not only does this trend not appear in the data, the trend seems to be nearly the opposite (though these trends are highly variable by country. In PISA data from more than one million adolescents in thirty-seven countries, school loneliness increased between 2012 and 2018 in thirty-six of them, with the share reporting elevated loneliness nearly doubling. The rise was particularly visible across the Anglosphere and in the Nordic countries—societies that differ sharply in inequality, welfare provision, healthcare, gun violence, and social trust. More strikingly, national GDP and income inequality did not predict school loneliness, while higher unemployment was associated withlowerloneliness. A separate study of more than 900,000 adolescents in thirty-six mostly European countries found that declines in well-being were concentrated in the higher-income nations, although the size and direction of change varied considerably by country.

Now a caution: the safest conclusion is not that mental illness is a “first-world problem.” Children in poorer countries experience enormous psychological suffering, and the data needed to measure changes over time are far weaker there: a recent global review found that almost 90 percent of countries lacked nationally representative mental-health prevalence data for children. The narrower conclusion is harder to evade:material prosperity did not protect affluent adolescents, and material deprivation does not map neatly onto the timing of the crisis.America is a prominent case, but not a unique one. The same basic reversal appeared across wealthy countries with very different economic and political arrangements. Whatever changed was capable of crossing national borders—and appears to have traveled especially easily through rich, highly connected societies.

Ultimately, social conditions may explain why the tinder was dry, but it cannot be the lit match.

The Stigma Hypothesis

What if more young people are "ok with not being ok?" What if diagnostic criteria and removing stigma from mental health have led more adolescents to get improved health care?

Diagnostic practices really have changed. Stigma really has declined. Schools screen more often. Mental-health language became part of ordinary conversation. Students and families became more willing to disclose symptoms and seek help. Experiences once described as nervousness, sadness, shyness, stress, or a difficult period may now be interpreted through the language of anxiety, depression, trauma, or disorder.

Much of this is progress, but can it explain the full range of increases in mental health distress? Researchers have called the stronger version of this argument the prevalence inflation hypothesis: mental-health awareness may improve recognition of genuine problems while also expanding the range of experiences interpreted as mental illness. A related phenomenon, which we will discuss later, is the social contagion hypothesis: the idea that young people speaking more about mental health distress may spread just like a communicable disease might.

Why can't the destigmatization hypothesis explain the crisis? Because the crisis isn't just about feeling a little worse; it's about hospitalizations for self-harm. Changes in language and disclosure may account for some increase in reported symptoms and diagnoses. They are less capable of explaining converging changes in severe impairment, self-harm, suicidal thinking, and suicidal behavior.

The Rewired-Childhood Hypothesis

The rewired-childhood hypothesis argues that the structure of childhood changed in two connected ways.

First, smartphones and social media transformed young people’s social environments. Digital technology can displace sleep, fragment attention, intensify social comparison, expose young people to harmful content, quantify popularity, and extend peer conflict beyond the school day. A humiliating interaction that once ended when a student went home can now continue through the night before an invisible audience.

Second, the unregulated online world replaced a healthy offline world of play, challenge, and resilience, as childhood became more restricted. Over several decades, many children received less unsupervised play, independence, manageable risk, face-to-face negotiation, and meaningful responsibility. These experiences provide practice in solving problems, tolerating uncertainty, managing conflict, recovering from mistakes, and discovering that difficult situations can be survived.

Jonathan Haidt’s argument joins these changes together: a phone-based childhood displaced a more independent, play-based childhood. Young people became increasingly protected from manageable risks in the physical world while becoming increasingly exposed to social and psychological risks online.

This account has important strengths. Its timing overlaps with part of the deterioration. It proposes specific mechanisms involving sleep, attention, comparison, conflict, avoidance, and lost independence. It also explains why technology might matter even when the content on a screen is not obviously harmful: time online can displace activities that development requires.

But the conclusion has been rebuffed by serious scholars in part because it simply lacks the explanatory needed for the full crisis. Effects differ among users, platforms, activities, developmental periods, and social contexts. Young people who are already distressed may use technology differently, making cause and effect difficult to separate. Digital spaces can also provide friendship, identity exploration, information, and support—especially for students who are isolated offline. The U.S. Surgeon General’s position is appropriately cautious: the evidence raises serious concern, but important questions remain about which forms of use harm which young people under which conditions.

The loss-of-independence portion of the argument also requires restraint. Trauma, violence, chronic humiliation, discrimination, poverty, and overwhelming stress do not become beneficial because someone calls them “challenge.” Schools should not manufacture hardship. The defensible claim is narrower: development requires opportunities for agency and manageable difficulty, and a childhood organized entirely around preventing discomfort may carry costs.

The Field Has Suspects, Not a Solved Case

Each explanation suggests a different response. The social-conditions hypothesis directs attention toward institutions and environments. The recognition hypothesis demands more precise language and greater care in distinguishing distress from disorder. The rewired-childhood hypothesis emphasizes sleep, attention, face-to-face relationships, independence, and manageable challenge.

None can yet explain the crisis by itself. The harsh conclusion therefore survives scrutiny: psychology can identify mechanisms, associations, and plausible contributors, but it cannot confidently allocate causal responsibility. It can often say more about treating one anxious student than about why so many students became anxious during the same historical period.

Act Without Pretending the Case Is Solved

Teachers do not need a final theory of the crisis to make defensible choices. The leading explanations overlap around several practical protections: connection, sleep, attention, agency, competence, precise language, predictable help, and access to qualified care.

Major concept 2 · Real Help Exists. So does decoration.

Evidence-Based Mental Health Practice

There is some good news: some mental-health help genuinely works. Cognitive-behavioral approaches produce real, replicated, average benefits for youth anxiety and depression. Treatment matched to a real need and delivered by someone trained to deliver it reduces suffering that would otherwise have continued.

The difficulty is not thatnothingworks; it is that the market for mental health treatments is far larger than only the things that show serious evidence of being effective:worksandfor saleare printed in the same font, and rarely do markets come with enough information to tell which is which. Almost all of it describes itself as evidence-based. Some of it is. Some of it is a sensible theory that collapses when delivered universally, a real effect too small to justify its cost, or a questionnaire score that moved while nothing in a student’s life did. The phrase “evidence-based” is often used as glitzy advertising rather than serious claim.

What do we do about this problem? Fortunately, you've already got some of the tools. In Chapter 4 you built an identity around evidence-based practice: best available evidence, professional judgment, and student context, with the evidence hierarchy to rank what you are told, all culminating in the "scholar-practitioner" identity. Now we're going to apply it to mental health, which may well be the single noisiest domain you will encounter—the one where good intentions, real suffering, and commercial pressure are hardest to pull apart.

A Few Things That Work—and a Few That Don’t

The honest headline is that the strongest evidence in youth mental health is narrow and specific, not broad and universal. Cognitive-behavioral therapy has repeatedly outperformed control conditions for anxiety and depression in young people. Exposure-based approaches help with anxiety; behavioral activation and CBT help with depression. What these share is telling: a specific problem, a specific set of skills, real practice, and a trained person delivering it to someone who actually needs it. Help that is targeted and practiced tends to work.

Now the uncomfortable half. Some of the most emotionally compelling programs in the history of youth intervention do not work, and a few make things worse.Scared Straight, which brought at-risk teenagers into prisons to frighten them away from crime, was beloved, widely copied, and the subject of an award-winning documentary. When researchers finally ran randomized trials, the programsincreasedoffending rather than reducing it. After a school tragedy, single-sessiondebriefingsthat walk students through the traumatic event feel obviously humane; the evidence is that they do not prevent post-traumatic stress and may raise the risk of it. One-off assemblies and awareness weeks feel like doing something, and mostly do little.

The pattern is worth naming. The interventions that backfire tend to be universal rather than matched to need, one-shot rather than practiced, and emotionally powerful rather than tested. A moving story and a good intention are not evidence—and occasionally they are cover for harm.

What Effective Programs Have in Common

When a school program does help, it is usually not because of its brand. It is because of its design. The largest analysis of school-based social-emotional programs—more than two hundred programs and over a quarter-million students—found that the ones producing real gains shared four features, summarized as SAFE: they wereSequenced(skills built in a deliberate order),Active(students practiced, rather than just listened),Focused(time was actually set aside for the skill), andExplicit(the specific skill was named and taught). Programs with all four tended to help; vaguer “general awareness” programs did not. And even well-designed programs lost their benefit when they were implemented poorly.

That gives you a usable filter for the next program that arrives. Is it matched to a defined need, or sprayed at everyone? Does it build and practice specific skills, or raise awareness? Is there time and training to run it as designed, or is it a binder and a hope? You do not need to memorize a catalog of programs. You need to recognize the features that make any program worth the room it takes.

Evidence Must Survive Context

As Chapter 4 established, evidence-based practice is not obedience to a study; it is judgment informed by the best available evidence, weighed against professional expertise, local conditions, student need, feasibility, and cost. In mental health that judgment only gets harder, because the same intervention can be clinically useful, educationally inappropriate, poorly implemented, or simply unnecessary for a particular population.

Before adopting a mental-health practice, ask:

  • What outcome changed?Symptoms, attendance, belonging, functioning, discipline, grades, or a custom satisfaction survey?
  • Compared with what?No treatment, a waitlist, usual practice, or a credible alternative?
  • By how much?Statistical significance does not establish practical importance.
  • For whom?Do the study population, age, setting, and level of need resemble your students?
  • Who delivered it?A trained clinician, researcher, counselor, or classroom teacher with twenty minutes of preparation?
  • Did anyone independent replicate it?Vendor-funded evidence deserves scrutiny, not automatic rejection or acceptance.
  • What did it cost?Include money, class time, staff attention, opportunity cost, privacy, stigma, and possible unintended effects.
  • What happens when the program ends?A temporary activity is not automatically a durable system.

Build Conditions Before Buying Solutions

A mental-health-supportive classroom is not a miniature clinic. It is a classroom designed to reduce needless threat while preserving worthwhile effort. Students know what is expected. Help is available without humiliation. Errors can be corrected. Adults notice withdrawal. Relationships survive correction. Attention is protected. Meaningful difficulty is scaffolded rather than automatically removed.

These conditions are not a cure for mental illness, and they should not be marketed as one. They are the ordinary infrastructure that makes learning, belonging, help-seeking, and recovery more possible.

Programs may still have a role. A strong program can provide training, coordination, shared language, or a tested sequence of support. But a program should enter a functioning system, not substitute for one. No curriculum can compensate for an unknown referral pathway, chronic humiliation, incoherent expectations, or adults too depleted to notice students.

The final stance is neither anti-psychology nor credulous. Use what the field has earned. Question what it merely advertises. Treat every intervention as a claim about outcomes, not a declaration of virtue.

Major concept 3 · Challenge or Support? The answer is both

Avoidance vs. Resilience

A student freezes before a presentation and says, “I cannot do this. I have anxiety.” Two responses are immediately available.

The first is rescue: “Of course. You never have to present.” The second is indifference: “Life is hard. Get up there.” Rescue treats distress as proof that the demand must disappear. Indifference treats distress as irrelevant. One offers support without challenge. The other offers challenge without support.

The apparent conflict between compassion and rigor is false. Students need both.

Relief Can Strengthen Avoidance

Avoidance is compelling because it works immediately. When a feared situation disappears, distress falls. That relief reinforces the escape: the person becomes more likely to avoid the next time. The short-term success of avoidance can therefore become part of the long-term maintenance of anxiety.

This mechanism helps explain why exposure is central to established clinical treatments for many youth anxiety disorders. In treatment, exposure is planned, gradual, condition-specific, and delivered by qualified professionals. It is not surprise, humiliation, coercion, or the celebration of suffering. It creates opportunities for new learning: distress can rise and fall without escape, feared outcomes may be less likely than expected, and difficult situations can be survived.

Teachers do not conduct exposure therapy. They do, however, make daily decisions about ordinary academic demands. Those decisions can either preserve a supported path into participation or turn escape into the default response.

Support is not the same as accommodation

Support communicates: “I believe that this is difficult, and I will help you through it.” Accommodation can mean many things, including legally required and educationally essential access supports. The relevant question is not whether a change was made. It is what the change enables.

An accommodation is productive when it removes an irrelevant barrier while preserving meaningful participation. It becomes risky when its primary function is to guarantee that the feared experience never occurs. A student might present to a smaller group, rehearse in advance, use notes, receive a clear rubric, or complete several low-stakes speaking tasks before a larger one. Those changes preserve movement toward the goal. A permanent exemption from all speaking may instead allow avoidance to organize the student’s education.

The chapter’s central distinction
The problem is not too much warmth. The problem is warmth being expressed only as removal of challenge. Validate the person and the reality of the distress. Do not automatically validate the conclusion that distress proves danger or incapacity.

Resilience is neither invulnerability nor manufactured hardship

Resilience is sometimes presented as a trait possessed by unusually tough people. That framing lets environments off the hook. Adaptation depends on skills, relationships, resources, prior experience, meaning, and the severity and duration of the difficulty. A student who needs help is not thereby nonresilient, and a student who survives harm does not prove that the harm was beneficial.

Schools should not manufacture suffering. The aim is not more adversity. The aim is greater capacity for meeting the ordinary, meaningful difficulties that education and life already contain.

That is why support matters. A secure relationship makes exploration more possible, not less. Predictability frees attention for the hard task. Scaffolding makes the next step visible. Feedback turns failure into information. Revision proves that mistakes need not become verdicts. Challenge is the growth mechanism; support makes the challenge usable.

Preserve the Goal and Redesign the Path

When a student is distressed, first ask what part of the task is educationally meaningful and what part is unnecessary threat. The meaningful goal may be explaining an idea aloud. The unnecessary threat may be an unprepared performance before thirty peers. Keep the goal. Redesign the path.

Use rehearsal, models, clear criteria, smaller first attempts, planned pauses, choice among equivalent formats, and opportunities to try again. Coordinate required accommodations with the student’s support team. If anxiety is severe, persistent, or functionally impairing, connect the student to qualified help rather than improvising treatment.

A useful sentence is: “I believe this is hard, and I am going to help you take the next step.” The first clause is support. The second is challenge. The sentence needs both.

Outcome matters

Two interventions are compared. Full removal of a feared task produces a large drop in distress today but more avoidance six weeks later. Scaffolded participation produces only a small drop today but less avoidance six weeks later. Which intervention “worked”? Your answer depends on the outcome and time horizon. Immediate comfort and long-term recovery are not interchangeable.

Retrieval practiceA student with anxiety asks to be permanently excused from every presentation.▶

Rescue removes every presentation because distress occurred. Indifference forces immediate performance without support. Supported challenge preserves the speaking goal while coordinating appropriate accommodations and adding rehearsal, smaller audiences, clear steps, and gradual participation. A teacher scaffolds the classroom task and refers when clinical treatment is needed; the teacher does not conduct exposure therapy.

Major concept 4 · Students can't lean on you unless you stand on your two feet

Teacher Burnout

Every intervention in this chapter runs through a single instrument — you. Your attention notices the withdrawn student. Your steadiness holds the room. Your judgment decides when to scaffold and when to refer. And a depleted instrument does not read as depleted; it reads as impatience, a shorter fuse, a student who used to get noticed and now does not. So the first application of evidence-based practice is not to a program. It is to your own capacity to keep showing up. On an airplane you are told to secure your own oxygen mask before helping the person beside you—not because you matter more, but because an unconscious rescuer rescues no one. Teaching runs on the same logic and pretends it does not.

Because when sustaining the work gets hard, the explanations we reach for turn personal. You need better boundaries. You need more self-care. You care too much. You are not resilient enough. Or, in the harshest version, good teachers simply find a way to keep going.

These explanations convert an occupational problem into a character test.

Burnout Is About Chronic Workplace Stress

The World Health Organization classifies burnout as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition. It describes burnout through three dimensions: exhaustion, greater mental distance or cynicism toward one’s work, and reduced professional efficacy. The definition matters because “burnout” is not a synonym for every hard week, and it should not be casually used as a diagnosis.

DimensionWhat it meansWhat it may sound like
ExhaustionEnergy depletion that is chronic rather than the ordinary tiredness following demanding work.“The well is empty, and it is not refilling.”
Mental distance or cynicismPsychological withdrawal from the work or from the people the work serves.“I need everyone to stop needing something from me.”
Reduced professional efficacyA deteriorating sense that one’s effort produces competent or meaningful work.“Nothing I do makes a difference.”

Healthy boundaries and cynicism are not the same. Choosing not to answer email late at night can preserve the capacity to care tomorrow. Beginning to experience students primarily as burdens is a warning that the relationship to the work has changed. One creates a limit around care. The other signals that care is being consumed.

Personal practices matter, but they do not erase working conditions. Sleep, movement, relationships, recovery, and boundaries can make a teacher more sustainable. They cannot single-handedly repair impossible workloads, role confusion, unsafe environments, weak administrative support, or the expectation that a teacher absorb clinical needs without adequate systems. Telling a teacher to take a bath while leaving the demands untouched is not an intervention. It is a way of relocating responsibility.

Important distinction
Burnout can coexist with depression, anxiety, trauma, physical illness, or ordinary exhaustion, but it is not interchangeable with any of them. Persistent or severe symptoms deserve appropriate professional attention rather than self-diagnosis through a workplace label.

Structure Care So It Can Continue

One of the primary findings around workplace burnout is that it comes, in part, from caring too much. Should a teacher respond by deliberately caring less? No. The goal is to stop treating limitless availability as evidence of care. A great teacher pours everything he or she has into their work, and then goes home. And then, that same great teacher pours everything he or she has into family, friends, community, and joy.

Setting boundaries is a way of making the care you have sustainable. Start with the parts of the job that can be made more predictable. Establish communication windows: I will answer emails between time A and time B; I will call parents only during the hours immediately following school, and so on. Reuse and improve sound materials instead of rebuilding every lesson to prove devotion. Create classroom routines that reduce repeated decision-making. Document concerns and use referral systems rather than carrying every student problem alone. Protect sleep and movement as requirements for professional functioning, not rewards earned after all work is complete.

Note the importance of choosing a high-functioning school in the first place. Some challenges cannot be solved individually. A teacher cannot meditate their way out of chronic understaffing or set boundaries that somehow make an unsafe workplace safe. Peer support, administrative priorities, caseloads, planning time, role clarity, and access to qualified mental-health staff are organizational conditions. Naming them accurately prevents self-care from becoming self-blame. Note, too, that one of the best predictors of sustainable work is an effective administrator, and a strong relationship with that administrator.

A sustainable teacher also maintains evidence of efficacy. Learn to savor the successes. Burnout can narrow attention toward unfinished work and unmet need. Keep contact with completed work, student growth, and problems that were handled adequately—not as forced gratitude, but as a correction to an attentional system that records every failure and quietly discards ordinary success.

Statistical workout

A district tests a wellness app. Teachers assigned to the app report slightly lower stress than a waitlist group after eight weeks ,p= .03 andd= .15. The district calls the app “proven.” What does statistical significance tell you? What does the small effect size add? What outcomes, comparison conditions, attrition data, costs, and organizational alternatives would you want before adopting it?

Retrieval practiceA second-year teacher feels numb toward her most difficult class and calls it “finally setting boundaries.”▶

A strong response distinguishes deliberate limits from involuntary distancing. Not answering messages at night may be a sustainable boundary. Losing the capacity to experience students as individuals may reflect cynicism or mental distance and deserves attention. The response should examine both personal recovery and workplace demands rather than treating the problem as weakness.

Major concept 5 · Your lane is narrow, but it is not small

Tier I Intervention

One of the best things you can do for both yourself and students is to understand your role. You are not a clinician. You are not a therapist. Recognizing this keeps you safe from the overwhelm of carrying every student challenge, but it also means that students can get support from professionals when they need it rather than teachers who arewinging it.

Be wary of mental-health messaging quietly recruits teachers into clinical roles: recognize the disorder, deliver the intervention, monitor the symptoms, and manage the crisis. At the same time, be wary of the opposite message: teachers are not therapists, and as such, mental health is none of their business.

Both positions are dangerous. Teachers can build a classroom that is high-functioning, where learning happens, and where students meaningfully connect with each other. They are close enough to notice important changes. They do not thereby acquire the training, information, consent, or legal role required for diagnosis and treatment, nor should they be expected to shoulder such challenges.

Tier I Is Universal, Relational, and Bounded

In a multitiered system of support (MTSS), Tier I refers to universal practices available to all students. Tier II adds targeted support, and Tier III provides more intensive, individualized intervention. The exact system varies by school, but the boundary is useful: most classroom teaching belongs at Tier I. Clinical assessment and treatment do not. To put it another way, it is not the teacher's duty to clinically diagnose a student who is struggling; it is a teacher's duty to notice a child who is struggling and refer, and it is also their duty to build a classroom where students hear correct, evidence-based, effective messages about mental health.

The teacher’s sequence is straightforward:notice, check in, maintain the relationship, document, and connect.

  1. Notice change.Look for differences from the student’s usual pattern, not isolated moments that merely annoy or surprise you.
  2. Check in privately.Describe what you observed: “You have missed three assignments and seem quieter than usual. How are you doing?”
  3. Maintain ordinary relationship.Do not let concern turn the student into a case file. Continue greeting, teaching, and including the student.
  4. Document observations.Record specific behavior, timing, frequency, and impact rather than speculative labels.
  5. Connect through the school’s process.Use counselors, psychologists, social workers, nurses, administrators, families, and emergency procedures according to local policy.
Pattern you may observeHelpful Tier I responseReason to connect
Anxiety:avoidance, physical complaints, reassurance-seeking, freezing, distress around uncertaintyPredictable routines, clear steps, rehearsal, calm confidence, supported participationPersistent impairment, panic, school refusal, escalating avoidance, or safety concern
Depression-related change:withdrawal, loss of interest, missing work, irritability, hopeless languagePrivate check-in, reduced shame, continued inclusion, specific observationsPersistent decline, severe impairment, self-harm, suicidal thinking, or hopelessness

These patterns are not diagnostic checklists. The same behavior can arise from many causes. Missing work might reflect depression, anxiety, attention difficulty, family crisis, a learning problem, lack of sleep, or a class the student does not understand. Curiosity about function is safer than certainty about labels.

When safety is in question

Talk of suicide or self-harm must be taken seriously. The folklore that asking directly about suicide is not supported by the research, and the current recommendation is to ask pointedly: "are you considering suicide?" A calm, direct question can help identify risk. You will receive training as you enter schools on this, and you should follow your school’s safety protocol immediately. Do not promise secrecy. Do not leave a student alone when imminent risk is suspected. Activate the designated counselor, administrator, family, crisis, or emergency pathway required by policy and circumstance. In the United States, 988 provides crisis support, and 911 is appropriate for an immediate life-threatening emergency. Local procedures and laws govern the teacher’s specific duties.

Language that preserves the relationship
“This is bigger than what I can handle alone, and I care about you enough to bring in someone who can help.” Referral is not the abandonment of relationship. Done well, it is the completion of the teacher’s role.

Build Tier I Before a Crisis

Know the referral pathway before you need it. Know which person receives a concern, what information should be documented, how urgent concerns are escalated, and what happens after a referral. A protocol that exists only in a handbook is not a functioning system.

Build the universal conditions that make help easier to seek: dignity, clear routines, connectedness, predictable adult response, and freedom from public humiliation. CDC defines school connectedness as the belief that adults and peers care about students as people and about their learning. That sense of belonging is associated with better mental-health and safety outcomes. It is not therapy. It is part of the environment in which therapy, teaching, and development either become easier or harder.

What can a teacher do beyond knowing the referral pathway?

Warm, low-conflict relationships with teachers are among the most consistent correlates of student engagement: across ninety-nine studies and well over a hundred thousand students, positive teacher–student relationships showed medium-to-large associations with engagement and small-to-medium associations with achievement. Those are correlations, not proof of cause—but they point the same way as the experimental work. Proactive classroom management—clear expectations, predictable routines, consistent responses—has been tested in controlled trials: a meta-analysis of fifty-four intervention studies found small but statistically reliable gains across behavioral, social-emotional, and academic outcomes, averaging about a fifth of a standard deviation (Hedges' g ≈ 0.22), with the social-emotional emphasis contributing most. None of this is dramatic. All of it is the kind of small, durable edge a teacher actually controls.

Now, an effect that size is a population average—a nudge across a whole class, not a rescue for the one student in acute distress, who still needs the referral pathway rather than a better routine. You should build strong relationships, you should model good mental health, and you should preach the values of shooting down distorted thinking; but some students will still need a referral. Some interventions seem like they should work, but have yet to show serious evidence of efficacy. An example: teaching everyone CBT-style coping skills—has very thin evidence. Meta-analyses of school-based depression and anxiety prevention find only small effects immediately after the program (around g = 0.23 for depression, 95% CI 0.19–0.28; g = 0.20 for anxiety) that fade to roughly half that within a year, with universal delivery weaker than programs aimed at students already at risk, and study quality generally poor. Several recent large trials of universal prevention found no significant benefit at all, and a few hinted at small iatrogenic effects (meaning they might actively harm). The lesson is not "do nothing." It is that the universal layer earns its keep through relationships, structure, and belonging—cheap, durable, and low-risk—while the heavier clinical work belongs with the people trained and resourced to deliver it to the students who actually need it.

Observation or diagnosis?

Compare: “Jordan is depressed” with “Jordan has stopped sitting with friends, missed four assignments, and said that nothing matters.” Which statement belongs in teacher documentation? Why does the distinction matter for accuracy, stigma, communication, and referral?

Retrieval practiceA student stops submitting work, sits alone, and says, “It does not matter anyway.”▶

Notice and describe the change, check in privately, maintain connection, document observations, and follow the school’s referral process. Ask directly about safety when concern warrants and policy supports it. Do not diagnose depression, promise secrecy, minimize the statement, or manage a safety concern alone.

Major concept 6 ·The Case against psychology; and then, the reason to keep it.

Limitations and Capacities

This chapter has explained what to do with mental health, both your own and your students. But now it is time to return to how we opened: the critique of the mental health profession goes beyond missing the adolescent mental health crisis. There are things you can do, rooted in real evidence: take distress seriously without teaching fragility. Tell burnout apart from an ordinary hard week, and protect your capacity before you spend it. Understand how relief can quietly train avoidance. Combine challenge with support. Notice change, check in, document, refer. Ask a program to prove it works before you let it into your room.

But we can't stop there. The same field that produced those tools also produced the crisis it failed to see coming—and, before that, a long record of confident harm. Honesty about what psychology gives requires honesty about what it has cost.

Consider thelargeMYRIAD trial,a recenttest of agentle, sensible, well-designed idea: universal mindfulness lessons in schools. Across more than eighty schools and eight thousand adolescents, the program did not outperform ordinary school provision on its main outcomes. A good theory, delivered with care, at scale, that simply did not do what everyone expected.

We know what to do with MYRIAD: no practical significance, so we leave it out of our teacher toolkit.

But what do we do when an intervention activelyharms?

A Short History of Confident Harm

Consider several episodes, none the work of quacks. Each was advanced by credentialed professionals, published in real journals, and offered to real families as help. Notice two things: these are not ancient sins, they are recent ones. Second, notice that they are not neutral: the intervention meant to produce positive effects creatednegative ones. The term for this isIatrogenic harm— harm caused by an intervention meant to help. It is a permanent possibility, not a historical one. It requires no malice, only confidence outrunning evidence, and it is not confined to crude methods.

In the early 1990s,facilitated communicationpromised that nonspeaking autistic children could type fluent sentences if an aide gently supported their hand. Families heard, for the first time, what they believed were their children’s inner voices. Then researchers ran the obvious blinded test: show the child one picture and the facilitator another. The typed answer always matched what thefacilitatorhad seen. The messages—including, in some cases, accusations of abuse that tore families apart—were coming through the ideomotor effect, the same unconscious mechanism that moves a Ouija board. The technique was discredited by the mid-1990s. It is still sold today under new names.

Around the same time,recovered-memory therapyused hypnosis, guided imagery, and suggestion to help adults recover buried memories of childhood abuse. The theory was compassionate and the practitioners sincere. But memory is not a tape you rewind; it is reconstructive and suggestible, and the techniques reliably manufactured vivid, detailed memories of events that had never happened. The result was a wave of false accusations, shattered families, and lawsuits. By the end of the decade the specialized clinics had largely closed.

Further back—but not as far back as we would like—thelobotomysevered connections in the frontal lobes to quiet agitated psychiatric patients. Its inventor won the Nobel Prize in 1949. Its most famous American practitioner was still performing the procedure in 1967, sometimes with an instrument resembling an ice pick, on patients including children, before effective medications and long-term follow-up made the damage undeniable. It was not fringe. It was the celebrated frontier of its moment.

Another indictment of the field is one you are already aware of: the replication crisis. The ordinary, everyday results that fill the textbooks? In 2015, a collaboration of hundreds of psychologists tried to reproduce one hundred published studies from the field’s best journals. Ninety-seven percent of the originals had reported a clear positive result. Barely a third of the replications did, and the effects that survived were about half as strong. A substantial share of what psychology has confidently shown does not reliably reappear when someone checks.

Why These Aren’t Just Scandals

It would be comforting to file these as isolated failures of bad actors. They are not. They follow from how the discipline tends to look at human beings—and the same tendencies operate, in gentler and less visible forms, every day.

Psychologyindividualizeswhat may be social: it is often better at helping a student adapt to conditions than at asking whether the conditions are tolerable. Itscategories harden into identities—a diagnosis that begins as a useful description can become a self, until change feels like betrayal and ordinary variation looks like pathology. Itpathologizes the ordinary burdens of being human—grief, adolescence, fear before a real performance—until symptom language can no longer tell disorder from pain. Itsaverages conceal the person: a treatment that helps on average still helps some greatly, some not at all, and harms a few. Itsvalues hide inside technical words—healthy,adjusted,functional, andresilientall sound descriptive and all smuggle in the questionadjusted to what, and for whose purposes?And its knowledgeenters a marketplacethat rewards enlarging problems and selling solutions.

The Tool That Caught It

Here is the turn, and it may be the most important sentence in this chapter: every one of those failures was exposed by psychology itself.

The blinded test that undid facilitated communication was designed by psychologists. The memory science that dismantled recovered-memory therapy was Elizabeth Loftus’s—a psychologist demonstrating, experiment by experiment, how easily a false memory can be planted. The follow-up studies that ended the lobotomy came from within medicine. And the replication crisis is not a leak that embarrassed the field; it is a project the fieldorganized against itself—hundreds of researchers volunteering years of unpaid labor for the sole purpose of finding out how much of their own literature was wrong.

That is the difference between a discipline and a doctrine. A doctrine can only defend itself. A discipline builds the instruments of its own correction—comparison groups, blinding, replication, effect sizes, harm monitoring, published disagreement—and then turns them on its own most cherished claims. The replication crisis is at once the strongest case against trusting psychology and the strongest case for it. The rot was real. So was the fact that the field is what found the rot.

Calibrated Trust

This is why the answer to psychology’s failures is not “stop listening to psychologists.” Refuse the field and you do not escape into neutrality; you fall back on folk theory, tradition, ideology, and marketing—none of which monitor their own harms, and all of which have their own long record of calling distress weakness, laziness, or bad character. The choice was never psychology versus common sense. It is between a body of knowledge that can find its own errors and a set of intuitions that cannot.

So the teacher’s stance iscalibrated trust: confidence matched to the quality, relevance, and limits of the evidence. Trust more when findings replicate, effects are meaningful, and the population fits. Trust less when a claim is monocausal, identity-defining, commercially convenient, or protected from disconfirmation. Separate a useful diagnosis from a total account of a person. Ask whether an intervention expands a life or merely quiets a symptom. Revise when outcomes contradict intentions.

But calibration is not the reason we bother. It is the discipline. The reason is underneath it.

The Purpose Underneath the Rigor

Strip away the failures, the fads, the oversold programs, and the averages that hide the person, and something stubborn remains. Psychology, at its best, does help. Not everyone, not completely, not always—but real suffering has been reduced by people who insisted on checking whether their help actually helped. That is a modest claim, and it is an honest one.

It is also in service of something that is not modest at all. The point of all this apparatus—the trials, the effect sizes, the blinded tests, the harsh audits of its own mistakes—is not to manage symptoms or defend a profession. It is to help human beings live fuller, freer, more flourishing lives. The rigor exists to protect the purpose.

You are not a clinician, and this chapter has been insistent that you not pretend to be one. But you share that purpose. The student in the third row, quieter this month than last, is not a case or a diagnosis or a data point. She is a person the whole apparatus ultimately exists to serve—and on most days, in most classrooms, you will be closer to her than any of it. Hold the field to account precisely because its aim is worth getting right. That is what my economics professor was doing on the last day of class when he taught us everything his discipline could not do. He was not attacking economics. He was taking it seriously enough to tell the truth about it. Take psychology that seriously, and you can use it without being used by it—in the service of students who deserve both your skepticism and your care.

Put the field on trial

Choose one claim from this chapter. Make the strongest case that psychology has clarified the problem, then the strongest case that psychological language has narrowed or distorted it. What evidence would change your confidence? What decision remains moral or educational rather than merely technical?

Retrieval practiceA colleague says, “Psychology failed to predict the mental-health crisis, its diagnoses are socially constructed, and some interventions cause harm. Teachers should stop listening to psychologists.”▶

The critique gets important things right: the field missed the crisis, diagnostic boundaries involve judgment, social conditions can be individualized, average benefits conceal variation, and good intentions do not prevent harm. It overreaches by treating limitation as uselessness. Diagnosis can reduce blame and guide support; many treatments show real average benefit; psychological mechanisms can correct intuition; and the field supplies methods for detecting error. Calibrated trust means matching confidence to evidence, context, incentives, uncertainty, and observed outcomes while retaining moral and educational judgment.

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

Mental-health support in a school context is not separate from teaching. It is a dimension of the same job. Here is what this chapter should change about how you do it.

  • Treat the crisis as real without pretending its cause is settled.
  • Use mental-health language precisely; do not turn every difficult emotion into a diagnosis.
  • Protect your own capacity with boundaries, recovery, systems, and an honest account of workplace demands.
  • Combine meaningful challenge with strong support. Redesign the path before removing the goal.
  • Notice changes, check in privately, document observations, and connect through established processes.
  • Know the safety protocol before a student discloses self-harm or suicidal thinking.
  • Require mental-health programs to demonstrate outcomes, magnitude, fit, feasibility, and acceptable costs.
  • Use calibrated trust: take psychology seriously without surrendering judgment about people, schools, or the conditions to which students are being asked to adapt.
Chapter synthesis
The crisis is real, but panic is not a plan. Psychology did not see it coming, and it has not yet produced a satisfying explanation for why so many young people began struggling at once. That failure deserves scrutiny. It does not erase everything the field knows. Students need adults who can take suffering seriously without teaching fragility, and teachers need sustainable ways to care without becoming consumed by care. The teacher’s task is to live inside that tension: skeptical without becoming dismissive, compassionate without turning rescue into care, and active without pretending to be a clinician. Psychology should inform judgment, not replace it. This chapter has examined the crisis, the help that survives scrutiny, the balance of challenge and support, the teacher’s own sustainability, the boundaries of the Tier I role, and the field’s limits and capacities. Chapter 8 turns from what psychology helps us reduce to what it might help us build: not merely the absence of suffering, but flourishing.
The crisis is real; single-cause explanations remain inadequate.
Burnout is an occupational phenomenon, not proof of weak character or a synonym for every hard week.
Support and challenge are not rivals. Resilience grows when meaningful difficulty is met with structure, dignity, relationship, and a usable next step.
The Tier I role is narrow but consequential: notice, check in, maintain connection, document, scaffold, and refer.
“Evidence-based” should begin an investigation. Mental-health practices must earn their place in a classroom.
The right response to psychology’s limitations is calibrated trust: use what the field has earned, inspect what it assumes, and retain responsibility for moral and educational judgment.
