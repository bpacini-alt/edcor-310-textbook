Agency and Self-Belief

What Actually Builds It?

Chapter 16~ 60 min read

If you went to elementary school in the United States in the last few decades, you probably remember the posters. Believe in yourself. You can be anything you want to be. Maybe you got a ribbon just for showing up.

Those posters have a history. In 1986, California created a state task force to promote self-esteem. Its 1990 report called self-esteem a kind of “social vaccine” against school failure, drug abuse, crime, and welfare dependency. The idea spread quickly, and “help students feel good about themselves first” became common teaching advice.

It was a hopeful idea, and an easy one to like. If students just believed in themselves, wouldn’t everything else follow?

But the results never showed up. In February 1987, the comic strip Doonesbury imagined the task force’s first meeting:

The research volume the task force itself commissioned, published in 1989, found little support for its central claim.

When psychologist Roy Baumeister and colleagues reviewed decades of studies in 2003, they found that raising self-esteem did little to raise grades. One later study even found that self-esteem messages made struggling college students do worse.

This chapter is about the beliefs we hold about ourselves: self-esteem, mindsets, efficacy, and more. By the end, our hope is that you’ll have a better sense of which beliefs about yourself actually matter, and where they come from.

The big picture

Beliefs about ourselves matter, but not in the way the posters promised. The beliefs that help students most are specific (“I can solve this kind of problem”), and they are built from experience, especially success at things that were hard. General good feelings, pep talks, and quick belief tricks do much less. Our faith teaches that agency is a gift from God; this chapter is about how students learn to use it well, and what teachers can do to help.

We start with the self-esteem movement and other popular ideas that didn’t hold up, then ask how much of what we do we really choose. The rest of the chapter covers how control is learned, the stories students tell about success and failure (and how our feedback shapes them), how to design work that builds real confidence, and how to give students choices they can actually use.

Major concept 1 · Feel Good First?

Self-Esteem

The self-esteem movement rested on a simple chain of reasoning: help students feel good about themselves, and better grades, better behavior, and better lives will follow. It was a testable idea, and researchers spent decades testing it.

The evidence never matched the hope. When Roy Baumeister and colleagues reviewed the research in 2003, they found self-esteem only modestly related to grades. Critically, most of that relationship ran from doing well to feeling good, not the other way around.

ResearchSelf-esteem emails that backfired

In 2007, Donelson Forsyth and colleagues sent weekly emails to college students who had earned a C, D, or F on their first psychology exam. Every email included review questions. Some students got only the questions. Some also got messages about keeping their self-esteem high. A third group got messages urging them to take personal control of their grades.

The D and F students who got the self-esteem messages dropped from an average of 57% to 38% on the final exam. The other groups held steady. The personal-control messages didn’t help either (we’ll come back to this in Concept 3: being told you are in control is not the same as learning that you are). With only about 15 students in each group, this study is a vivid example, not proof.

Self-esteem is not worthless. More recent work by Ulrich Orth and Richard Robins finds that it predicts some later outcomes, such as relationships, work, and mental health, though its effect on school achievement is small. They also found that beliefs about specific schoolwork predict achievement better than a general sense of self-worth does.

The movement’s mistake was the order. It tried to install a general good feeling first and expected success to follow. The research points the other way: specific beliefs, built from real success, are the ones that matter. The rest of this chapter is about how those beliefs get built.

ResearchTeachers too: does belief build success, or does success build belief?

Chapter 4 introduced collective teacher efficacy, a faculty’s shared belief that it can help its students succeed. It is often described as one of the most powerful influences in education. But most of the studies behind that claim measured belief and achievement at the same time, so they can’t show which came first.

When researchers have looked at timing, past success turns out to be a major source of the belief. In a study of 47 elementary schools, Roger Goddard found that schools’ past achievement explained much of the difference in their teachers’ collective efficacy. John Ross and colleagues, studying 2,170 teachers in 141 schools, found that earlier student achievement predicted collective efficacy, and that school practices giving teachers real ownership of decisions predicted it even more strongly.

Belief and success probably feed each other. But for faculties, as for students, the evidence suggests that success fuels belief more reliably than pep talks do.

Self-esteem was not the only popular idea of this kind that let researchers down. Several famous findings promised that a small change in what people believe or feel would change what they do. Here is what happened when other researchers tried to repeat them.

Notice the pattern. Most of these were small studies of a quick manipulation (a passage, an email, a pose) that promised a large change in behavior. That simply has not held up when subjected to rigorous empirical investigation.

But there is something teachers can do that does appear to hold up so far: experience, practice, and environment. There is no quick path to success, but there is a slow, careful, laborious one. That is the lesson from Unit 1 at work. One exciting study is a starting point, not a conclusion. It is also the main idea of this chapter.

Retrieval practiceA school wants to raise achievement by having every student start the day with an affirmation: “I am smart. I am capable. I can do anything.” Using what you’ve learned in this concept, what would you predict, and what would you suggest instead?►

The research predicts little or no effect on achievement. General good feelings follow success more than they cause it, and quick belief manipulations have a poor track record. Forsyth’s study even suggests that self-esteem messages can backfire for struggling students, and Orth and Robins note that people with low self-esteem can react badly to affirmations that don’t match how they see themselves.

A better use of those first minutes is a short, solvable warm-up on the day’s material. It gives students a real, specific success to start from, which builds the kind of belief that actually predicts achievement. (Concept 5 covers how to design for this.)

Major concept 2 · Are We NPCs?

Determinism

From the classroom · Mayleigh Barth

During student teaching, one of my students looked up from his math work and asked, “Miss May, when do you leave the school?” I told him I had about three weeks left and asked why. “Well,” he said, “you’re kind of just an NPC in my life. You’re just kind of there.” I looked right back at him and said, “Buddy, I’m pretty sure I’m the main character. Now let’s get back to math.”

In a video game, an NPC (a non-player character) follows a script. It walks the same path, says the same lines, and never decides anything. Mayleigh’s student meant it as a joke, but the question underneath it is serious. Are people running a script that their genes, their upbringing, and their circumstances wrote for them? Some of your students will seem to live that way. They do the minimum and seem sure that nothing they do will change what happens to them.

Philosophers have argued about this for thousands of years. Determinism is the view that every choice is the product of earlier causes. Psychology can’t settle that debate, but it can tell us how much of what we do is consciously chosen. The honest answer is less than it feels like.

Connecting to Chapter 2

In Chapter 2 you met confabulation: we make up reasons for our own behavior without knowing we are doing it. Chapter 2 also described the mind as an elephant and a rider, with fast intuition doing most of the driving.

One striking demonstration of confabulation is choice blindness. In 2005, Petter Johansson and Lars Hall showed people pairs of photos of faces and asked them to pick the more attractive one. On some trials, the researchers secretly swapped the photos and handed people the face they had rejected. Most people didn’t notice, and many explained why they had chosen a face they never chose. In short, our feelings of agency may well be partly confabulatory.

Habits run much of the day, too. When researchers had college students log what they were doing every hour, about 43% of their behaviors were habits, done almost daily in the same place, often while thinking about something else. Genes matter as well. A review of fifty years of twin studies found that, across thousands of traits, genes accounted for about half of the differences between people (Chapter 9 covers this in depth).

So in a real sense, we are all a little bit NPC. Much of what we do runs on autopilot, and we sometimes explain our choices after the fact.

But that does not mean we have no agency. Deliberate plans change behavior in ways researchers can measure. A review of 94 studies found that simple if-then plans (“If it’s 7 p.m., I’ll open my chemistry notes”) helped people follow through on their goals, with a medium-to-large effect. Habits themselves can be built on purpose. When researchers followed people adding a new daily behavior, it took a median of about 66 days for the behavior to become automatic, with a wide range from person to person. Conscious choices may not steer every moment, but they can set up the autopilot.

Common misconception“Neuroscience has proved that free will is an illusion.”►

This claim usually traces back to Benjamin Libet. In the early 1980s, he asked people to flick their wrist whenever they felt like it and report when they first felt the urge. Brain activity tied to the movement, called the readiness potential, began about a third of a second before people said they had decided. Many concluded that the brain decides first and the conscious mind just finds out.

Later work weakened that reading. Libet’s participants made a choice with no reasons behind it. In 2012, one research team showed that the signal may be ordinary background brain activity that happens to cross a threshold, not a hidden decision. In 2019, another team found that the signal appeared when people made arbitrary choices but was, in their words, “strikingly absent” when people made deliberate ones, such as which charity would receive $1,000.

Libet’s experiment tells us something about trivial choices and very little about meaningful ones.

All of this shows that we are partly on autopilot. It does not settle whether we are free. Philosophers are still debating that question, and many accept compatibilism, the view that a choice can have causes and still be yours, as long as it comes from your own values and reasoning. Psychologists are still debating how much our conscious choices drive what we do. We will come back to the question at the end of the chapter.

One distinction makes this easier to think about. Determinism says your choices have causes. Fatalism says the outcome will happen no matter what you choose. They are different claims. Even in a world where every choice has causes, your choices are among the causes of what happens next. Your effort is part of the chain.

Autopilot also has a history. The habits that run your day were built by earlier choices and repetition. In Chapter 2’s terms, the rider can’t steer every step of the elephant, but the rider can train it. A lot of agency works this way, upstream: in what you practice, the places you put yourself, and the habits you build.

This matters for the student who acts like an NPC. He is not a determinist who has thought through the philosophy. He is a fatalist about school. Somewhere along the way, he concluded that nothing he does here changes anything. That belief is the subject of Concept 3.

Retrieval practiceA classmate says, “Habits and genes explain most of what students do, so it makes no sense to hold them accountable for their choices.” Using this concept, what is right about that argument, and what is wrong with it?►

What’s right: a lot of behavior does run on autopilot. In one study, about 43% of daily behaviors were habits. Genes account for about half of the differences between people, and we sometimes make up our reasons after the fact.

What’s wrong: the argument slides from determinism into fatalism. Even if behavior has causes, a student’s choices are among the causes of what happens next, so accountability still makes sense. Habits were also built by earlier choices, and they can be retrained.

For teachers, the takeaway is to pay attention to the habits and environments students run on, not to give up on accountability.

Major concept 3 · Learned Helplessness, Reversed

Learned Helplessness

ResearchThe marshmallow test, revisited

In 2013, Celeste Kidd, Holly Palmeri, and Richard Aslin added one step to the marshmallow test. Before the test began, an adult promised 28 preschoolers better art supplies if they waited. For half the children, the adult came back with the supplies. For the other half, the adult came back empty-handed and apologized. Then each child got the familiar choice: one marshmallow now, or two if they waited.

Children who had met the reliable adult waited about 12 minutes on average, and 9 of 14 waited the full 15 minutes. Children who had met the unreliable adult waited about 3 minutes, and only 1 of 14 made it to the end. It is a small study, but the point is hard to miss. What looked like a trait (willpower) was partly a reasonable judgment about whether waiting would pay off.

The idea that giving up can be a reasonable response to experience has a long history in psychology. In 1967, psychologists Martin Seligman and Steven Maier gave dogs mild electric shocks they could not escape. Later, the same dogs were placed in a box where they could escape a shock by jumping over a low barrier. Many of them didn’t try. They lay down and waited for the shock to end. Dogs that had earlier been able to stop the shocks learned to jump quickly.

Seligman and Maier called this learned helplessness. The explanation seemed clear: the animals had learned that nothing they did mattered, so they stopped trying. The idea spread far beyond the lab, into theories of depression, poverty, and school failure.

Fifty years later, in 2016, the same two researchers looked back at what neuroscience had found since, and they concluded that the original explanation was backward. Passivity, they wrote, is not learned. It is the brain’s default response to prolonged bad events. What gets learned is control. When an animal discovers that its actions can stop a bad event, a region in the front of the brain switches on and holds back the automatic shutdown. Without that experience, the default wins.

Most of this brain evidence comes from studies of rats, and researchers have not yet shown the same mechanism in people. But the shift in thinking matters for teachers. If helplessness is the default and control has to be learned, then a passive student is not necessarily lazy or broken. They may never have learned that their actions work.

Worth remembering

Agency is a gift, but using it well is learned.

Students don’t learn to use their agency by being told they have it. They learn it from experiences where their own effort visibly works.

Psychologist Julian Rotter described the general belief that this kind of learning produces. People with an internal locus of control believe their own actions mostly shape what happens to them. People with an external locus believe that luck, fate, or powerful others mostly do. Internal locus is linked to more persistence and higher achievement. External locus is linked to passivity.

A student with an external locus is often reading their past accurately. A student who grew up in a chaotic home, or who has failed in school for years no matter what they tried, has real evidence that effort doesn’t pay off. That belief was built by experience, so it changes through experience. Reassurance alone is just more words from an adult, and the student has heard those before. Recall the personal-control emails from Concept 1: telling students they are in control did nothing.

Adults are not just on the receiving end of all this. They are also a big part of what teaches it. In Kidd’s study, nothing about the children differed between the two groups; the adult did. The reliable adult taught one group that waiting pays off, and the unreliable adult taught the other group that it doesn’t. Every classroom works the same way. When a teacher’s promises hold, when effort is noticed consistently, and when the rules mean the same thing on Friday as they did on Monday, students learn that their actions work. When those things are unpredictable, students learn the opposite, however warm the teacher is. You are one of the causes of what your students learn about control.

The same is true among teachers. A faculty that has watched students struggle for years can slide into the belief that “these kids” can’t be reached, and that belief shapes how hard teachers push. You met the opposite belief, collective teacher efficacy, in Chapter 4, and Concept 1’s research box showed that success tends to build it. When you join a school, its culture will press on you, but you can press back. Small, documented wins with real students are evidence your colleagues can see. You don’t have to be an NPC in your school. You can be one of the causes of its culture.

Retrieval practiceA student from a chaotic home has stopped trying in your class. She seems sure that what happens to her has nothing to do with her choices. A colleague says, “Just tell her that her effort matters.” Based on the 2016 reversal of learned helplessness, what is her teacher’s real job?►

The colleague is pointing in the right direction with the wrong method. If passivity is the default and control has to be learned, then telling her that effort matters will not do much. She learned her external locus from real experience, and words are not a different experience.

Her teacher’s job is to arrange situations where her own actions produce visible, consistent results: short tasks with a clear link between what she did and what happened, quick feedback, and a record of progress she can see. Like the children with the reliable adult, she needs evidence that effort pays off before it makes sense for her to invest it.

Major concept 4 · The Stories We Tell About Why

Attribution

Locus of control is a general belief. Attribution is more local: it’s about what happens in the moment. Every time a student gets a test back, they tell themselves a story about why they got that score. That story, called an attribution, shapes what they do next.

Martin Seligman, the same researcher behind learned helplessness, found that people tend to explain bad events in a consistent way, which he called their explanatory style. People with a pessimistic style treat setbacks as personal, permanent, and pervasive: it’s my fault, it will always be this way, and it ruins everything. For teachers, the question that matters most is simpler: is the cause something the student can do anything about?

A story that shuts students downA story that keeps them going
“I’m bad at this.”“The way I studied didn’t match what the test asked.”
“I’ll never get it.”“I didn’t get it this time.”
“I’m a failure.”“I struggled with this unit.”
“There’s nothing I can do.”“I can change how I prepare.”

The stories on the right still take ownership. Blaming the test may feel better, but it gives the student nothing to do next. The most useful explanation for a setback is usually something the student did and can change, like strategy or preparation. It keeps responsibility with the student, treats the setback as temporary, and points straight to a next step.

There is an important limit. Not every cause is controllable. A student who missed a test because her family was evicted does not need to hear that she should have prepared better. Good attribution work is honest: it helps students find the part they can change without pretending that everything was up to them.

Can attributions be changed on purpose? In 1982, Timothy Wilson and Patricia Linville worked with first-year students at Duke University who were worried about their grades. Half were shown survey results and short interviews with older students, all making one point: grades usually improve after the first year. The message reframed a bad start as temporary and fixable.

By the end of their second year, students who saw the information had raised their GPAs by about a third of a grade point, while the comparison group stayed flat. Only 1 of 20 had dropped out, compared with 5 of 20 in the comparison group. This was a small study at one university, and the dropout difference fell just short of statistical significance. But it shows the idea at work: a new story about why things went wrong, delivered at the right moment, changed what students did next.

Common misconception“Teaching students a growth mindset raises achievement.”►

An attribution explains one event. A mindset is a standing belief behind all of them: can ability itself change? Carol Dweck’s research describes two answers. A fixed mindset holds that ability is a set amount you either have or don’t. A growth mindset holds that ability develops through effort, good strategies, and help from others. The growth belief is true. Brains change with learning throughout life, and schooling raises measured intelligence.

The question is whether teaching students this belief raises their achievement, and here the evidence is modest. The strongest test, led by David Yeager in 2019, gave about 12,000 ninth graders across the United States two short online lessons. Among lower-achieving students, grades rose by about a tenth of a grade point. A follow-up found the benefit appeared mainly in classrooms where the teacher also held a growth mindset.

Two 2023 reviews of the research reached different headlines. One found a very small average effect that disappeared in the best-designed studies. The other found small benefits, mostly for lower-achieving and disadvantaged students. When a third researcher reanalyzed the first review’s studies with the second review’s methods, the two agreed more than their headlines suggested. Mindset lessons help a little, for some students, in some classrooms.

A growth mindset is also not praising “great effort!” no matter what happened, telling students that anyone can achieve anything, or dismissing real barriers as excuses. A poster or a single lesson is easily contradicted by a classroom that sorts students into permanent groups, praises only the fastest finishers, or treats mistakes as embarrassing.

Teachers also shape attributions directly, every time they respond to student work. Your feedback hands students ready-made attributions. When you tell a student why something went well or badly, you are offering a story they may adopt. That makes feedback one of the most direct ways a teacher shapes how students explain their own success and failure.

In 1996, two researchers combined more than 600 results from studies of feedback. On average, feedback improved performance. But in more than a third of the cases, feedback made performance worse. One of the clearest patterns was this: feedback that pulled attention toward the person, rather than toward the task, tended to help less or even hurt.

When John Hattie and Helen Timperley reviewed the research on feedback in schools, the most effective feedback had a few things in common:

  • It is about the work, not the person. “Your second paragraph doesn’t support your thesis” helps more than “You’re a good writer.”
  • It is specific enough to act on. The student can tell exactly what to keep doing or what to change.
  • It points to a next step. Good feedback answers three questions for the student: Where am I trying to go? How am I doing? What should I do next?

Praise aimed at the person (“You’re so smart”) was among the least useful kinds of feedback, mainly because it carries almost no information. It doesn’t tell a student what to do again or what to change.

Advice about praise has had a complicated history. In a famous 1998 study, Claudia Mueller and Carol Dweck found that fifth graders praised for being smart handled a later failure worse than children praised for their effort. “Never praise intelligence, always praise effort” became one of the best-known rules in education.

The rule turned out to be too simple. Later studies, including a close repeat of the original and a 2024 study from a lab that had earlier supported the rule, found no reliable difference between the two kinds of praise. Effort praise has its own problems: teenagers can read heavy effort praise as a sign that the teacher thinks they lack ability. And over-the-top praise (“That’s not just good, it’s incredible!”) made children with low self-esteem more likely to avoid challenges.

So calling a student smart is not dangerous by itself. The risk comes when generic praise for a trait becomes the student’s whole explanation for success. If “I succeed because I’m smart” is the story, a failure can only mean “maybe I’m not.” Good praise is accurate, specific, and about the work. “You checked each step against the example, and that’s why this one worked” tells a student exactly what to do again, and it gives them an explanation for their success that they control.

Retrieval practiceA student fails an exam and says, “I’m just not smart enough for this class. I’ve never been good at science, and I never will be.” What story is he telling himself, and why is it likely to make him give up? Then write two or three sentences of feedback you could give him that offers a more productive story.►

He is explaining the failure with something he sees as permanent (“never will be”), sweeping (all of science), and out of his control (how smart he is). If that is the cause, nothing he does will help, so giving up is the logical next step. It also reflects a fixed mindset about science ability.

Possible feedback: “Your answers on the definitions were solid. The questions you missed asked you to apply the ideas to new cases, which takes a different kind of studying. Let’s practice with new examples before the next test.” It is accurate, names what went well, and points to a cause he controls (how he studies) instead of a trait. If something outside his control also played a part, an honest version names that too.

Major concept 5 · Confidence Follows Success

Efficacy Engineering

Connecting to Chapter 12

In Chapter 12 you met Albert Bandura’s idea of self-efficacy: belief in your ability to succeed at a specific task. Bandura named four sources of that belief, and the strongest was mastery experience, actually succeeding at something that was hard for you.

Which comes first, confidence or success? In 2018, Kate Talsma and colleagues combined studies that measured both over time. Each shaped the other, but past performance shaped later confidence much more than confidence shaped later performance. In children, the effect ran only one way: doing well built belief, and belief did not predict later success.

That changes how a teacher should think about confidence. It is not something students must have before good teaching can begin. It is mostly a result of good teaching. This is exactly where the self-esteem movement from Concept 1 went wrong. It tried to install the feeling first. Specific beliefs earned through real success are the kind that last.

Worth remembering

Praise says you can. Success shows you can.

Encouragement has its place, but students trust evidence more than words. Success on something genuinely hard is evidence they can’t argue with.

If success builds belief, then the design of everyday work matters a great deal. On Seinfeld, George Costanza discovers a rule for social life: always leave on a high note. Researchers have found something similar in schoolwork. In 2024, Bridgid Finn, David Miele, and Allan Wigfield gave 570 third and sixth graders two sets of math problems. One set had ten difficult problems. The other had the same kind of difficult problems plus five easier ones. Even though the longer set took more work, students preferred it and were more willing to choose it again. Where the easier problems appeared made little difference. What mattered was having some real chances to succeed inside the hard work. This is one study, and the researchers are still testing when it works best.

This book calls that kind of design efficacy engineering: building work so students collect evidence of their own competence. Three moves do most of the work.

Three design moves
1
On-ramps. Start independent practice or homework with one or two problems most students can solve. Getting started is often the hardest part, especially for students with a history of shutting down.
2
Winnable items mixed in. Put some problems students can succeed at inside hard assignments and tests, and don’t end on a wall of the hardest items. The work stays challenging and still includes some wins.
3
Milestones. Break long projects into smaller pieces, each with its own feedback. A strong thesis statement or a finished outline is evidence a student can carry into the next step.

Efficacy engineering is not lowering the bar. A task that is too easy gives no mastery experience, because there was nothing to master. A task that is far too hard just produces failure. The aim is work just beyond what students can do now, with enough wins along the way to keep them going.

Mastery is not the only thing that shapes how capable students feel. They also compare themselves with the people around them. Researchers led by Herbert Marsh have found that students of the same ability tend to rate their own ability lower when their classmates are high achievers. This big-fish-little-pond effect has shown up across many countries and subjects. For teachers, it is a reason to make each student’s own progress visible, so the evidence they collect is about their growth and not only their rank.

Go deeperThe Great Plateau►

The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild opens with the player waking in a cave with no equipment. Before the game lets you explore its dangerous world, you must finish a few short challenges on the Great Plateau, a walled-off area with weak enemies and low stakes. By the time you leave, you have collected evidence that you can survive. Game designers call this a tutorial. Bandura would call it a sequence of mastery experiences.

Retrieval practiceTo keep the rigor high, a teacher builds a 40-question unit test made entirely of the hardest items from the unit. Many of her struggling students give up halfway through. Using remembered success and efficacy engineering, what would you change, and what would you keep?►

Keep the hard items; they are what make the test rigorous. Add some items most students can answer correctly, mixed through the test rather than saved for one spot, and make sure the test doesn’t end on its hardest stretch. The remembered success research suggests students will judge the experience more positively and be more willing to take on hard work again.

Before the test, she could also build milestones, such as short practice quizzes with feedback, so students arrive with evidence that they can do this kind of work.

Major concept 6 · Choice Needs a Frame

Autonomy Support

This chapter has been about what students believe about themselves and their choices. The last question is practical. How can a teacher give students real choices that help them learn?

Autonomy support means teaching that gives students meaningful choices, explains the reasons behind requirements, and takes students’ views seriously. Chapter 20 explains why autonomy matters so much for motivation. Here we focus on what it looks like in a classroom.

Choice does help, within limits. In 2008, Erika Patall and colleagues combined decades of studies on choice. Giving people choices raised their intrinsic motivation, effort, and performance on the task. The benefits were largest when people made a few choices, two to four, rather than just one or a long series of them.

Common misconception“Too many options paralyze people.”►

In a famous 2000 study, shoppers at a grocery store saw a tasting table with either 24 jams or 6. The big display drew more people, but far fewer of them bought anything. The “paradox of choice” became a popular idea.

When Benjamin Scheibehenne and colleagues combined 50 experiments with more than 5,000 people in 2010, though, the average effect of having more options was close to zero. Choice overload seems to show up only under certain conditions, such as when people know little about what they are choosing.

That condition is worth noticing, because it describes students learning something new. The problem is usually not the number of options. It is whether students have what they need to choose well.

That is where structure comes in. Structure means the things teachers do to make success possible: clear goals, predictable routines, worked examples, criteria for good work, guided practice, and feedback. Patall and colleagues reviewed studies of classroom structure from preschool through high school, including 46 studies that tested structure directly. When teachers added structure, students’ achievement and engagement both improved.

Two details matter. Structure was more strongly linked to achievement when teachers paired it with autonomy or emotional support. And structure that leaned on control, such as close monitoring, rewards, and punishments, helped less. Good structure guides without controlling.

Autonomy and structure are not opposites. A student given total freedom without the skills to use it may feel abandoned rather than empowered. The strongest classrooms tend to start with more structure and hand over more choice as students build competence, the same logic as scaffolding in Chapter 12, where support is gradually removed as competence grows. This also explains a common experience with inquiry learning (Chapter 11). Open-ended inquiry is engaging, but without enough guidance, that engagement doesn’t always turn into learning.

In practice, that looks like:

  • Bounded choices among a few real options, such as two or three topics, formats, or problem sets.
  • Choices that matter. Picking the color of the poster board is not autonomy.
  • Criteria and examples so students know what good work looks like before they choose.
  • Checkpoints with feedback along the way.
  • A reason when there is no choice, because a requirement students understand feels less controlling.

The gospel teaches a similar idea about agency itself. Lehi taught that “it must needs be, that there is an opposition in all things” (2 Nephi 2:11), and that without law there could be no meaningful choice (2 Nephi 2:13). Commandments don’t take agency away. They are part of what makes real choice possible, and they help people choose well. John Taylor reported that when Joseph Smith was asked how he governed so many people so well, he said, “I teach them correct principles, and they govern themselves.” Notice the order. The teaching comes first, and it is real work: people have to understand a principle and practice it before they can govern themselves by it. The Lord teaches the same way, giving “line upon line, precept upon precept, here a little and there a little” (2 Nephi 28:30). That is the pattern of this concept: start with clear teaching and structure, then hand over more choice as students show they can use it well.

Retrieval practiceA teacher wants to boost student agency, so she assigns a fully open-choice project: any topic, any format, due in four weeks. Two weeks in, most students have barely started, and several say they don’t know what she wants. What was missing, and how could she keep the choice while fixing the problem?►

She gave autonomy without structure. Students had choice but not the tools to use it: no clear criteria, no examples, no checkpoints, and no feedback along the way.

She can keep real choice while adding structure. She could offer a few strong topic and format options with room to propose others, show examples, share the criteria for quality, and set milestones with feedback (a proposal, a draft, a revision). The choice stays theirs, and the structure makes success possible.

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

Each idea in this chapter has a concrete classroom expression, one per concept.

  • Replace affirmations with evidence. Instead of telling students they are capable, give them a task where they can see it for themselves. (Concept 1)
  • Build habits, not just beliefs. Much of what students do runs on routine. Design the routines and environments you want them to run on, instead of relying on pep talks. (Concept 2)
  • Arrange actions that visibly work. For a student who has stopped trying, design short tasks where what they do clearly changes what happens, and make the result easy to see. (Concept 3)
  • Make the controllable cause visible. When a student explains a setback, steer the story toward a cause they control. In your feedback, skip “You’re so smart” and “Great effort” and name what worked or what to change. (Concept 4)
  • Build in on-ramps, winnable items, and milestones. Start hard work with something students can do, include some wins inside challenging tasks, and break long projects into pieces with feedback. (Concept 5)
  • Give bounded choices with structure. Offer a few real options, show what good work looks like, and add checkpoints. Hand over more choice as students build skill. (Concept 6)
Chapter synthesis
The posters promised that if students believed in themselves, everything else would follow. The research says belief does matter, but it gets built from the other direction: from experiences where effort visibly works, from honest stories about setbacks, and from success at things that were hard. Students who act like NPCs in their own lives aren’t stuck there. Agency is a gift, but using it well is learned, and teachers can arrange the experiences that teach it.
Feeling good first doesn’t work. The self-esteem movement and other belief shortcuts mostly failed when tested. Belief matters, but it follows success more than it causes it.
We are all a little bit NPC, but autopilot is not fate. Much of our behavior runs on habit, yet our choices are still among the causes of what happens to us.
Control is learned. Passivity is the default response to bad events we can’t control. Students learn control by experiencing their actions working, not by being told they matter.
The stories students tell shape what they do next, and your feedback writes much of that story. The most useful explanation for a setback is honest, changeable, and points to something the student controls.
Confidence follows success. Belief mostly comes after performance. Design work so students collect evidence of their own competence.
Choice needs a frame. Real choices motivate students, and structure is what lets them use those choices well.

So, are we objects or agents? Philosophers are still debating it, and so are psychologists. Science can describe the machinery of choosing, but it has not settled the question.

Our religious values are a distinct source of knowledge, and on this question they are overwhelmingly clear. Lehi taught his son Jacob that God created “things to act and things to be acted upon” (2 Nephi 2:14), and that people are free “to act for themselves and not to be acted upon” (2 Nephi 2:26). They “are free to choose liberty and eternal life … or to choose captivity and death” (2 Nephi 2:27). The Lord ties that freedom to responsibility: every person may act “according to the moral agency which I have given unto him, that every man may be accountable for his own sins” (D&C 101:78).

We are agents, and we are accountable. A teacher can’t give students agency; it is already theirs. But a teacher can help them learn to use it.

One more finding makes that responsibility personal. In one version of Stanley Milgram’s famous obedience experiments, two other “teachers” (actually actors) refused to keep shocking the learner. With their example in the room, only 10% of participants went all the way, compared with about 65% in the best-known version. When Jerry Burger tried a milder version in 2009, in which one person quietly hesitated, it made little difference. A clear, visible example seems to be what moves people.

Remember the difference between determinism and fatalism: your choices are among the causes of what happens next. That includes your students’ choices.

Be the example worth following.

  2. Praise says you can. Success shows you can.
