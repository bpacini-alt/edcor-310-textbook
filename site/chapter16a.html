Unit 3 · Motivational Psychology

Agency and Determinism

Agents or Objects?

Chapter 16~ 55 min read
From the classroom · Mayleigh Barth

During student teaching, one of my students looked up from his math work and asked, “Miss May, when do you leave the school?” I told him I had about three weeks left and asked why. “Well,” he said, “you’re kind of just an NPC in my life. You’re just kind of there.” I looked right back at him and said, “Buddy, I’m pretty sure I’m the main character. Now let’s get back to math.”

In a video game, an NPC (a non-player character) follows a script. It walks the same path, says the same lines, and never decides anything. Mayleigh’s student meant it as a joke, but the question underneath it is serious. Are people running a script that their genes, their upbringing, and their circumstances wrote for them? Or can they change it?

You will teach students who already act like NPCs in their own lives. They sit quietly, do the minimum, and seem sure that nothing they do will change what happens to them. Where does that certainty come from, and what can a teacher do about it?

The big picture

Agency is given, but acting as an agent is learned. Students learn it, or fail to learn it, from experience: from whether their effort has ever visibly changed what happened to them. That means teachers can arrange the experiences that teach it.

We start by clearing away two illusions: that agency is easy to boost by changing what people believe, and that we consciously choose everything we do. Then we look at how control is learned, the stories students tell about success and failure, what our feedback teaches them, how to design work that builds real confidence, and how to give students choices they can actually use. Telling students to believe in themselves does little. Building that belief through evidence does much more.

Major concept 1 · Are We NPCs?

The Illusion of Agency

Two popular ideas about agency turn out to be illusions. The first is that agency is easy to boost: change what people believe or how they feel, and their behavior will follow. The second is that we consciously choose everything we do. Both are wrong, and the truth sits somewhere between them.

Illusion 1: You can believe your way to agency

In 1986, California created a state task force to promote self-esteem. Its 1990 report described self-esteem as a kind of “social vaccine” against school failure, drug abuse, crime, and welfare dependency. The idea spread quickly. Schools across the country added self-esteem lessons, and “feel good about yourself first” became common teaching advice.

The evidence never matched the hope. Even the research volume the task force commissioned found that the links between self-esteem and the problems it was supposed to prevent were mixed, weak, or absent. In 2003, Roy Baumeister and colleagues reviewed the research and found self-esteem only modestly related to grades. Most of that relationship ran from doing well to feeling good, not the other way around.

ResearchSelf-esteem emails that backfired

In 2007, Donelson Forsyth and colleagues sent weekly emails to college students who had earned a C, D, or F on their first psychology exam. Every email included review questions. Some students got only the questions. Some also got messages about keeping their self-esteem high. A third group got messages urging them to take personal control of their grades.

The D and F students who got the self-esteem messages dropped from an average of 57% to 38% on the final exam. The other groups held steady. The personal-control messages didn’t help either, which is worth remembering for Concept 2: being told you are in control is not the same as learning that you are. With only about 15 students in each group, this study is a vivid example, not proof.

Self-esteem is not worthless. More recent work by Ulrich Orth and Richard Robins finds that it predicts some later outcomes, though its effect on school achievement is small. The movement’s mistake was the order. It tried to install the feeling first and expected success to follow.

Self-esteem was not the only idea of this kind that let researchers down. Several famous findings in the psychology of agency promised that a small change in belief or feeling would change what people do. Here is what happened when other researchers tried to repeat them.

What holds up better is slower: experience, practice, and environment. That is the lesson from Unit 1 at work. One exciting study is a starting point, not a conclusion. It is also the main idea of this chapter.

Illusion 2: You are the author of everything you do

The opposite illusion is that every action starts with a conscious decision. In the early 1980s, Benjamin Libet asked people to flick their wrist whenever they felt like it while watching a fast clock, then report when they first felt the urge to move. Brain activity tied to the movement, called the readiness potential, began about a third of a second before people said they had decided. Many people concluded that the brain decides first and the conscious mind just finds out.

Later research challenged that reading in three ways.

  1. The task. Libet’s participants made a choice with no reasons behind it. Flicking now or a moment later mattered to no one.
  2. The signal. In 2012, Aaron Schurger and colleagues showed that the readiness potential may be ordinary random brain activity that happens to cross a threshold. On this view, it shows when background noise tips into a movement, not a hidden decision.
  3. Meaningful choices. In 2019, Uri Maoz and colleagues compared arbitrary choices with deliberate ones, such as which charity would receive $1,000. The readiness potential showed up for the arbitrary choices. For the deliberate ones, it was, in the researchers’ words, “strikingly absent.”

Libet’s experiment tells us something about trivial choices and very little about meaningful ones.

Connecting to Chapter 2

Still, part of the challenge holds. In Chapter 2 you met confabulation: we make up reasons for our own behavior without knowing we are doing it. Chapter 2 also described the mind as an elephant and a rider, with fast intuition doing most of the driving.

One striking demonstration of confabulation is choice blindness. In 2005, Petter Johansson and Lars Hall showed people pairs of photos of faces and asked them to pick the more attractive one. On some trials, the researchers secretly swapped the photos and handed people the face they had rejected. Most people didn’t notice, and many explained why they had chosen a face they never chose.

Habits run much of the day, too. When Wendy Wood and colleagues had college students log what they were doing every hour, about 43% of their behaviors were habits, done almost daily in the same place, often while thinking about something else. Genes matter as well. A review of fifty years of twin studies found that, across thousands of traits, genes accounted for about half of the differences between people (Chapter 9 covers this in depth).

So in a real sense, we are all a little bit NPC. Much of what we do runs on autopilot, and we sometimes explain our choices after the fact.

Autopilot is not fatalism

Two words get confused here. Determinism says your choices have causes. Fatalism says the outcome will happen no matter what you choose. They are different claims. Even in a world where every choice has causes, your choices are among the causes of what happens next. Your effort is part of the chain.

Autopilot also has a history. The habits that run your day were built by earlier choices and repetition. In Chapter 2’s terms, the rider can’t steer every step of the elephant, but the rider can train it. A lot of agency works this way, upstream: in what you practice, the places you put yourself, and the habits you build.

This matters for the NPC student. He is not a determinist who has thought through the philosophy. He is a fatalist about school. Somewhere along the way, he concluded that nothing he does here changes anything. That belief is the subject of Concept 2.

Objects or agents?

So, are we agents, or objects being acted upon? Philosophers are still debating it. Many accept compatibilism, the view that a choice can have causes and still be yours, as long as it comes from your own values and reasoning. Psychologists are still debating how much our conscious choices drive what we do. Science can describe the machinery of choosing, but it has not settled the question.

Our religious values are a distinct source of knowledge, and on this question they are overwhelmingly clear. Lehi taught his son Jacob that God created “things to act and things to be acted upon” (2 Nephi 2:14), and that people are free “to act for themselves and not to be acted upon” (2 Nephi 2:26). They “are free to choose liberty and eternal life … or to choose captivity and death” (2 Nephi 2:27). The Lord ties that freedom to responsibility: every person may act “according to the moral agency which I have given unto him, that every man may be accountable for his own sins” (D&C 101:78). We are agents, and we are accountable.

If agency is a gift, why do so many students act as if they don’t have it? That is where psychology becomes very useful.

Worth remembering

We are all a little bit NPC. That doesn’t make us objects.

Retrieval practiceA classmate says, “Libet proved free will is an illusion, so it makes no sense to hold students accountable for their choices.” Using this concept, explain what is wrong with that argument.►

First, Libet’s experiment doesn’t show what the classmate claims. Participants made meaningless choices, the brain signal may be random activity crossing a threshold rather than a hidden decision, and the signal was absent when people made deliberate choices that mattered.

Second, even if all our choices have causes, that is determinism, not fatalism. A student’s choices are still among the causes of what happens to them, so accountability still makes sense.

The fair point in the classmate’s argument is that much behavior runs on habit and that we sometimes invent our reasons afterward. That means teachers should pay attention to habits and environments, not give up on accountability.

Major concept 2 · Learned Helplessness, Reversed

Learned Helplessness

ResearchThe marshmallow test, revisited

In 2013, Celeste Kidd, Holly Palmeri, and Richard Aslin added one step to the marshmallow test. Before the test began, an adult promised 28 preschoolers better art supplies if they waited. For half the children, the adult came back with the supplies. For the other half, the adult came back empty-handed and apologized. Then each child got the familiar choice: one marshmallow now, or two if they waited.

Children who had met the reliable adult waited about 12 minutes on average, and 9 of 14 waited the full 15 minutes. Children who had met the unreliable adult waited about 3 minutes, and only 1 of 14 made it to the end. It is a small study, but the point is hard to miss. What looked like a trait (willpower) was partly a reasonable judgment about whether waiting would pay off.

The dogs that stopped trying

In 1967, psychologists Martin Seligman and Steven Maier gave dogs mild electric shocks they could not escape. Later, the same dogs were placed in a box where they could escape a shock by jumping over a low barrier. Many of them didn’t try. They lay down and waited for the shock to end. Dogs that had earlier been able to stop the shocks learned to jump quickly.

Seligman and Maier called this learned helplessness. The explanation seemed clear: the animals had learned that nothing they did mattered, so they stopped trying. The idea spread far beyond the lab, into theories of depression, poverty, and school failure.

Fifty years later, reversed

In 2016, the same two researchers looked back at what neuroscience had found since, and they concluded that the original explanation was backward. Passivity, they wrote, is not learned. It is the brain’s default response to prolonged bad events. What gets learned is control. When an animal discovers that its actions can stop a bad event, a region in the front of the brain switches on and holds back the automatic shutdown. Without that experience, the default wins.

Most of this brain evidence comes from studies of rats, and researchers have not yet shown the same mechanism in people. But the shift in thinking matters for teachers. If helplessness is the default and control has to be learned, then a passive student is not necessarily lazy or broken. They may never have learned that their actions work.

Worth remembering

Passivity is the default. Control has to be learned.

Locus of control

Psychologist Julian Rotter described the general belief that this kind of learning produces. People with an internal locus of control believe their own actions mostly shape what happens to them. People with an external locus believe that luck, fate, or powerful others mostly do. Internal locus is linked to more persistence and higher achievement. External locus is linked to passivity.

A student with an external locus is often reading their past accurately. A student who grew up in a chaotic home, or who has failed in school for years no matter what they tried, has real evidence that effort doesn’t pay off. That belief was built by experience, so it changes through experience. Reassurance alone is just more words from an adult, and the student has heard those before. Recall the personal-control emails from Concept 1: telling students they are in control did nothing.

Teachers learn it too

The same thing happens to adults. A faculty that has watched students struggle for years can slide into the belief that “these kids” can’t be reached, and that belief shapes how hard teachers push. You met the opposite of this belief as collective teacher efficacy in Chapter 4. When you join a school, its culture will press on you. Like your students, you can learn control: through small, documented wins with real students. You don’t have to be an NPC in your school either.

Retrieval practiceA student from a chaotic home has stopped trying in your class. She seems sure that what happens to her has nothing to do with her choices. A colleague says, “Just tell her that her effort matters.” Based on the 2016 reversal of learned helplessness, what is her teacher’s real job?►

The colleague is pointing in the right direction with the wrong method. If passivity is the default and control has to be learned, then telling her that effort matters will not do much. She learned her external locus from real experience, and words are not a different experience.

Her teacher’s job is to arrange situations where her own actions produce visible, consistent results: short tasks with a clear link between what she did and what happened, quick feedback, and a record of progress she can see. Like the children with the reliable adult, she needs evidence that effort pays off before it makes sense for her to invest it.

Major concept 3 · The Stories We Tell About Why

Attribution

Locus of control is a general belief. Attribution is what happens in the moment. Every time a student gets a test back, they tell themselves a story about why they got that score. That story, called an attribution, shapes what they do next.

Martin Seligman, the same researcher behind learned helplessness, found that people tend to explain bad events in a consistent way, which he called their explanatory style. People with a pessimistic style treat setbacks as personal, permanent, and pervasive: it’s my fault, it will always be this way, and it ruins everything. Bernard Weiner added the dimension that matters most for teachers: controllability. Is the cause something I can do anything about?

DimensionThe question it asksA damaging answerA productive answer
LocusWas this about me or something outside me?“I’m bad at this.”“The way I studied didn’t match what the test asked.”
StabilityWill it always be this way?“I’ll never get it.”“I didn’t get it this time.”
GlobalityDoes this affect everything?“I’m a failure.”“I struggled with this unit.”
ControllabilityCan I do anything about it?“There’s nothing I can do.”“I can change how I prepare.”

The productive answers still take ownership. Blaming the test may feel better, but it gives the student nothing to do next. The most useful explanation for a setback is usually internal, changeable, and controllable, like strategy or preparation. It keeps responsibility with the student and points straight to a next step.

There is an important limit. Not every cause is controllable. A student who missed a test because her family was evicted does not need to hear that she should have prepared better. Good attribution work is honest: it helps students find the part they can change without pretending that everything was up to them.

Worth remembering

Teach controllable next steps without blaming students for conditions they did not choose.

Changing the story

Can attributions be changed on purpose? In 1982, Timothy Wilson and Patricia Linville worked with first-year students at Duke University who were worried about their grades. Half were shown survey results and short interviews with older students, all making one point: grades usually improve after the first year. The message reframed a bad start as temporary and fixable.

By the end of their second year, students who saw the information had raised their GPAs by about a third of a grade point, while the comparison group stayed flat. Only 1 of 20 had dropped out, compared with 5 of 20 in the comparison group. This was a small study at one university, and the dropout difference fell just short of statistical significance. But it shows the idea at work: a new story about why things went wrong, delivered at the right moment, changed what students did next.

Common misconception“Teaching students a growth mindset raises achievement.”►

An attribution explains one event. A mindset is a standing belief behind all of them: can ability itself change? Carol Dweck’s research describes two answers. A fixed mindset holds that ability is a set amount you either have or don’t. A growth mindset holds that ability develops through effort, good strategies, and help from others. The growth belief is true. Brains change with learning throughout life, and schooling raises measured intelligence.

The question is whether teaching students this belief raises their achievement, and here the evidence is modest. The strongest test, led by David Yeager in 2019, gave about 12,000 ninth graders across the United States two short online lessons. Among lower-achieving students, grades rose by about a tenth of a grade point. A follow-up found the benefit appeared mainly in classrooms where the teacher also held a growth mindset.

Two 2023 reviews of the research reached different headlines. One, by Brooke Macnamara and Alexander Burgoyne, found a very small average effect that disappeared in the best-designed studies. Another, by Jeni Burnette and colleagues, found small benefits, mostly for lower-achieving and disadvantaged students. When a third researcher reanalyzed the first review’s studies with the second review’s methods, the two agreed more than their headlines suggested. Mindset lessons help a little, for some students, in some classrooms.

A growth mindset is also not praising “great effort!” no matter what happened, telling students that anyone can achieve anything, or dismissing real barriers as excuses. A poster or a single lesson is easily contradicted by a classroom that sorts students into permanent groups, praises only the fastest finishers, or treats mistakes as embarrassing.

Worth remembering

A growth mindset message only works if the classroom makes it true.

Retrieval practiceA student fails an exam and says, “I’m just not smart enough for this class. I’ve never been good at science, and I never will be.” Map his statement onto the four dimensions. Then rewrite it in its most productive form.►

His statement is internal (“I’m not smart enough”), stable (“never been, never will be”), global (all of science), and uncontrollable (he treats smartness as something he cannot change). That is the pattern that predicts giving up. It also reflects a fixed mindset about science ability.

A productive version: “I didn’t study the right way for this exam. If I figure out what the questions were really asking and practice those, I can do better next time.” It is still internal, so he keeps ownership. But it is changeable, limited to this exam, and points to something he controls. If something outside his control also played a part, an honest version names that too, without letting it become the whole story.

Major concept 4 · What Your Words Teach

Feedback

Your feedback hands students ready-made attributions. When you tell a student why something went well or badly, you are offering them a story they may adopt. That makes feedback one of the most direct ways a teacher shapes how students explain their own success and failure.

Feedback helps, but not always

In 1996, Avraham Kluger and Angelo DeNisi combined more than 600 results from studies of feedback. On average, feedback improved performance. But in more than a third of the cases, feedback made performance worse. One of the clearest patterns was this: feedback that pulled attention toward the person, rather than toward the task, tended to help less or even hurt.

John Hattie and Helen Timperley later sorted feedback into four levels:

1 · The task
Is this answer right? “Your second paragraph doesn’t support your thesis.”
2 · The process
How was the work done? “You checked each step against the example.”
3 · Self-regulation
How can the student monitor their own work? “What could you check before you turn this in?”
4 · The self
What does this say about the person? “You’re such a good student!”

They found that feedback at the first three levels can be powerful. Praise aimed at the self was the least useful, mainly because it carries almost no information. “You’re so smart” doesn’t tell a student what to do again or what to change.

What happened to the praise advice

In a famous 1998 study, Claudia Mueller and Carol Dweck found that fifth graders praised for being smart handled a later failure worse than children praised for their effort. The finding became one of the best-known rules in education: never praise intelligence, always praise effort.

The rule turned out to be too simple. In 2019, Yue Li and Timothy Bates ran three studies with more than 600 children, including a close repeat of the original, and did not find a reliable effect. In 2024, a preregistered study from Elizabeth Gunderson’s lab, which had published earlier work supporting the rule, found no difference between praising children for being smart and praising them for their effort. Praising effort turned out to have its own problems. Teenagers can read heavy effort praise as a sign that the teacher thinks they lack ability.

Other findings point the same direction. Over-the-top praise (“That’s not just good, it’s incredible!”) made children with low self-esteem more likely to avoid challenges. Praise that doesn’t match what students know about their own work tends to be ignored or distrusted.

So calling a student smart is not dangerous by itself. The risk comes when generic praise for a trait becomes the student’s whole explanation for success and then they hit a failure. If “I succeed because I’m smart” is the story, a failure can only mean “maybe I’m not.”

What good praise looks like

The best praise is accurate, specific, and about the work. “You checked each step against the example, and that’s why this one worked” tells a student exactly what to do again. It also hands them a controllable explanation for their success, which is the kind of attribution from Concept 3 that keeps students going.

Worth remembering

Praise the work, not the worker, and make it true.

Retrieval practiceA teacher writes this on a struggling tenth grader’s essay: “You’re such a gifted writer! Great effort!” The essay has a strong opening but loses its argument halfway through. What is weak about this feedback, and how would you rewrite it?►

Both lines are aimed at the self, not the work, so they give the student no information about what to repeat or fix. “Gifted” also offers an uncontrollable explanation for success, and the student may know the essay has problems, which makes the praise easy to distrust.

A stronger version: “Your opening grabbed me because you started with a specific example. Around the fourth paragraph, the argument drifts. Try rewriting each paragraph’s first sentence so it connects back to your thesis.” This is accurate, names what worked, and gives a next step the student controls.

Major concept 5 · Confidence Follows Success

Efficacy Engineering

Connecting to Chapter 12

In Chapter 12 you met Albert Bandura’s idea of self-efficacy: belief in your ability to succeed at a specific task. Bandura named four sources of that belief, and the strongest was mastery experience, actually succeeding at something that was hard for you.

Performance builds belief

Which comes first, confidence or success? In 2018, Kate Talsma and colleagues combined studies that measured both over time. Each shaped the other, but past performance shaped later confidence much more than confidence shaped later performance. In children, the effect ran only one way: doing well built belief, and belief did not predict later success.

That changes how a teacher should think about confidence. It is not something students must have before good teaching can begin. It is mostly a result of good teaching. This is exactly where the self-esteem movement from Concept 1 went wrong. It tried to install the feeling first. Specific beliefs earned through real success are the kind that last.

Worth remembering

Believing in students is a feeling. Building their belief is a practice.

Remembered success

On Seinfeld, George Costanza discovers a rule for social life: always leave on a high note. Researchers have found something similar in schoolwork. In 2024, Bridgid Finn, David Miele, and Allan Wigfield gave 570 third and sixth graders two sets of math problems. One set had ten difficult problems. The other had the same kind of difficult problems plus five easier ones. Even though the longer set took more work, students preferred it and were more willing to choose it again. Where the easier problems appeared made little difference. What mattered was having some real chances to succeed inside the hard work. This is one study, and the researchers are still testing when it works best.

Three design moves

Efficacy engineering means designing work so students collect evidence of their own competence. Three moves do most of the work.

Three design moves
1
On-ramps. Start independent practice or homework with one or two problems most students can solve. Getting started is often the hardest part, especially for students with a history of shutting down.
2
Winnable items mixed in. Put some problems students can succeed at inside hard assignments and tests, and don’t end on a wall of the hardest items. The work stays challenging and still includes some wins.
3
Milestones. Break long projects into smaller pieces, each with its own feedback. A strong thesis statement or a finished outline is evidence a student can carry into the next step.

Not the same as easy

Efficacy engineering is not lowering the bar. A task that is too easy gives no mastery experience, because there was nothing to master. A task that is far too hard just produces failure. The aim is work just beyond what students can do now, with enough wins along the way to keep them going.

Where comparison fits

Mastery is not the only thing that shapes how capable students feel. They also compare themselves with the people around them. Researchers led by Herbert Marsh have found that students of the same ability tend to rate their own ability lower when their classmates are high achievers. This big-fish-little-pond effect has shown up across many countries and subjects. For teachers, it is a reason to make each student’s own progress visible, so the evidence they collect is about their growth and not only their rank.

Go deeperThe Great Plateau►

The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild opens with the player waking in a cave with no equipment. Before the game lets you explore its dangerous world, you must finish a few short challenges on the Great Plateau, a walled-off area with weak enemies and low stakes. By the time you leave, you have collected evidence that you can survive. Game designers call this a tutorial. Bandura would call it a sequence of mastery experiences.

Retrieval practiceTo keep the rigor high, a teacher builds a 40-question unit test made entirely of the hardest items from the unit. Many of her struggling students give up halfway through. Using remembered success and efficacy engineering, what would you change, and what would you keep?►

Keep the hard items; they are what make the test rigorous. Add some items most students can answer correctly, mixed through the test rather than saved for one spot, and make sure the test doesn’t end on its hardest stretch. The remembered success research suggests students will judge the experience more positively and be more willing to take on hard work again.

Before the test, she could also build milestones, such as short practice quizzes with feedback, so students arrive with evidence that they can do this kind of work.

Major concept 6 · Choice Needs a Frame

Autonomy Support

This chapter has been about choice: whether we really make choices, and how students learn that their choices matter. The last question is practical. How can a teacher give students real choices that help them learn?

Autonomy support means teaching that gives students meaningful choices, explains the reasons behind requirements, and takes students’ views seriously. Chapter 20 explains why autonomy matters so much for motivation. Here we focus on what it looks like in a classroom.

Choice helps, within limits

In 2008, Erika Patall and colleagues combined decades of studies on choice. Giving people choices raised their intrinsic motivation, effort, and performance on the task. The benefits were largest when people made a few choices, two to four, rather than just one or a long series of them.

Common misconception“Too many options paralyze people.”►

In a famous 2000 study, shoppers at a grocery store saw a tasting table with either 24 jams or 6. The big display drew more people, but far fewer of them bought anything. The “paradox of choice” became a popular idea.

When Benjamin Scheibehenne and colleagues combined 50 experiments with more than 5,000 people in 2010, though, the average effect of having more options was close to zero. Choice overload seems to show up only under certain conditions, such as when people know little about what they are choosing.

That condition is worth noticing, because it describes students learning something new. The problem is usually not the number of options. It is whether students have what they need to choose well.

Structure makes choice work

Structure means the things teachers do to make success possible: clear goals, predictable routines, worked examples, criteria for good work, guided practice, and feedback. Patall and colleagues reviewed studies of classroom structure from preschool through high school, including 46 studies that tested structure directly. When teachers added structure, students’ achievement and engagement both improved.

Two details matter. Structure was more strongly linked to achievement when teachers paired it with autonomy or emotional support. And structure that leaned on control, such as close monitoring, rewards, and punishments, helped less. Good structure guides without controlling.

Autonomy and structure are not opposites. A student given total freedom without the skills to use it may feel abandoned rather than empowered. The strongest classrooms tend to start with more structure and hand over more choice as students build competence, the same logic as scaffolding in Chapter 12, where support is gradually removed as competence grows. This also explains a common experience with inquiry learning (Chapter 11). Open-ended inquiry is engaging, but without enough guidance, that engagement doesn’t always turn into learning.

In practice, that looks like:

  • Bounded choices among a few real options, such as two or three topics, formats, or problem sets.
  • Choices that matter. Picking the color of the poster board is not autonomy.
  • Criteria and examples so students know what good work looks like before they choose.
  • Checkpoints with feedback along the way.
  • A reason when there is no choice, because a requirement students understand feels less controlling.

Agency needs a frame, too

The gospel teaches a similar idea about agency itself. Lehi taught that “it must needs be, that there is an opposition in all things” (2 Nephi 2:11), and that without law there could be no meaningful choice (2 Nephi 2:13). Commandments don’t take agency away. They are part of what makes real choice possible, and they help people choose well.

Worth remembering

Meaningful choice works best when students have the tools to use it.

Retrieval practiceA teacher wants to boost student agency, so she assigns a fully open-choice project: any topic, any format, due in four weeks. Two weeks in, most students have barely started, and several say they don’t know what she wants. What was missing, and how could she keep the choice while fixing the problem?►

She gave autonomy without structure. Students had choice but not the tools to use it: no clear criteria, no examples, no checkpoints, and no feedback along the way.

She can keep real choice while adding structure. She could offer a few strong topic and format options with room to propose others, show examples, share the criteria for quality, and set milestones with feedback (a proposal, a draft, a revision). The choice stays theirs, and the structure makes success possible.

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

Each idea in this chapter has a concrete classroom expression, one per concept.

  • Build habits, not just beliefs. Much of what students do runs on routine. Design the routines and environments you want them to run on, instead of relying on pep talks. (Concept 1)
  • Arrange actions that visibly work. For a student who has stopped trying, design short tasks where what they do clearly changes what happens, and make the result easy to see. (Concept 2)
  • Help students find the part they can change. When a student explains a setback, steer the story toward a cause they control, without blaming them for what they didn’t choose. (Concept 3)
  • Praise the work, specifically and truthfully. Instead of “You’re so smart” or “Great effort,” name what the student did that worked, or what to change next time. (Concept 4)
  • Build in on-ramps, winnable items, and milestones. Start hard work with something students can do, include some wins inside challenging tasks, and break long projects into pieces with feedback. (Concept 5)
  • Give bounded choices with structure. Offer a few real options, show what good work looks like, and add checkpoints. Hand over more choice as students build skill. (Concept 6)
Chapter synthesis
Mayleigh’s student thought his teacher was an NPC, a character following a script. Some students come to see themselves that way too. This chapter argued that they aren’t stuck there. Agency is given, but acting as an agent is learned, and teachers can arrange the experiences that teach it.
We are all a little bit NPC, but not objects. Shortcuts to agency through belief alone mostly failed when tested, and much of our behavior runs on habit. Science hasn’t settled the deeper question, but our faith answers it: we are agents, and we are accountable.
Control is learned. Passivity is the default response to bad events we can’t control. Students learn control by experiencing their actions working, not by being told they matter.
The stories students tell shape what they do next. The most useful explanation for a setback is honest, changeable, and points to something the student controls.
Your feedback writes much of that story. Accurate, specific feedback about the work teaches students what to do again. Empty praise teaches very little.
Confidence follows success. Belief mostly comes after performance. Design work so students collect evidence of their own competence.
Choice needs a frame. Real choices motivate students, and structure is what lets them use those choices well.
Lehi taught that people are free “to act for themselves and not to be acted upon” (2 Nephi 2:26). A teacher can’t give students agency; it is already theirs. But a teacher can help them learn to use it.
Seven ideas worth remembering
  1. We are all a little bit NPC. That doesn’t make us objects.
  2. Passivity is the default. Control has to be learned.
  3. Teach controllable next steps without blaming students for conditions they did not choose.
  4. A growth mindset message only works if the classroom makes it true.
  5. Praise the work, not the worker, and make it true.
  6. Believing in students is a feeling. Building their belief is a practice.
  7. Meaningful choice works best when students have the tools to use it.
