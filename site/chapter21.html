Executive Function & ADHD

The Brain’s CEO

Chapter 21~ 40 min read

Picture the CEO of a busy company. She’s sharp and good at her job, and she has an assistant who does a lot of quiet work. The assistant keeps her calendar, reminds her about the 2:00 meeting, and puts the right file on her desk before she needs it.

One Monday the assistant calls in sick, and the calendar app crashes. By noon the CEO has missed one meeting, walked into another without her notes, and snapped at a coworker who interrupted her. Anyone passing her office might decide she’s careless. But she’s the same person she was on Friday. The only thing missing is the help.

Now picture a sixth grader who never had the assistant in the first place.

When a student doesn’t start the assignment, loses the paper again, or blurts out the answer for the third time, a teacher has to decide what they’re looking at. Is this a student who won’t, or a student who can’t yet?

The big picture

Every brain has a management system that plans, remembers the next step, puts on the brakes, and shifts gears when plans change. When it works, nobody notices it. When it struggles, people see a student who seems lazy, careless, or defiant. This chapter follows one question from start to finish: when a student doesn’t do what we expect, is it a can’t or a won’t?

The answer shapes everything a teacher does next. A “can’t” calls for a different response than a “won’t,” and ADHD is one of the most common reasons a student can’t yet. The research also points to a practical approach. When the skill isn’t there, build the system into the room, then hand it to the student a piece at a time.

Major concept 1 · The brain’s CEO

Executive Function

Every school day asks students to do things that have little to do with the subject being taught. They have to remember three directions at once, wait their turn, start on their own, and switch from one activity to the next. The skills behind all of that are called executive function. It is the brain’s management system, the CEO from the opening story. It’s the part of the mind that organizes the person doing the reading and the math.

Executive function depends heavily on the prefrontal cortex and on the networks that connect it to the rest of the brain. Researchers usually describe three core skills (Diamond, 2013).

SkillWhat it doesWhen it struggles, you might see
Working memoryHolds information in mind while you use itA 3rd grader who finishes step one of the directions and has no idea what came next. A 10th grader who loses the start of a long question before reaching the end.
Inhibitory controlStops an automatic response so a better one can happenBlurting out answers. Grabbing a classmate’s pencil. Quitting a problem the moment it gets hard.
Cognitive flexibilitySwitches between tasks, rules, or ideasA meltdown when an assembly changes the schedule. Using last week’s math rule on this week’s problems.

These three skills work as a team. A student writing an essay has to keep the prompt in mind, ignore the conversation at the next table, and switch between planning, drafting, and fixing. A weakness in one usually shows up in the others.

The elephant and the rider offers another way to see this. Picture the slow, deliberate rider trying to steer the fast, emotional elephant. Executive function is much of what the rider has to work with. Inhibitory control is the rider pulling back on the reins. Working memory is the rider keeping the destination in mind. A young child, or a student whose executive function is struggling, is a rider with a weak grip on a strong elephant.

It develops slowly

Executive function starts growing in the preschool years and keeps developing through adolescence and into the twenties. You may have heard that the brain is “fully developed at 25.” Treat that number as a ballpark. Brain imaging shows the prefrontal cortex keeps maturing well into the twenties, but no single birthday marks the end. A 2025 study of brain scans from nearly 3,800 people found that the brain’s wiring keeps reorganizing in an adolescent-like pattern until about age 32, on average (Mousley et al., 2025). Brains also differ. Some settle earlier and some later.

These skills matter well beyond school. A study in Dunedin, New Zealand, followed about 1,000 children from birth to age 32. Children with better self-control, which leans heavily on inhibitory control, grew into adults with better physical health, fewer substance problems, stronger finances, and fewer criminal convictions. That held even after the researchers accounted for IQ and family social class. Children whose self-control improved over the years also did better as adults than their early scores predicted (Moffitt et al., 2011). Executive function can grow, and helping students build it is worth the effort.

StageWhat typically develops
Early childhood (3–5)Basic impulse control begins to appear. Children can follow simple two-step directions. Pretend play builds early flexibility.
Middle childhood (6–12)Flexibility improves, and children can stick with a goal longer. Planning and organization begin to form but still need adult support.
Adolescence (13–18)Planning and self-control grow, but risk-taking and impulsivity stay high, especially around peers.
Early adulthood (18 to late 20s)Decision-making and emotional control keep improving. Development tapers off gradually instead of stopping at one age.

This matters for expectations. A sixth grader who can’t keep track of a two-week project may not be choosing to fail. That may simply be what a sixth-grade brain can do without help.

Key aphorism

Kids do well if they can.

When a student keeps struggling, look first for a skill that isn’t there yet.

So before deciding a student doesn’t care, ask a simpler question: is this a can’t or a won’t? A can’t calls for teaching and support. A won’t calls for motivation. Each fix tends to fail when it’s used on the other problem.

About the Aphorism

Where it comes from: “Kids do well if they can” is the title of Chapter 2 of Lost at School (2008) by Ross Greene, a clinical psychologist who works with children with serious behavior problems. Greene sets it against a more common belief: kids do well if they want to.

What it means here: when a student keeps failing at something, start by assuming a skill is missing, not the will. That’s the “can’t” side of this chapter’s question, and for executive function problems it’s usually the right first guess. Doing well feels better than doing badly. A student who keeps doing badly may not know how to do anything else yet.

Where it stops: Greene goes further than this book does. He argues that rewards and consequences should be replaced with a collaborative problem-solving process. The evidence in Concept 4 doesn’t support dropping them. Point systems and the Daily Report Card are among the best-supported classroom tools for ADHD. And some students really do have a “won’t.” So this chapter borrows Greene’s starting assumption, not his whole program. Ask “can’t or won’t?” Start with “can’t,” and let the pattern tell you if you were wrong.

Retrieval practiceA fourth grader has three habits. She starts the directions but forgets the later steps. She shouts answers before being called on. She falls apart when an assembly changes the schedule. Which core executive function skill is most involved in each? What’s one way to support each one?►
  • Forgetting the later steps: working memory. She can’t hold all the steps at once. Repeating them louder won’t help. Writing them where she can see them does, because then she doesn’t have to hold them in her head.
  • Shouting answers: inhibitory control. The urge to answer arrives before the brake catches. A clear routine (“hand up, wait for my nod”), plus practice and specific praise for waiting, gives the brake a chance.
  • Falling apart at schedule changes: cognitive flexibility. Switching plans is hard for her. Warning her ahead of time and showing the change on a posted schedule make the switch smaller.

Notice that starting work on her own isn’t one of the three core skills. That skill, task initiation, draws on all three, which is why so many students get stuck right at the start.

Major concept 2 · When the CEO struggles

Executive Dysfunction

When executive function skills break down in a way that lasts, psychologists call it executive dysfunction. It describes a pattern, not a diagnosis. Almost everyone has some executive dysfunction now and then. Think of how hard it is to plan anything after a night without sleep, during finals week, or while you’re sick. That kind of breakdown is normal and temporary.

What makes it a real concern is the pattern. Clinicians look for three things:

  • It lasts: months, not a rough week.
  • It shows up across settings: at home, at school, and with friends, not just in one class.
  • It interferes: it keeps the student from doing things they otherwise could.

In a classroom, executive dysfunction can look like a messy backpack that never improves, work that never gets started, and poor time management even after it’s been taught. It can also look like big emotional reactions when plans change, or missing assignments that don’t match what the student actually knows. These are easy to read as laziness or defiance. Sometimes that reading is right. Often it isn’t, and the only way to tell is to look at the pattern.

Executive dysfunction can come from many sources. These include ADHD, autism, traumatic brain injury, depression, anxiety, learning disabilities, chronic stress, and poor sleep. That overlap has a practical upside. The supports that help students with ADHD, like written steps, predictable routines, and smaller chunks of work, tend to help many other students too, including students with no diagnosis at all.

A key distinction

Almost every student will show some signs of executive dysfunction at some point. The concern starts when those signs last, appear across different settings, and get in the way of the student’s life. One rough afternoon is not executive dysfunction. A year of daily struggles across every class is a pattern worth looking into.

A gospel lens: look on the heart

When the prophet Samuel went to anoint a new king, he was impressed by the appearance of Jesse’s oldest son. The Lord corrected him: “for the Lord seeth not as man seeth; for man looketh on the outward appearance, but the Lord looketh on the heart” (1 Samuel 16:7).

The Church’s guide for teachers, Teaching in the Savior’s Way, applies that principle to the classroom. It asks teachers to see learners the way God sees them, and it counsels, “Be careful not to make assumptions based only on what you see” (The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, 2022). The Church’s guidance for Primary teachers of children with disabilities makes the same point about behavior. It asks teachers to “pay careful attention to what is happening when challenging behaviors arise” (The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, 2023).

The research in this chapter arrives at a similar warning by a different road. Behavior is what we see, and the cause is often hidden. The scripture teaches a disciple to look past outward appearance. The research gives a teacher careful ways to do it: watch for patterns, check across settings, and hold off on the quick verdict.

Retrieval practiceYou’re on a seventh-grade team. A colleague says about a shared student: “He does fine in PE and plays video games for hours. He can focus when he wants to. It’s just laziness.” Using the pattern test from this concept, how would you respond? What would you suggest the team do next?►

Start with what your colleague noticed, because it’s real. Different tasks make different demands on executive function. Activities that are active, interesting, or quickly rewarding ask less of a struggling system. Slow, routine, adult-assigned work asks the most. So doing well in PE and poorly on a worksheet fits executive dysfunction. It doesn’t rule it out.

Then apply the pattern test. Is the struggle lasting? Does it show up in other classes and at home? Is it keeping him from doing work he knows how to do? Your team is in a good position to answer, because each of you sees him in a different setting.

As a leader, suggest that the team compare notes using specific behaviors (“started independent work without a reminder in 2 of 10 class periods”) instead of labels. If the pattern holds, bring it to the student support team. Pressure alone is a poor bet. A student who lacks a skill and gets punished for not using it often learns to avoid the task, not to do it.

Major concept 3 · Treating focus like a skill to be built

ADHD and Executive Function

ADHD (attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder) is one of the most common reasons a student’s executive function struggles. It is a neurodevelopmental disorder marked by ongoing patterns of inattention, hyperactivity, and/or impulsivity that interfere with daily life. In a 2022 national survey, parents reported that about 1 in 9 U.S. children ages 3 to 17 had been diagnosed at some point (Danielson et al., 2024). Boys were diagnosed about twice as often as girls. In a class of 25, two or three students with ADHD is typical.

What we know about causes, and what we don’t

Researchers still don’t know exactly what causes ADHD or exactly how it works in the brain. No blood test or brain scan can diagnose it. Clinicians identify ADHD by its symptoms: what a person does, how often, and how much it gets in the way.

What we do know is that ADHD is partly genetic. Twin studies compare how alike identical and non-identical twins are. Across 37 of them, genetic differences accounted for about 74% of the differences between people in ADHD traits, on average (Faraone & Larsson, 2019). That number describes a group, not a person. It doesn’t mean a student’s ADHD is 74% genes, and it doesn’t mean the environment can’t help. ADHD also involves differences in how the brain develops, but researchers are still working out which differences matter and how.

You may hear that ADHD is simply “low dopamine.” Dopamine is involved, and most ADHD medications act on it. But a 2024 review found limited evidence that ADHD is a simple dopamine shortage. The authors called that idea one of the most common misconceptions about ADHD (MacDonald et al., 2024). Be wary of any classroom strategy sold as a way to “boost dopamine.” That’s brain-talk doing the work that evidence should do.

We also know that some conditions make symptoms worse and others make them better. Poor sleep, stress, and chaotic routines make attention and self-control harder for anyone, and especially for students with ADHD. Medication, behavioral supports, and predictable structure tend to help. That’s the practical point for teachers. You can’t change a student’s genes, but you can change a lot about the conditions around them.

Some popular explanations don’t hold up. Sugar doesn’t cause ADHD. A review of controlled studies found that sugar didn’t affect children’s behavior or thinking (Wolraich et al., 1995). Poor parenting doesn’t cause it either, though a calm, structured home can make daily life easier. Screen time is less settled. One study followed about 2,600 teens without ADHD symptoms for two years. Teens who used digital media more often were somewhat more likely to report new ADHD symptoms later (Ra et al., 2018). The link was modest, and the study couldn’t show that screens were the cause.

Three features, three presentations

ADHD has three core features:

  • Inattention is trouble steering attention. A student with ADHD may lock onto an interesting task for hours and still drift away from a routine one within minutes.
  • Hyperactivity often eases with age. In teens and adults, it may show up as inner restlessness instead of running around the room.
  • Impulsivity is the brake catching too late: acting before thinking it through. Of the three, it is the one most often mistaken for deliberate misbehavior.

Clinicians describe three presentations, depending on which features stand out.

Predominantly Inattentive
Trouble sustaining focus, following through, and staying organized, without much hyperactivity. It often looks like a quiet daydreamer and is often missed, especially in girls. This is what people used to call ADD.
Predominantly Hyperactive-Impulsive
Lots of movement and acting on impulse, with fewer obvious attention problems. Most often seen in young children.
Combined Presentation
Significant symptoms of both. Usually needs support across many settings and skills.

A student’s presentation can change over time. A child diagnosed as hyperactive-impulsive at age 7 may meet the criteria for the combined presentation in middle school, when schoolwork asks more of attention. ADHD also tends to last. One long-term study followed children with ADHD into their mid-twenties. Only about 9% fully recovered and stayed that way. Most had symptoms that came and went over the years (Sibley et al., 2022).

ADHD often looks different in girls. In childhood, girls meet the criteria at a little under half the rate of boys, but the gap narrows to nearly even by adulthood (Hinshaw et al., 2022). Girls with ADHD more often show inattention, along with anxiety or low mood, and many work hard to hide their struggles. A quiet girl who daydreams, loses track of assignments, and worries in silence doesn’t stand out the way a boy out of his seat does. Because her symptoms are less obvious, she’s more likely to be overlooked or diagnosed years later, even when the struggle is just as real. Teachers are often the first adults in a position to notice.

What ADHD is and isn’t

Because ADHD is so common, a lot of what people “know” about it is wrong. Some of these ideas are close cousins of neuromyths: a real finding stretched past what the evidence shows.

What people sayWhat the evidence says
“He can focus on video games for hours, so it isn’t ADHD.”This fits ADHD. Games give fast, frequent feedback and rewards. The struggle shows up on slow, routine work.
“It’s just laziness.”ADHD is a real condition, and it is partly genetic. Effort matters, but effort alone doesn’t make the symptoms go away.
“Sugar causes it.”Controlled studies don’t support this.
“Bad parenting causes it.”Parenting doesn’t cause ADHD, though structure at home can make it easier to manage.
“Screens cause it.”Heavy use has a modest link with later symptoms. Cause and effect isn’t established.
“Kids outgrow it.”Hyperactivity often eases, but for most people symptoms continue in some form into adulthood.
“ADHD is mostly a boy thing.”Boys are diagnosed more often as children, but girls are more often missed. Their symptoms tend to be quieter, and the gap nearly closes by adulthood.
“Everyone’s a little ADHD.”Everyone loses focus sometimes. A diagnosis requires symptoms that last, show up across settings, and interfere with life.
“ADD is a different condition.”ADD is an outdated name. Today it’s ADHD, predominantly inattentive presentation.
“ADHD means you can’t pay attention.”The attention is there. It’s hard to steer and hard to sustain on low-interest tasks. Some advocates have proposed new names, such as DAVE (Dopamine Attention Variability Effect), to make that point. These aren’t official terms, and putting “dopamine” in the name oversimplifies the science, but the core point holds: the attention varies.
Retrieval practiceA parent tells you, “My son can play video games for three hours straight. He can’t have ADHD.” Explain why both behaviors fit with ADHD. What does that suggest for your classroom?►

The two behaviors don’t contradict each other. ADHD is trouble steering and sustaining attention, especially on tasks that are slow, routine, or chosen by someone else. A video game is the opposite. It gives instant feedback, frequent rewards, constant novelty, and a goal the player chose. A worksheet gives none of that until the end, if at all.

You can’t turn every assignment into a game, but you can borrow what the game does well. Break work into short pieces, give feedback quickly, and make progress easy to see. Those are the same ingredients the strongest classroom supports in the next concept use. (With the parent, stick to what you see in class and leave questions of diagnosis to professionals. Concept 5 covers why.)

Major concept 4 · What every teacher can do

Tier 1 Interventions

Think back to the CEO from the opening. She didn’t need a lecture on trying harder. She needed her assistant back. For many students, the teacher can play that role at first: keeping the calendar, posting the steps, giving the reminder. Over time, the goal is to teach the student to run the system, then step back a little at a time.

Schools usually organize support in three tiers. Tier 1 is what every student in a classroom gets. Tier 2 adds small-group help, and Tier 3 adds intensive, individual help. This concept is about Tier 1: what a classroom teacher can build into the room for everyone.

Supports can be misused. If they never come down, a student never learns to carry the load. Recall antifragility, the idea that people grow stronger through challenge, as long as the challenge is pitched at the right level with the right support. Good supports work that way. They make the task possible now, then fade as the student’s skill grows.

A word about the evidence

The research on classroom-wide supports for executive function is thinner than most teachers assume. The strongest evidence is for teacher-run behavioral systems and for teaching organization skills directly. Many popular tips have little or no research behind them.

Education research also rarely gives clean, final answers. A strategy can look strong in one group of schools and then show little effect when another team tries it somewhere else. When that happens, science is doing its job. Testing results again in new places is how researchers find out what holds up. The strategies below are sorted by how much evidence supports them right now, and some of those ratings may change as new studies come in.

StrategyEvidence right now

Strong evidence

Strong evidenceBehavioral classroom management
Clear rules, specific praise, and point or token systems that reward the behaviors you want. Reviews of treatment research rate behavioral classroom management a well-established treatment for ADHD. That means it has worked in multiple studies by independent research teams (Evans et al., 2018). Game-style point systems belong here. What makes them work is frequent feedback and reward. The Good Behavior Game, which you met in Chapter 19, belongs here too. In it, teams earn small privileges by keeping disruptive behavior low. It has a long record of success, though one large replication in English schools found no effect (Ashworth et al., 2020).
Example: The class earns points for starting work within two minutes and for turning in finished work. Points add up to a privilege the class picks each Friday.
Strong evidenceThe Daily Report Card
A Daily Report Card is a behavioral system built for one student. You saw it in Chapter 19 as a behavior tool, and it does executive function work too. The teacher picks 2 to 5 specific target behaviors, rates them during the day, and sends the card home. Parents follow up with praise or a small reward when the student meets the goal. In a randomized trial with elementary students with ADHD in special education, the card improved classroom behavior, progress on IEP goals, and how much work students got done (Fabiano et al., 2010). It did not raise achievement test scores. That’s a realistic picture: the card changes daily behavior, and better grades may take longer.
Example (a 4th grader’s card for the math block):
Target✓ or ✗
Started work within 2 minutes 
Had materials ready 
Turned in the assignment 
Goal: 7 of 9 checks across three class periods earns 15 minutes of a chosen activity at home.
ResearchReward the steps, not the score

In the late 2000s, economist Roland Fryer ran randomized trials in more than 200 urban schools to test whether paying students would raise achievement (Fryer, 2011). In New York and Chicago, students were paid for test scores or grades. Those programs didn’t move state test scores. In Dallas, second graders were paid $2 for each book they read and passed a quiz on. That reward was tied to a step students could control, and it was the one that showed gains, though only for some students.

Fryer’s explanation is an executive function story. When researchers talked with New York students about how they planned to raise their scores, students mostly described test-taking tricks. They didn’t talk about reading more, studying, doing homework, or asking for help. They wanted the reward but didn’t know the steps that would earn it. That’s the CEO without her assistant. Rewards work best when they’re tied to steps a student knows how to take, which is exactly how a Daily Report Card is built.

Strong evidenceOrganization training
Organization training teaches a system directly and practices it until it sticks: a homework checklist, a binder with a set place for everything, a planner routine at the end of each class. It is also rated a well-established treatment for ADHD (Evans et al., 2018). This is some of the clearest evidence that building systems helps. Students aren’t told to “be more organized.” They’re shown exactly what organized looks like, then checked and praised until the habit holds.
Example: At the end of every class, a 7th grade science teacher runs the same 90-second check: planner open, assignment written, papers in the right folder. In September she checks every binder. By October, students check a partner’s. By December, they check their own.
Strong evidence (mostly small studies)Self-monitoring
Self-monitoring teaches students to track their own behavior. A soft signal goes off every few minutes, and the student marks a card: Was I working? In a review of self-regulation strategies for students with ADHD, self-monitoring produced large gains in on-task behavior and in how much work students finished and got right (Reid et al., 2005). Most of these studies were small, so we can be more sure that it helps than about how much. Self-monitoring is where the hand-off happens. A Daily Report Card puts the teacher in charge of tracking. Self-monitoring moves that job to the student.
Example: A 9th grader with a 504 plan wears a watch set to vibrate every 6 minutes during independent work. Each time it buzzes, he marks a plus or minus on a sticky note. At the end of class, he and his teacher compare notes for 30 seconds.
Key aphorism

Lend them your systems until they can run their own.

Supports should make the work possible now and fade as the skill grows.

That’s the thread running through all three strong-evidence tools. A Daily Report Card starts with the teacher keeping track. Organization training starts with the teacher checking every binder. Self-monitoring hands the checking to the student.

Good evidence

Good evidenceRoutines, written steps, and smaller chunks

These supports take load off working memory. Post the daily schedule in the same place every day. Give directions in writing as well as out loud. Break big assignments into small, labeled steps, and hand out one step at a time when a student is stuck at the start. Teach transitions as a routine and practice them, the way you’d practice a fire drill. The Church’s guidance for Primary teachers makes a similar point about children with disabilities: a posted class schedule “can help reduce feelings of uncertainty, which may increase anxiety in some children” (The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, 2023).

Academic supports such as peer tutoring and direct instruction have shown some of the largest effects on schoolwork for students with ADHD (DuPaul et al., 2012).

Some teachers worry that supports like these lower the bar. Ask what the lesson is actually for. If the goal is persuasive writing, remembering six spoken directions isn’t part of the rigor. Removing that load lets the student spend effort where you want it.

Example: Instead of “Write your essay,” a 10th grade teacher hands out step one only: “Write three possible claims on this sticky note. You have six minutes.” Step two comes after.
Good evidence, smaller than claimedMovement and exercise
Physical activity can help executive function in students with ADHD. Exercise programs have shown benefits for attention and self-control. When researchers set aside the weaker studies, though, the effect shrinks to small-to-moderate (Chen et al., 2026). Movement breaks are cheap and good for everyone. Just don’t expect them to do the work of the strategies above.
Example: A “movement menu” by the door lists quick options (wall push-ups, a lap to the drinking fountain, stretches) that students can use with a hand signal.

Popular, but not yet tested

Many strategies teachers share online have little or no direct research behind them. That doesn’t mean they fail. It means we don’t know yet. Most are cheap and low-risk, so it’s reasonable to try one with a student who needs help. Do it the way a scholar-practitioner would. Pick one strategy and track the student’s on-task behavior or finished work for a couple of weeks. Keep the strategy only if the numbers improve.

The 5-minute ruleCommit to just five minutes to get startedUntested. Low cost, and it targets task initiation.

Good advice that’s hard to test: the 2-minute rule

Some advice is sensible, widely used, and almost impossible to study. The 2-minute rule is a good example. It comes from David Allen’s productivity book Getting Things Done (2001): if a task takes less than two minutes, do it now instead of putting it on a list. For a student who struggles with working memory, that makes sense. A permission slip turned in right away can’t be forgotten later.

But no one has tested it as a classroom support for ADHD, and it would be hard to. It covers dozens of tiny tasks a day, and a researcher would struggle to count what didn’t get forgotten. So label it honestly. It’s a reasonable habit to teach and model, not a proven intervention, and it shouldn’t take the place of the strong-evidence tools above.

Tested, and didn’t help

A few popular ideas have been tested and failed.

StrategyThe promiseWhat happened
Fidget spinnersA way to channel restlessness and improve focusIn a classroom study of young children with ADHD, spinners led to more attention problems, even while children were in an evidence-based program (Graziano et al., 2018).
Brain-training gamesComputer games that drill working memory to strengthen attentionStudents get better at the game. Across many studies, the gains didn’t carry over to reading, math, or other schoolwork (Melby-Lervåg et al., 2016). Pediatric guidelines don’t recommend cognitive training for ADHD (Wolraich et al., 2019).
Social skills groups (for ADHD)Group lessons on making friends and getting alongA Cochrane review of the randomized trials found no clear improvement in social skills or behavior as teachers rated them (Storebø et al., 2019). The evidence is weak overall, so this verdict could change. See the note below.

Social skills training is a good example of matching the support to the problem. Students with ADHD usually know the social rules. Their trouble is using those rules in the moment, which is an executive function problem: knowing what to do and doing it are different things (Aduen et al., 2018). Lessons that teach the rules again don’t fix that. Social skills programs can help when the problem really is a missing skill. For example, the PEERS program has good evidence from randomized trials with autistic teens (Laugeson et al., 2012). It’s a version of the chapter’s question: is this a can’t, or is it a knows-how-but-can’t-do-it-yet?

Brain-training games are worth a closer look because they sound so scientific. They promise to strengthen working memory the way a gym strengthens a muscle. You learned about neuromyths in Chapter 4, and brain training is a good example of one. A real finding (practice improves the practiced task) gets stretched into a claim the evidence doesn’t support (practice on one task improves everything). Compare that with organization training. Drilling a skill in isolation doesn’t carry over to schoolwork. Teaching a real system, in the real setting where it’s needed, does.

Retrieval practiceDuring independent work, a 5th grader with ADHD sits still and produces nothing for the first ten minutes, every day. Using the evidence in this concept, describe a plan that starts with the strongest strategies. Then name one untested strategy you might add, and explain how you’d find out whether it helps.►

Start with the stronger evidence.

  • Shrink the start (good evidence). The stall is likely a task initiation problem. Hand out only the first step, make it small and concrete, and set a short time limit: “Answer number one. You have four minutes.”
  • Reward the start (strong evidence). Add “started within two minutes” as a target on a Daily Report Card or a class point system. Now starting is a step he knows how to take, and it earns something.
  • Plan the hand-off (strong evidence). Once starting improves, teach him to self-monitor. He makes a check mark when he begins, and you compare it with yours at the end of class. Over the weeks, your check fades and his stays.

Then test something untested if you want to. You might sit beside him for the first two minutes (body doubling). Track how long it takes him to start for two weeks with it and two weeks without. Keep it only if the data show a difference.

Major concept 5 · Professional Lines

Assessment and Diagnosis

Identifying ADHD is complex. It can’t be done from one behavior, one setting, or a quick checklist. Clinical guidelines require that symptoms last over time, show up in more than one setting, and interfere with daily life.

Three terms come up often:

  • A screener is a short checklist or rating scale that flags patterns worth a closer look.
  • A diagnostic tool is a formal assessment used to decide whether a person meets the criteria for a specific diagnosis.
  • A diagnosis is a decision made by a qualified professional using several kinds of evidence gathered over time: observations, interviews, developmental history, and standard criteria.

A screener can be a useful start, but it doesn’t give answers.

Students and families now learn about ADHD through social media, online quizzes, and friends. That can raise awareness. It can also mislead, because many signs of ADHD overlap with ordinary stress, boredom, tiredness, and anxiety. An online quiz can’t track development over time or rule out other causes.

A label can also change how a student sees the future. People who come to see a struggle as fixed in their brain or genes tend to expect less change and feel less in control (Lebowitz et al., 2013). That’s one more reason a quiz result shouldn’t stand in for an evaluation. If someone thinks they may have ADHD, the right next step is an evaluation by a qualified professional who gathers information from several sources over time.

What treatment looks like

For school-age children, the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends FDA-approved medication together with behavioral treatment, preferably both parent training and classroom behavioral interventions (Wolraich et al., 2019). For preschoolers, behavioral treatment comes first. The evidence for medication is strong. So is the evidence for the behavioral tools in the last concept, which is why the guideline pairs them. Decisions about treatment belong to families and their doctors. Teachers help most by running strong classroom systems and sharing clear observations.

The professional line

Teachers play an important role in this process, but a limited one. The limits are firm.

What you never do:

  • Tell a family, or hint, that their child has ADHD or any other condition.
  • Recommend medication.
  • Discourage medication. “I don’t think he needs pills” crosses the line too.
  • Make medication a condition for anything.

What you do:

  • Describe what you’ve seen in plain, specific terms.
  • Share your observations with the family and the school’s student support team.
  • Point families to the school team or their child’s doctor for anything about diagnosis or treatment.

If a parent asks you directly, you can say, “That’s a question for his doctor. What I can share is what I see in class.”

Why the line is firm: Most ADHD medications are controlled substances. Federal special education law requires every state to forbid school staff from requiring a child to get a prescription for one. That holds for attending school, being evaluated, and receiving services (Individuals with Disabilities Education Act, 2004). Some states go further. Texas, for example, bars school employees from recommending a psychotropic drug or suggesting any particular diagnosis (Tex. Educ. Code § 38.016). Rules vary by state and district, so this book teaches the strictest version. When you’re hired, learn your own state’s and district’s rules too.

Clear observations are your best contribution. Compare “He shows ADHD symptoms” with “He started independent work without a reminder in 2 of the last 10 class periods, and he lost track of multi-step directions 4 times this week.” The second gives the evaluation team something to work with, and it keeps you on your side of the line.

Retrieval practiceA parent tells you her daughter “definitely has ADHD” because of an online quiz. She asks you to note it in your records, and she wants to know whether you think medication would help. What do you say and do? What do you avoid?►

What to say and do: Thank her for raising the concern, and take it seriously. Share your own observations in specific terms (“I’ve noticed she often stops after the first step of multi-part directions”). Explain that a formal evaluation is the way to get a real answer, and offer to connect her with the school’s student support team. You can also suggest she talk with her daughter’s doctor. On medication: “That’s a question for her doctor. What I can share is what I see in class.”

What to avoid: confirming or doubting the quiz, writing “ADHD” in your records when no professional has diagnosed it, and giving any opinion on medication, for or against.

Why: You’re protecting the family’s chance to get an accurate answer, and you’re staying inside the law and your professional role. Your observations are valuable because they describe what you saw instead of what you think it means.

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

Executive function struggles are invisible. What you see is behavior. These practices help close the gap between what a student seems to be doing and what they can actually do yet.

  • Ask “can’t or won’t?” first. A skill problem needs teaching and support. A motivation problem needs a reason. Pressure on a skill problem usually backfires.
  • Start with the strongest evidence: clear rules, specific praise, and point systems for the class, and a Daily Report Card for individual students.
  • Teach systems, then hand them over. Teach checklists, binders, and planners, and check them until they stick. Self-monitoring moves the tracking from you to the student.
  • Take the load off working memory. Post schedules, write directions down, and break big tasks into small steps. Done well, this protects rigor by clearing space for the real work.
  • Test before you trust. Try popular but untested tips, like body doubling or Pomodoro timers, with one student. Track the results, and keep what works.
  • Skip what’s been tested and failed, like fidget spinners and brain-training games. Match the support to the problem: a student who knows the skill but can’t use it yet needs a system and practice, not the same lesson again.
  • Stay on your side of the line. Observe, describe, and refer. Never suggest a diagnosis, and never recommend or discourage medication.
  • Look past the behavior. Ask what’s happening when the hard moment comes. Students with ADHD have strengths and interests like anyone else, and those are often the best way in.
Chapter synthesis
The CEO in the opening story wasn’t careless. She had lost her assistant. Many students who look careless never had one.
Executive function is the brain’s management system. Working memory, inhibitory control, and cognitive flexibility develop slowly, into the twenties, with no single finish line.
Executive dysfunction is a pattern, not a diagnosis. It matters when it lasts, shows up across settings, and gets in the way. Everyone has bad days.
ADHD is a partly genetic condition that makes attention hard to steer. Its exact causes are still unknown, so it is identified by symptoms. It often looks quieter in girls. Sugar and bad parenting don’t cause it, “low dopamine” oversimplifies it, and the right conditions can make symptoms better or worse.
The best-supported classroom tools are behavioral and organizational. Rules, praise, points, Daily Report Cards, organization training, and self-monitoring have the strongest evidence. Many popular tips are untested, and fidget spinners and brain-training games have failed.
Build the system, then hand it over. Supports should make the work possible now and fade as the skill grows. Reward the steps students can control, not just the outcome.
Teachers observe, describe, and refer. They never suggest a diagnosis, and they never recommend or discourage medication.

So, can’t or won’t? On the first day, a teacher often can’t tell. What a teacher can do is watch for the pattern, start with the supports that have the best evidence, and keep handing the system back to the student until they can run it alone.

The research doesn’t settle every question. Classroom-wide supports for executive function are less studied than they should be, and some well-known findings haven’t held up when tested again. That’s a reason to test and track in your own room. It’s how a student who never had an assistant learns to become their own.

Key aphorisms
  1. Kids do well if they can.
  2. Lend them your systems until they can run their own.
