Extrinsic Motivation & Behaviorism

Will Work for Stickers

Chapter 19~ 45 min read

Walk into almost any elementary classroom and you’ll find some kind of behavior system: a sticker chart, a marble jar, or a clip chart running from purple at the top down to red. Plenty of teacher-prep courses warn against these systems. You’ll find them everywhere anyway.

Here’s a scene that plays out in those rooms every day. Billy leans over and starts talking to Johnny. Johnny is trying to finish his math. The teacher looks up, hears voices from that corner, and says, “Johnny, go move your clip down to orange.”

Johnny gets up and walks to the chart while the whole class watches. Billy goes back to his paper.

What did Johnny just learn? What did Billy learn? What did the other twenty-four kids learn? And was the clip chart the problem, or something else? (We’ll come back to Johnny in Concept 5.)

Most of us say we want students to learn for the joy of it. Then we hand out stickers, move clips, and give the class free time because they “earned” it. Rewards and consequences run through every classroom, whether anyone planned them or not. This chapter makes the case for learning to see them, and for using them on purpose.

The big picture

Behaviorism has a reputation as a way to make kids comply. It’s better understood as a description of how all of us form habits and emotional associations, and those processes run in every classroom. It is also the best-tested foundation for classroom management we have. Reinforcement, routines, token systems, team games, and behavior plans all have decades of classroom evidence behind them.

Behaviorism isn’t the whole story of how students learn, and this chapter says plainly where it falls short. But a teacher who doesn’t know it will struggle to run a real classroom. We cover how conditioning works, when rewards help, why routines carry so much of the load, how teachers sometimes reward the very behaviors they’re trying to stop, and what to do when a student’s behavior points to something deeper. Chapter 20 takes up the other half of the story: the motivation that comes from inside.

Major concept 1 · The Science of What You Can See

Behaviorism

In the early 1900s, psychology had a measurement problem. Many psychologists studied the mind by asking people to look inward and describe their own thoughts and feelings, a method called introspection. The trouble was that no one could check anyone else’s report. In 1913, John B. Watson argued that psychology should stop studying what it couldn’t see and study what it could: behavior. That idea became behaviorism, and it has shaped schools, parenting, therapy, and workplaces ever since.

Behaviorism rests on three ideas. First, behavior is what science can actually observe and measure, so that’s what psychology should study. To a behaviorist, a behavior is anything a person does that someone else could see or measure: talking, raising a hand, finishing a worksheet, walking out of the room. It isn’t good or bad, just observable. Behaviorists use a simple test: if a dead body can do it, it isn’t a behavior. “Being quiet” and “not hitting” fail the test, which is why a good behavior goal names what the student will do, like “raises a hand and waits to be called on.” Second, most behavior is learned from our surroundings. Third, learning follows the same basic rules across species. That third idea is why so much early research used rats and pigeons. Researchers assumed the same laws applied to people, and animals were easier to study.

For teachers, behaviorism is both very useful and clearly limited. Its tools, like reinforcement, extinction, and token systems, are some of the most reliable in classroom management. But it can’t explain everything about learning. Language, deep understanding, meaning, relationships, and students’ own motivation all need more than stimulus and response. A teacher who knows behaviorism has powerful tools. A teacher who knows only behaviorism is working with half a map.

ResearchThe classroom experiments that made the case

In the late 1960s, researchers began testing behaviorism in ordinary classrooms, with teachers doing the work. When R. Vance Hall’s team had teachers give their attention to students who were studying and ignore students who weren’t, studying rose sharply. When the rule was flipped, so that attention followed off-task behavior, studying dropped. When it was flipped back, studying rose again, and it stayed higher after the study ended.

Charles Madsen, Wesley Becker, and Don Thomas found that posting classroom rules did little by itself. What changed the room was praising appropriate behavior while ignoring minor misbehavior. And when Don Thomas and colleagues asked the teacher of a well-behaved class to triple her reprimands, disruption went up.

Each of these studies followed only a few children or one or two classrooms. Their strength was the design: the researchers switched the teacher’s behavior on and off and watched the students follow, again and again. Studies like these launched applied behavior analysis and much of the classroom management research that came after.

Key aphorism

You are going to use behaviorism, so use it skillfully.

Every smile, sigh, and comment you let slide teaches students something. Knowing how conditioning works lets you choose what.

On the “B” word

Some people hear “behaviorism” and picture treating children like lab animals. That’s worth pushing back on. The basic insight, that consequences shape behavior, describes how people already work. The real questions are how we choose consequences and what we use them for. The end of the chapter comes back to that second question.

Go deeperWhat behaviorism misses►

Behaviorism’s tools work, but its critics have raised real concerns that every teacher should know.

  • It ignores thinking. Behaviorism treats the mind as a black box, but learning depends on what happens inside it: attention, memory, and meaning. In 1959, the linguist Noam Chomsky argued that Skinner’s approach couldn’t explain how children produce sentences they’ve never heard before.
  • Changes often don’t last. When rewards or consequences stop, behavior often slides back. Students can learn to behave only when someone is watching.
  • Compliance isn’t learning. A quiet room and a thinking room aren’t the same thing.
  • Rewards and punishment have side effects. Rewards can crowd out interest (Chapter 20), and punishment can breed resentment and avoidance (Concept 3).
  • Public systems can shame. Clip charts, leaderboards, and names on the board make consequences a public performance, and the same students often land at the bottom again and again.
  • It’s easy to slide from teaching into control. These tools work well enough that it’s tempting to use them to manage students instead of helping them grow.
Retrieval practiceA colleague says, “I don’t use behaviorism. I teach the whole child, not just their behavior.” What is right about this, and what does it miss?►

What’s right: behaviorism really is incomplete. Meaning, identity, relationships, and understanding matter, and a teacher who treats students as behavior machines misses them.

What it misses: this colleague almost certainly uses behaviorism every day without calling it that, by smiling at good answers, ignoring some disruptions, and setting expectations. The real choice is between using it on purpose and using it by accident.

Major concept 2 · Pavlov, Dogs, and Why School Can Trigger Anxiety

Classical Conditioning

Ivan Pavlov was a Russian scientist who won the Nobel Prize in 1904 for his work on digestion, not psychology. His most famous discovery was almost an accident. While measuring how much his dogs drooled when given food, he noticed they started drooling before the food arrived. They drooled at the sight of the assistant who fed them, or at the sound of his footsteps. The dogs had learned, without anyone teaching them, that these signals meant food was coming.

Pavlov tested this by pairing a sound, such as a metronome or buzzer, with food over and over. Soon the sound alone made the dogs drool. He called this kind of learning a conditioned reflex. Today we call it classical conditioning: a neutral signal, paired again and again with something that already causes a reaction, starts to cause that reaction on its own.

TermWhat it meansPavlov’s example
Unconditioned stimulus (US)Causes a reaction automatically, with no learning neededFood
Unconditioned response (UR)The automatic reactionDrooling at food
Neutral stimulus (NS)Causes no reaction at firstThe sound, before training
Conditioned stimulus (CS)The once-neutral signal, which now causes the reaction by itselfThe sound, after training
Conditioned response (CR)The learned reaction to the conditioned stimulusDrooling at the sound alone

Classical conditioning matters for teachers because it happens automatically and mostly out of sight. A student who has felt stress, failure, or embarrassment at school can come to feel anxious just walking into a classroom. Nothing has happened yet, but the room itself has become the signal.

It works the other way too. A teacher who is reliably warm, calm, and fair becomes a signal for safety, and students settle in faster. Whether we mean to or not, the emotional climate of our room is partly built by classical conditioning.

Conditioning you didn’t intend

A student who was embarrassed reading aloud in third grade may still freeze when called on years later, in a different school with a kind teacher. Being called on has become a conditioned stimulus, and fear is the conditioned response. That’s a normal learning process doing what it does, not a weakness or a character flaw. The point for teachers: you are conditioning your students whether you plan to or not.

Application: using conditioning to build routines

Because conditioning works automatically, it’s a great tool for the routine parts of the day. Pair a specific signal with a specific expectation, consistently, and the signal starts doing the work for you. Three places to start:

  • Transition signals. Use one distinct sound, like a chime or a short song, and pair it every time with calm, organized behavior. Over time, the sound itself starts to settle the room.
  • An opening routine. If students always walk in to the same calm music and the same kind of warm-up prompt on the board, the room starts telling them, “The hallway is over. It’s time to focus.”
  • Task cues. If independent writing always starts the same way, such as a visible timer and the same colored paper, starting stops feeling like a fight and becomes a habit.

Routines like these use both kinds of conditioning in this chapter. The signal tells students what to do, which is operant conditioning (Concept 3). Over time it also brings a calm feeling with it, which is classical conditioning. Concept 5 shows how to teach a routine and what the research says about it.

Retrieval practiceA seventh grader freezes whenever she’s called on, even when she knows the answer. What are the likely conditioned stimulus and conditioned response? What earlier experience might have created them, and what could a teacher do to help the reaction fade?►

The conditioned stimulus is being called on. The conditioned response is the freezing, racing heart, and blank mind. The original unconditioned stimulus was probably a moment of public embarrassment or failure that caused real fear.

To help the reaction fade, the teacher can call on her in low-risk ways again and again with no embarrassment attached: warning her a question is coming, letting her check with a partner first, and responding warmly when she isn’t sure. Each safe experience weakens the old link, a process called extinction. It’s slow, and it only works if the teacher is consistent.

Major concept 3 · Skinner and the Power of Consequences

Operant Conditioning

B. F. Skinner built on Pavlov’s work but asked a different question. Pavlov studied automatic reactions, like drooling. Skinner studied chosen behavior: the things people and animals do on purpose, and how what happens afterward shapes what they do next. He called this operant conditioning.

The main idea is simple. A behavior followed by something good becomes more likely. A behavior followed by something bad, or by nothing at all, becomes less likely. Skinner sorted consequences into four types:

Something is addedSomething is removed
Behavior goes up (reinforcement)Positive reinforcement: praise, a sticker, extra recessNegative reinforcement: a student who scores above 80% no longer has to do extra review, so studying goes up
Behavior goes down (punishment)Positive punishment: extra work, detentionNegative punishment: losing recess or a privilege

Positive reinforcement is one of the strongest tools a teacher has, especially when it’s specific and tied to what the student actually did. Punishment can stop a behavior, but overusing it has side effects: resentment, avoidance, and students who learn to use power the same way.

Skinner also found that how often and how predictably a behavior is reinforced matters a great deal. He called these patterns schedules of reinforcement.

ScheduleHow it worksWhat it producesClassroom example
ContinuousReinforce every timeFast learning, but the behavior fades fast when reinforcement stopsPraising every raised hand while teaching a new routine
Fixed ratioReinforce after a set number of responsesSteady work, with a pause after each rewardA reward after every five completed assignments
Variable ratioReinforce after an unpredictable number of responsesThe strongest, longest-lasting habitsSlot machines; spot-checking random homework assignments
Fixed intervalReinforce after a set amount of timeSlow at first, then a rush right before the deadlineA quiz every Friday, so studying spikes Thursday night
Variable intervalReinforce after unpredictable amounts of timeSteady, consistent effortPop quizzes; random check-ins during work time

The variable ratio schedule deserves extra attention. It powers gambling, social media notifications, and many other habits that are hard to break, because unpredictable rewards pull at us harder than predictable ones. That cuts both ways in a classroom. Unpredictable praise can keep good habits going a long time. But accidentally rewarding a bad habit unpredictably can make it very hard to stop.

The extinction burst

When a behavior that used to get rewarded suddenly stops getting rewarded, it usually gets worse before it gets better. A student whose interruptions used to get your attention will often interrupt louder and more often once you stop responding. This is called an extinction burst, and it’s where many good plans fall apart. The teacher sees the behavior get worse, decides the plan isn’t working, and finally gives in. Now the student has learned that bigger outbursts eventually pay off, and the behavior is stronger than before. A burst often means the plan is starting to work. Hold steady.

Retrieval practiceA teacher stops responding to a student’s blurting. Over the next two days, the blurting gets much worse. One colleague says, “It’s not working. Try something else.” Another says, “Give it more time.” Take the second teacher’s side. What might explain the increase, and what would happen if the teacher finally responded to the biggest outburst?►

This could be an extinction burst. Blurting used to earn attention, so when attention stops, the student tries harder to get it. That suggests the plan may be working.

If the teacher gives in at the worst moment, she rewards the escalated blurting, and on an unpredictable schedule, since it was ignored for days first. That makes the behavior stronger and harder to stop than before. The better move is to keep not responding to blurts while warmly and immediately responding every time the student raises a hand.

Major concept 4 · When Rewards Work

The Case for Behaviorism

Extrinsic motivation is doing something because of what comes from outside: grades, prizes, praise, consequences, or other people’s approval. It’s real, and it works. Students do try harder when something they care about is on the line. So do adults: few of us would file taxes or show up to a 7 a.m. meeting for the joy of it.

Rewards have critics, and Chapter 20 gives them a full hearing. But the research on when rewards help is clear enough to use. In 1999, Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, the researchers best known for the case against rewards, combined 128 experiments with their colleague Richard Koestner. Their review points to a few patterns:

  • Rewards are safest when interest is low. For a task most students find dull, like drilling math facts, a reward has little interest to undercut, and it can get students started when nothing else will.
  • Positive feedback is the safest reward of all. In the review, praise and other positive feedback raised interest overall, though more for college students than for children.
  • Surprise rewards are safe too. A reward that comes after the fact doesn’t change why the student did the work.
  • Informational feedback tends to build motivation; controlling rewards tend to undercut it. Feedback that tells students something real about their work (“Your second paragraph finally makes the argument clear”) builds a sense of competence. Rewards that feel like payment for compliance (“Do this and you’ll get that”) make the activity feel like the teacher’s, not theirs.

Some researchers, notably Judy Cameron and David Pierce, argue that even these warnings overstate the harm. Where rewards clearly can backfire is when a tangible reward is promised for something students already enjoy. “Read five books and earn a pizza” can turn reading into a chore done for pizza. This is called the overjustification effect, and Chapter 20 looks at it closely.

Behavior charts and trackers, explained

Most elementary classrooms run some kind of visible system for tracking behavior. They look alike on the wall, but they rest on different principles, and the research on them is very different.

ResearchWhat the evidence says about charts

Token systems. Reviews of single-case studies, where researchers track one student or one class closely before and after a change, find large improvements in behavior in both general and special education classrooms (Soares and colleagues, 2016; Kim and colleagues, 2022). A stricter 2011 review by Daniel Maggin and colleagues found that many of those studies weren’t designed well enough to call token economies a proven practice, though well-run programs did appear to work.

Group rewards. Here the evidence is stronger. A 2012 review found enough rigorous studies to rate group contingencies evidence-based, and a 2017 update found them generally effective, especially in general education classrooms.

Daily report cards. A 2010 analysis of 17 studies found consistent improvement, largest when families were involved and the card was used across the school day. A 2020 analysis of group studies found that report cards reduce ADHD symptoms in the classroom.

Clip charts. Researchers have mostly asked children what they think. Third graders in one study described how the chart shaped the way they saw themselves and their classmates. In a small second-grade study, most students said they disliked the chart, and many described feeling embarrassed by it. We found no studies showing that clip charts improve behavior.

The pattern is worth noticing. The evidence supports the principles: a specific target behavior, quick feedback, reinforcement, and consistency. It doesn’t support putting every student’s standing on public display.

Back to the behavior chart

So why are behavior charts everywhere, when so many teacher-prep courses warn against them? Partly because the principles behind them work, as the research above shows. Charts change behavior quickly, everyone can see them, and they’re easy to run. They work best for exactly what this concept described: tasks with little built-in interest, like learning a new routine.

There’s one more reason they stick around, and it’s a behaviorist one. When a teacher starts a chart and the room gets calmer, the noise goes away. That’s negative reinforcement, and it’s working on the teacher. The chart keeps going partly because it rewards the adult.

The trouble starts when the chart becomes permanent, gets used for things students already enjoy, or is so public that the same few kids end up at the bottom every day, like Johnny walking to the front of the room. Usually the answer is to fix the chart rather than throw it out. Make it private, name specific behaviors, reward far more often than you penalize, and decide up front what it’s for and when it comes down.

Connecting to Chapter 16: learned helplessness

When students’ efforts seem to make no difference, when they fail no matter how hard they try or consequences seem random, many stop trying. Martin Seligman first described this as learned helplessness in experiments with dogs. Chapter 16 (Concept 3) covers what researchers have learned since, including Seligman’s own conclusion that the original explanation was backward: giving up is the default, and what has to be learned is that our actions work.

For teachers, the lesson is the same either way. Make consequences consistent and tied to what students actually do. A student who has given up needs tasks where effort reliably leads to success, and feedback that shows what they can change. More rewards or punishments won’t do it.

Retrieval practiceA student who used to participate eagerly has stopped raising her hand, stopped turning in work, and answers every question with “I don’t know,” even on things she’s mastered. You don’t think she’s forgotten the material. What’s a likely explanation, and what’s the opposite of the usual “add more consequences” response?►

Learned helplessness is a strong possibility. She may have gone through a stretch where effort didn’t seem to change anything. Giving up in that situation is the brain’s default, not laziness (Chapter 16, Concept 3). Piling on consequences adds more things she can’t control and makes it worse.

Instead, give her back a sense of control: smaller, reachable goals where effort clearly pays off, and specific feedback that shows her what to change (“This part wasn’t clear yet; here’s what would fix it”) instead of only a grade.

Major concept 5 · The Best Managers Barely Manage

Behavior Management

In the 1950s, a psychologist named Jacob Kounin scolded a student in his college class for reading a newspaper, and noticed something odd. The students nearby sat up straighter too, even though he hadn’t said a word to them. He called it the ripple effect. Curious, he spent years studying how teachers handle misbehavior, expecting to find that the best disciplinarians ran the best classrooms.

He found nearly the opposite. How teachers responded to misbehavior barely predicted anything. Calm classrooms and chaotic ones handled problems in very similar ways. The difference was what happened before misbehavior started. Teachers who kept lessons moving, ran smooth transitions, and showed they were aware of the whole room, what Kounin called withitness, simply had far less to manage. The best classroom managers weren’t especially good at discipline. They were good at prevention.

That changes how to read everything below. These strategies are real tools, and you’ll need them. But the goal of a well-built classroom is to need them less. Think of each tool as a patch for a moment prevention didn’t cover. The fewer patches you need, the better your room is running.

Routines: where prevention starts

If prevention is the goal, routines are the main tool. A routine is a procedure students do the same way every time: how to enter the room, turn in work, get help, move into groups, or line up. Every routine is behaviorism at work. A clear signal starts it (Concept 2), students practice the behavior, and success gets noticed and reinforced (Concept 3). Once a routine is learned, it runs almost on its own, and that frees your attention, and your students’, for the actual lesson.

ResearchThe first weeks of school

In the late 1970s, Edmund Emmer, Carolyn Evertson, and Linda Anderson observed third-grade classrooms from the first day of school through the rest of the year. The teachers whose classes ran best had planned their rules and procedures before school started, then spent the first weeks teaching them as carefully as they taught content. Their students stayed on task more and moved between activities more smoothly all year. A follow-up study of junior high classrooms found the same pattern. A 2008 review by Brandi Simonsen and colleagues listed structure and predictable routines, and teaching and reinforcing expectations, among the classroom management practices with the strongest support.

The early studies were observational: they show what good managers do, more than they prove that doing it caused the results. But the pattern has held up across decades of research.

Teaching a routine works like teaching anything else:

  • Explain it, and why it matters.
  • Model it, step by step.
  • Practice it, with students actually doing it, not just hearing about it.
  • Reinforce it. Notice and name it when students get it right.
  • Reteach it when it slips, especially after breaks, without treating the slip as defiance.

Notice what’s missing from that list: posting the rules. Madsen’s team found that posted rules did little by themselves (Concept 1). Rules become routines when they are taught, practiced, and reinforced.

Classroom environmentProximity and withitness►
Simply moving toward a distracted student is one of the most effective and least disruptive tools you have. It redirects behavior without stopping the lesson or calling anyone out. Withitness is students’ sense that you notice everything. Students who believe you see what’s happening manage themselves better than students who think they can get away with things.
While explaining a concept, slowly walk toward the two students passing notes. Make eye contact once. Say nothing. Keep teaching.
ReinforcementSpecific praise►
Not all praise works the same way. Generic praise like “good job” quickly becomes background noise. Effective praise is sincere, specific, tied to what the student actually did, and about the work. Praise that names something specific tells the student what to do again. (The popular rule “praise effort, never ability” turned out to be too simple; see Chapter 16, Concept 4.)
Instead of “Great essay!” try “The way you organized paragraphs two and three made your argument much clearer. That’s exactly what revision is for.”
ResearchIs there a magic praise ratio?

You’ll hear about the ratio in almost every classroom management course: four positive interactions for every correction, or sometimes five. In 2020, Paul Caldarella and colleagues at BYU put the idea to the test. They counted praise and reprimands in 151 elementary classrooms in Missouri, Tennessee, and Utah. The more teachers praised compared with how often they reprimanded, the more time students spent on task. But there was no tipping point where 4:1 suddenly started working. More praise was simply better.

The study tracked how praise and on-task behavior rose together, so it can’t show that praise caused all of the difference. Use the ratio as a reminder rather than a rule: corrections land better inside a relationship that is mostly positive.

ReinforcementPositive narration►
Positive narration means describing out loud the behavior you want more of (“Table three is already writing”) instead of calling out the behavior you don’t. It reinforces the students doing the right thing and shows everyone else what that looks like, without singling anyone out. Two traps: narrating when nobody is on task (fix the task first), and narrating so much it becomes background noise.
Instead of “Stop talking and get started,” try “I see three people with their names on the paper who are already on question one.”
ExtinctionPlanned ignoring►
For behaviors that are about getting adult attention, like calling out or small disruptions, ignoring them on purpose can work. Without the attention, the behavior stops paying off. Three conditions matter. The behavior has to really be about attention, not escaping work or a sign of something deeper. It has to be safe to ignore. And it has to be paired with quick, warm attention for the behavior you want instead. Without that last step, ignoring just confuses the student. And expect the extinction burst.
A student calls out an answer. You don’t react and keep teaching. The moment the student raises a hand, you call on them warmly.
Key aphorism

Attention is a reward. Spend it on purpose.

A look, a word, or a laugh can keep a behavior going. Give your attention to the behavior you want more of.

Consequence designNatural, logical, and punitive consequences►
The kind of consequence matters as much as whether there is one. Natural consequences happen on their own: a student who won’t wear mittens gets cold hands. Logical consequences connect directly to the behavior: a student who writes on a desk cleans it. Both teach students how actions and outcomes connect. Punitive consequences are unrelated penalties. They make a student uncomfortable but rarely teach anything. Whenever you can, make consequences logical.
A student who disrupts a lab group finishes the lab on their own (logical). Losing five minutes of recess for the same thing is punitive: it’s just unpleasant.
SystemsToken economies and PBIS►
A token economy is a system where students earn points, tickets, or other tokens for specific behaviors and trade them for rewards or privileges. Done well, it gives students clear, consistent reinforcement all day. PBIS (Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports) is the school-wide framework many schools use to organize these systems. It has three tiers: teaching and reinforcing expectations for everyone (Tier 1), extra small-group support for students who need more (Tier 2), and intensive individual support for the few who need the most (Tier 3). The aim is always to teach and reinforce the right behavior, not just to punish the wrong one.
A school uses “Wildcat Bucks.” Any staff member can give them out when students are safe, respectful, and responsible, and students spend them at a monthly school store.
SystemsThe Good Behavior Game►
The class splits into teams. Each team gets a mark for certain behaviors, like being out of their seat or talking out. Teams that stay under a set number of marks earn a small privilege. Because the consequence belongs to the team, students encourage each other in ways a teacher’s corrections can’t. It’s one of the most carefully tested interventions in education.
Two teams, marks on the board for call-outs, and the winning team lines up first for lunch. Keep rounds short. Running it all day wears out the novelty that makes it work.
ResearchWhy the Good Behavior Game earns its own mention

Researchers Harriet Barrish, Muriel Saunders, and Montrose Wolf first tested the Game in 1969 and found it quickly reduced disruption in a rowdy fourth-grade class. What came later is what makes it stand out. Starting in 1985, Sheppard Kellam’s team ran it with first and second graders in 19 Baltimore schools and followed those children for years. By ages 19 to 21, students who had played the Game, especially boys who started first grade as the most aggressive, had lower rates of drug and alcohol problems, regular smoking, antisocial personality disorder, and violent crime. A first-grade classroom game, still showing effects in adulthood. Very little in education has evidence that reaches that far.

Not every trial has matched Baltimore. A large 2020 trial in 77 English primary schools found no overall effects. Even a well-tested tool depends on how it’s run and where.

ResearchDoes school-wide PBIS work?

In a randomized trial in 37 Maryland elementary schools, Catherine Bradshaw and colleagues compared schools trained in school-wide PBIS with schools that weren’t. Over several years, the PBIS schools had significantly fewer suspensions and office discipline referrals, and teachers rated children as having fewer behavior and concentration problems. Children in PBIS schools were about a third less likely to be sent to the office.

Retrieval practiceThink back to Johnny and the clip chart from the start of this chapter. Billy was talking; Johnny was working; Johnny moved his clip. Using what you’ve learned, what did each student likely learn from that moment, and what could the teacher have done instead?►

Billy learned that talking during work time costs him nothing. That’s accidental reinforcement.

Johnny learned that doing the right thing doesn’t protect him, which is a small step toward learned helplessness. The public walk to the chart may also start pairing the chart, or the class, with embarrassment.

The rest of the class learned that consequences depend on where the noise comes from, not who caused it.

Was the chart the problem? Mostly not. The teacher acted without seeing who was talking, which is the opposite of withitness, and the public chart made that mistake cost more. Better options: walk over first (proximity) to see what’s actually happening; quietly narrate Johnny’s focus (“Johnny’s already on number six”); redirect Billy privately; and if a consequence is needed, make it logical and private.

Major concept 6 · When Behavior Points to Something Deeper

Behavioral & Neurodevelopmental Considerations

The tools in Concept 5 handle the normal range of classroom behavior. But some students’ behavior falls outside that range. It’s persistent, intense, and doesn’t respond to the usual reinforcement and consequences. For these students, the behavior isn’t just a choice to be shaped. It’s a signal. A general education teacher’s job is to recognize the pattern, provide consistent support, and know when to bring in specialists. Diagnosis belongs to them.

Two clinical patterns show up often enough that every teacher should recognize them. Oppositional Defiant Disorder (ODD) is a lasting pattern of angry, argumentative, defiant behavior toward adults in charge. It goes well beyond normal limit-testing, lasts for months, and is out of proportion to the situation. Conduct Disorder (CD) is more serious: a repeated pattern of violating other people’s rights and major rules through aggression, destruction, lying, or serious rule-breaking. Both are disruptive behavior disorders. Both call for structured, consistent behavioral support, and almost always for help beyond the classroom.

A different group of conditions often gets lumped in with behavior problems, but shouldn’t be. Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) and ADHD are neurodevelopmental conditions. They certainly affect behavior, but they start with differences in how the brain develops and handles information, not with a choice to misbehave. A diagnosis doesn’t excuse misbehavior. But it can help explain how a child works, and that helps you choose responses that give the student the best chance to succeed.

ResearchBehavior management as treatment

Behavioral tools aren’t only for ordinary misbehavior. Reviews of treatment research have repeatedly rated behavioral classroom management a well-established treatment for ADHD, meaning it has worked in multiple studies by independent research teams (Pelham and Fabiano, 2008; Evans and colleagues, 2014 and 2018). That means clear expectations, frequent feedback, consistent reinforcement, and tools like daily report cards. For a student with ADHD, a teacher who knows behaviorism is part of the treatment.

Common misconception“‘ADD’ and ‘Asperger’s’ are still the right terms.”►

You’ll still hear “ADD” and “Asperger’s syndrome” in conversation and see them in older records. Neither is a current diagnosis, and teachers shouldn’t use them professionally. The manual clinicians use to diagnose these conditions, the DSM (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders), has replaced both:

Old termWhat it describedCurrent term
ADD (Attention Deficit Disorder)Attention problems without obvious hyperactivityRenamed ADHD in 1987. Today’s DSM-5 calls it ADHD, predominantly inattentive presentation. ADHD is one diagnosis with three presentations: inattentive, hyperactive-impulsive, or combined.
Asperger’s syndromePeople on the autism spectrum with strong language skills and no intellectual disabilityFolded into Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) by the DSM-5 in 2013. Clinicians now describe the level of support needed (Levels 1 to 3) instead of using separate labels.

Why this matters: parents, colleagues, and specialists notice the words you use. Current, precise terms show professionalism and build trust, and outdated terms can quietly cost you credibility. Precise language also matches the legal frameworks you’ll work within, like IDEA and 504 plans, and keeps the focus on a student’s actual needs instead of old stereotypes. This doesn’t mean you should correct a parent who says “ADD” or “Asperger’s.” That’s a judgment call that depends on the situation. It means that in your own professional speaking and writing, you use current terms.

Functional Behavior Assessments and Behavior Intervention Plans

When a student’s behavior seriously and persistently gets in the way of learning, your referral can start a process that both the law and the research take seriously: a Functional Behavior Assessment (FBA), followed by a Behavior Intervention Plan (BIP). You’ll almost never run an FBA yourself. That’s specialist work, usually led by a school psychologist or behavior analyst. But you need to understand it, because you’ll very often be the one carrying out the plan.

The logic is the behaviorism you already know, focused on one student. The FBA looks at the ABCs: the antecedent (what happens right before), the behavior, and the consequence (what happens right after). It asks one question: what is this behavior getting the student? Usually the answer is attention, escape from a hard task, access to something they want, or sensory relief.

The BIP then teaches a replacement behavior that gets the student the same thing in an acceptable way. A behavior you only punish has nowhere to go. A need you redirect does. The BIP is the individualized top tier of the PBIS system from Concept 5.

Why a half-followed plan can be worse than none

A BIP only works if every adult follows it, consistently and the same way. This is where plans quietly fail. If one teacher follows it and another doesn’t, or you hold the line Monday through Thursday and give in on Friday, the behavior isn’t being extinguished. It’s being reinforced on a variable ratio schedule, the most stubborn pattern there is. The student learns the behavior still pays off sometimes, so they push longer and harder. A half-followed plan can make the behavior stronger than no plan at all. Following the plan exactly, every time, by everyone, is how the plan works.

Retrieval practiceA first grader is constantly out of his seat, blurts out, and can’t wait his turn. He seems truly unable, not unwilling, to stop, no matter what reinforcement or consequences you try. A colleague says, “He’s just being defiant. Hold him accountable.” Why might that be the wrong way to see it, and what is your role?►

The pattern points away from defiance. ODD is defiance aimed at adults in charge, but this looks like “can’t” rather than “won’t,” it doesn’t respond to the usual tools, and it shows up everywhere. That suggests a neurodevelopmental explanation such as ADHD. The difference matters. Defiance is behavior to be shaped; ADHD is a difference in how attention and self-control develop. Treating it as defiance increases conflict and teaches the child that trying doesn’t help, which leads toward learned helplessness.

Your role isn’t to diagnose. Keep providing consistent support, write down what you observe, and refer to the people trained to evaluate: the school counselor, school psychologist, or the family’s doctor. Recognize, support, refer.

Retrieval practiceA student on a BIP is supposed to raise his hand instead of calling out, and you reinforce every raised hand. You follow the plan closely, but the paraprofessional who covers your prep period doesn’t, and the call-outs are going up, not down. Your team wonders if the plan is broken. Using what you know about reinforcement schedules, what’s most likely happening, and what needs to change?►

The plan isn’t broken; the follow-through is. During prep period, calling out still pays off, but only sometimes. That’s a variable ratio schedule, the most stubborn pattern there is, so the call-outs are getting stronger, not weaker. It’s the same thing that happens when a teacher gives in during an extinction burst.

What needs to change is consistency, not the plan. Every adult who works with the student, including paras, specialists, and substitutes, has to run the same plan the same way. That means writing the plan clearly enough to hand off and training the people who cover. Before deciding a BIP has failed, always ask first whether it was actually followed.

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

Behaviorism’s tools are some of the most reliable in teaching, and some of the easiest to misuse. These practices come straight from the chapter.

  • Prevent before you intervene. Smooth routines, steady pacing, and reinforcing the behavior you want will head off most of what you’d otherwise have to correct.
  • Teach routines in the first weeks the way you teach content. Explain, model, practice, and reinforce them, and reteach them after breaks.
  • Use proximity before words. Walking toward a distracted student while you keep teaching handles most small problems without stopping the lesson.
  • Make praise specific and about the work. Name what the student did that worked (“You checked each step against the example”).
  • Keep the positives well ahead of the corrections. Whether you call it 4:1 or 5:1, students respond better when most of your interactions with them are positive.
  • Choose logical consequences over punitive ones. Connect the consequence to the behavior so it teaches something. When you can, keep it private.
  • Use rewards and charts where they help, and build them well. Rewards fit new routines and tasks with little built-in interest. Keep charts private and specific, reward far more often than you penalize, skip promised prizes for things students already enjoy, and decide up front when a chart comes down.
  • Respond to giving up with success, not consequences. A student who “doesn’t try” may have learned that trying doesn’t work. Give them tasks where effort clearly pays off.
  • Expect the extinction burst and hold steady. When you stop rewarding an attention-seeking behavior, it usually gets worse first. Giving in at the peak teaches a stronger version of the behavior.
  • Recognize, support, refer. When behavior is persistent, intense, and doesn’t respond to the usual tools, document it, support the student consistently, and bring in specialists. If a student has a BIP, follow it exactly, every time.
Chapter synthesis
Behaviorism is the foundation of classroom management. It isn’t enough on its own, but a teacher who doesn’t know it will struggle to run a real classroom.
Behaviorism studies what can be seen. Its tools are among the best-tested in teaching, but on its own it’s half a map.
Classical conditioning is automatic. Students learn a room’s emotional climate whether the teacher intends it or not, and a teacher who is reliably warm and fair becomes a signal for safety.
Operant conditioning runs on consequences. Unpredictable reinforcement builds the most stubborn habits, good or bad, and holding steady through an extinction burst is often what makes a plan work.
Rewards and charts work when they follow the principles. Specific target behaviors, quick feedback, reinforcement, and consistency have strong evidence; public clip charts have almost none. Consequences have to track what students actually do, or students stop trying.
Routines carry much of the load. Taught, practiced, and reinforced in the first weeks, they let a classroom run almost on its own.
The best behavior management is prevention. The toolkit is for the moments prevention didn’t cover, and PBIS and token systems apply it school-wide.
Some behavior points past the toolkit. ODD and Conduct Disorder are disruptive behavior disorders; ASD and ADHD are neurodevelopmental differences. Your job is to recognize, support, and refer, and to follow a student’s BIP faithfully.

In the October 2018 general conference, Elder Dale G. Renlund of the Quorum of the Twelve Apostles described God’s goals as a parent in terms any behaviorist would recognize: “Our Heavenly Father’s goal in parenting is not to have His children do what is right; it is to have His children choose to do what is right and ultimately become like Him. If He simply wanted us to be obedient, He would use immediate rewards and punishments to influence our behaviors.” He went on: “But God is not interested in His children just becoming trained and obedient ‘pets’ who will not chew on His slippers in the celestial living room. No, God wants His children to grow up spiritually and join Him in the family business.” (“Choose You This Day,” October 2018)

That is a striking description of what behaviorism can and can’t do. Rewards and consequences can shape behavior, sometimes very well. They can’t make anyone want what is good. So we use them the way a wise parent does: to build safety, structure, and good habits while students grow into people who choose well on their own. Rewards alone produce students who behave when someone is watching. The aim is students who can govern themselves.

The goal isn’t students who obey, but students who choose well.
