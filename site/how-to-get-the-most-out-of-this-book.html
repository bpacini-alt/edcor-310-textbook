How to Get the Most Out of This Book

A short guide to learning educational psychology instead of merely reading about it

Read this before Chapter 1.

This book is about learning: how people change, how they develop, how they become capable, and how teachers can help them along the way.

You are not reading only to collect information for an exam. You are learning how to notice what is happening in people, how to evaluate claims about learning, and how to make wiser decisions as a teacher, leader, disciple, and evidence-based practitioner.

First, practice using a tool until you are consistent and competent.

Then practice choosing the right tool from the toolkit for the job at hand.

The book will sometimes present empirical findings, interpretive frameworks, and questions of value. Learn to tell those apart. Ask what the evidence shows, what an interpretation helps you notice, and what assumptions or commitments shape a conclusion.

Five study techniques that actually help

Jamie Clark’s one-page guide, Effective Study Strategies, describes five research-backed ways to learn more effectively. You can also find Jamie’s larger collection of teaching guides. These techniques may feel less smooth than rereading. That difficulty is often part of the point.

1. Retrieval practice: recall information

Retrieval practice means trying to remember something before looking at the answer. Close the book and ask: What was the main idea? What terms and relationships do I remember? How would I explain this to another student?

Use the book’s retrieval questions, interactive practice, and reflection prompts for this purpose. Try first. Then check your answer and repair what you missed.

2. Spaced practice: distribute your studying

Spaced practice means returning to material over several days or weeks rather than studying everything in one long session.

Read a section today, recall it tomorrow, and use it in a new situation several days later. You do not need to forget everything before returning. You do need to make your memory do some work.

3. Interleaving: mix up what you practice

Interleaving means mixing related topics, theories, or problem types instead of practicing one kind repeatedly until it feels easy.

Early on, it is useful to practice the same kind of task several times. You are learning how to use the tool. Once you can use it consistently, start mixing. Practice deciding which concept, framework, or research principle helps you interpret a new situation.

Several frameworks may offer useful perspectives on the same situation. The goal is to practice recognizing what each framework makes visible, not to pretend that every human problem has one mechanical answer.

4. Dual coding: combine words and meaningful visuals

Dual coding means representing an idea with both words and a useful visual. Draw a developmental timeline, map the relationship between concepts, sketch a model, or label a process.

The visual should clarify the idea. A simple concept map is more useful than a page decorated with colors and pictures.

5. Elaboration: explain how and why

Elaboration means connecting a new idea to what you already know and asking deeper questions:

  • Why might this happen?
  • How does this idea relate to another theory?
  • What is an example and a non-example?
  • What evidence would support or weaken the claim?
  • What would this framework help me notice?
  • What might it leave out?
First learn the move. Then learn when to make it.

Blocked practice and spiral practice do different jobs. Blocked practice helps you learn how to perform a skill. Spiral or interleaved practice helps you learn when and why to use it.

Practice typeMain question
Blocked practiceCan I perform this skill?
Spiral or interleaved practiceCan I recognize when this skill is useful?
Transfer practiceCan I adapt the skill in a new situation?
Take notes that help you think

If your instructor asks you to take notes, you do not need to write down every sentence. High-quality notes help you reconstruct the important ideas later. They preserve the main claim, important relationships, evidence or examples, questions, and possible applications.

A weak note might say:

Spaced practice means studying over time. It helps memory.

A stronger note might say:

Spaced practice

Main idea: Spread study and retrieval across days or weeks instead of cramming.

Why it helps: Returning after some forgetting makes recall effortful, which strengthens later access.

Example: Review a chapter today, tomorrow, and again several days later.

Caution: Cramming may feel smoother, but fluency during one study session is not the same as durable learning.

After taking notes, close the book and use your retrieval prompt. Write or explain what you remember. Then check your notes and repair anything inaccurate or incomplete. The goal is not to produce beautiful pages. The goal is to create notes that help you think when the chapter is no longer open.

Use the book’s features

Retrieval practice asks you to recall an idea before looking back. Interactive practice lets you make a prediction or work through a problem. Intuition builders help you develop a feel for an abstract idea. Common misconception sections show where an appealing idea can go wrong. What to notice sections direct your attention toward an important feature. Think about questions ask you to connect the material to your own experience and future work.

Use the figures, audio, examples, and videos when they help you encounter the idea in another way. But make a prediction before touching an interactive. Otherwise, you may only be watching the answer happen.

Use AI as a tutor, not a substitute for thinking

AI can give you additional practice, but only if you make it ask something of you. Try a prompt like this:

Quiz me on this chapter one question at a time. Do not show me the answer until I respond. Ask me to explain my reasoning, identify misconceptions, interpret evidence, and apply the idea to a teaching or discipleship situation. If my answer is weak, give me a hint rather than solving the problem for me. Use the chapter text I provide as the main source.

You can also ask AI to create a new example, challenge you with a plausible misconception, compare two frameworks, or ask what evidence would change your mind. Do not assume that an AI response is correct. Compare important claims with the book, the cited research, and your instructor’s guidance.

A simple study routine
  1. Read a manageable section.
  2. Attempt the retrieval or interactive practice.
  3. Write a short explanation from memory.
  4. Return to the text and correct yourself.
  5. Revisit the idea later.
  6. Apply it to a real teaching, leadership, or discipleship situation.

You do not need to use every feature every time. You do need to make the ideas do some work.
