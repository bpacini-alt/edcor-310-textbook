Constructivism

What Piaget Learned From (Experimenting on) His Own Children

Chapter 11~ 60 min read

A toddler points at a cat through the window and says, with complete confidence: "Dog."

You correct her. She looks at the cat again, unconvinced. "It has four legs," she says. "It lives in a house. It's a dog."

She is not wrong about her method. She is reasoning from what she already knows — four legs, lives indoors, must be a dog — and the cat fits that framework well enough to pass. This is not a mistake in the sense of carelessness. It is a mistake in the sense of a mind doing exactly what minds do: taking a new thing and forcing it into the shape of an old thing, because the old thing is what's already built.

Chapter 10 met Piaget the observer — the naturalist cataloguing what his own children got wrong, patient enough to ask why instead of just correcting them. This chapter is about what he did with what he saw. The toddler's "dog" is not a fact she needs corrected. It's a window into how she thinks — and into a process every learner in your future classroom is running, on every new idea, all the time.

The big picture
A toddler calling a cat a "dog" isn't confused — she's reasoning from a schema that almost fits. What Piaget discovered from watching moments like this would change how the world thinks about learning: children are not empty vessels waiting to be filled with knowledge, they are active builders who construct understanding from the inside out. This chapter takes that insight seriously — and honestly. We begin with what the theory gets right and what it means for your classroom. Then we complicate it, because the history of constructivism in education is also a case study in what happens when a sound theory becomes an ideology — and the consequences that followed when instructional methods outran the evidence.

Major concept 1 · Learners build on what they already know

Constructivism

Constructivism is a theory about how learning works. At its core: learners do not passively receive knowledge like a file being downloaded — they actively build understanding by connecting new information to what they already know. The toddler calling a cat a "dog" already showed you the mechanism. This happens through assimilation (fitting new information into existing mental frameworks — the cat gets filed under "dog" because it's close enough) and accommodation (revising those frameworks when the new information doesn't fit — eventually, "cat" becomes its own category). Every learner who encounters a new idea brings a prior mental structure to it, and learning is always, at some level, a negotiation between the new and the already-known.

What moves a learner from one to the other? Piaget called it equilibration: the push to get back to balance when an experience doesn't fit what you already know. When something fits, you assimilate it and move on. When it doesn't, you feel a small mental imbalance, and your mind looks for a way back. In practice, it usually tries the cheaper fix first: bending the new information until it fits the old schema (the cat must be an odd sort of dog). It does the harder work of accommodation, rebuilding the schema itself, only when the cheap fix keeps failing. Hold on to that preference. It explains why first ideas last so long, and why misconceptions are so hard to dislodge.

Two versions matter for this course. Cognitive constructivism, most associated with Piaget, focuses on the individual learner's internal process: knowledge is built through the learner's own active engagement with ideas and the physical world. Social constructivism, most associated with Vygotsky, insists that this building happens primarily through interaction with other people — learning is social before it becomes individual. Vygotsky is the focus of Chapter 12. Both versions share the core insight: the learner is always an active participant in constructing their own understanding, never a purely passive recipient. What they don't share is a single answer about how the teacher should behave.

Keep this distinction alive

Constructivism is a theory about the learner's active interpretation during any learning event—including a lecture, a worked example, guided practice, or an open-ended project. It does not by itself identify the best teaching method. Treating a descriptive theory as a complete prescription is the central error examined later in the chapter.

Worth remembering

Every mind constructs. The teacher chooses the materials.

Students build understanding during a lecture just as surely as during a project. The question for a teacher is what they will build with.

Retrieval practiceThe toddler from the hook eventually learns the word "cat" and can point to cats correctly. A week later, she sees a small dog and calls it a cat. What's happening — and is this a step backward?►

This isn't a step backward — it's the same process running again, one category later. She's assimilating the small dog into her newly built "cat" schema because it's close enough on the features she's tracking: small, four legs, lives in a house. The schema that was accurate yesterday is now the thing that needs revising. This is normal and expected — new schemas are rarely precise on the first try, and the same fitting-and-revising process has to run again to narrow "cat" down to what actually makes something a cat rather than just small and four-legged.

For a teacher, the lesson generalizes past toddlers and pets: a student who has just built a new, mostly-correct schema will often overextend it to the next similar case. That overextension isn't evidence the learning didn't take — it's evidence the schema is still being sharpened, and it's exactly the moment where a well-placed correction does the most good.

Major concept 2 · Schemas are load-bearing infrastructure

Prior Knowledge

Learners never arrive empty. They arrive with schemas — the mental frameworks the brain uses to organize knowledge and interpret new experiences. And the most important thing to understand about schemas is this: prior knowledge is not background. It is load-bearing infrastructure. New learning must be built on it. You cannot place new information alongside an existing schema and simply ignore the schema — it will shape, filter, and often distort whatever you say next.

Underneath a schema is an associative network — a web of connected meanings in which activating one idea can make related ideas easier to retrieve. We understand multiplication by connecting it to repeated addition, a new revolution by comparing it with revolutions already studied, and an unfamiliar word by linking it to known words, images, and experiences. A familiar restaurant schema, for example, lets you anticipate a host, a menu, and a bill without relearning the situation every time. Learning is therefore less like filling a container than extending and reorganizing a network.

When a student believes that heavier objects fall faster, or that plants get their mass from the soil, or that the earth is closer to the sun in summer — they don't have a void where knowledge should be. They have a schema. And that schema will actively shape how they interpret anything you say about the topic. If you simply explain the correct idea without confronting the incorrect one, what typically happens is that the student files your explanation under "things school says" while leaving their actual schema intact. Asked on a test, they produce the right answer. Asked in a real context, they act on the wrong belief.

Schemas also organize many categories around prototypes — examples that feel especially typical. Ask someone to picture a bird and they are more likely to imagine a robin than a penguin. Ask for a mammal and a furry land animal may come to mind more readily than a whale. Prototypes act as reference points, so less typical cases can take longer to classify or can be misclassified.

That gives a teacher's early examples real importance, though not absolute control. Early, salient examples can shape what students treat as central to a category; later variation can broaden or correct that representation. A student who insists a whale cannot be a mammal may be reasoning from an overnarrow prototype in which mammals are furry animals that walk on land. Begin with a clear central case, then deliberately vary examples and include boundary cases so the category does not harden around a single image.

Worth remembering

Your brain thinks in examples, not definitions.

Ask a room of adults to draw a house the way a kindergartner would, and almost everyone draws the same one: triangle roof, door, window, chimney. Few houses look like that anymore, but it is the house our minds keep on file.

ResearchFirst examples stick: three studied cases

The equal sign. After years of problems written as 3 + 4 = __, many children read “=” as “write the answer next.” Asked to solve 8 + 4 = __ + 5, they often answer 12 or 17 instead of 7. Mixing in other formats early, such as 7 = 3 + 4, builds the idea that “=” means “is the same as,” and that idea predicts later success solving equations. (McNeil, 2008; Knuth et al., 2006)

Fractions. Students often decide that 1/8 is bigger than 1/6, because 8 is bigger than 6. Their prototype of a number is a whole number. Struggling fourth graders who were taught fractions as amounts on a number line, rather than mainly as parts of a whole, outperformed classmates in their school's regular program. (Ni & Zhou, 2005; Fuchs et al., 2013)

Triangles. Young children often say that an upside-down triangle, or a long, skinny one, is not a triangle. Their prototype is an equal-sided triangle sitting flat on its base. (Clements et al., 1999)

Remember equilibration. A first schema that works well enough gets defended, because bending new information to fit it is cheaper than rebuilding it. That gives your first examples extra weight. Building a good prototype at the start is far easier than tearing down a weak one later.

Worth remembering

Your brain avoids thinking, so make the right decision once.

For teachers: build it right the first time.

A habit set right on day one doesn't have to be fought later. A prototype taught right the first time doesn't have to be untaught.

Prior knowledge does more than shape categories. It also decides how much of a new lesson a student can take in at all.

ResearchKnowing baseball beat reading well

Junior high students read a passage describing half an inning of baseball, then acted it out with figures on a model field. The researchers sorted students by reading ability and by how much they knew about baseball. Knowing the game made a big difference. Reading ability made little. Weaker readers who knew baseball did as well as or better than strong readers who didn't, and strong readers who didn't know baseball did no better than weak readers who didn't. Much of what we call reading comprehension turns out to be background knowledge. (Recht & Leslie, 1988)

Worth remembering

What you already know decides what you can learn.

David Ausubel put it this way in 1968: “The most important single factor influencing learning is what the learner already knows. Ascertain this and teach him accordingly.”

Prior knowledge strongly predicts student achievement, but its relation to how much they gain from a particular lesson varies widely: accurate, relevant knowledge can support learning, while incomplete or misleading knowledge can interfere. The practical task is therefore not merely to “activate prior knowledge,” but to discover what students are activating and help them connect, revise, or replace it as needed. The following strategies are useful ways to do that.

Anticipation Guides
Give students statements about the upcoming content — some true, some false — and have them commit to agree or disagree before instruction. Surfaces misconceptions and creates investment in finding out who was right.
Pre-Assessments
Not for grading — for mapping. What do students already know or believe? Where are the misconceptions that instruction will need to directly confront? The answer changes what you teach first.
Bridging Analogies
Connecting new concepts to domains where students already have strong schemas. Students who can't feel Ohm's Law can feel water pressure through a pipe. Abstraction needs somewhere to land. An analogy lets students assimilate the new idea into a schema they already have, which is much easier than building one from scratch. Then show where the analogy breaks down.
Memory Dumps
Ask students to write what they currently know or predict before instruction. Attempts made before learning—even unsuccessful ones—can improve later learning when they are followed promptly by correct information and feedback. (Richland, Kornell, & Kao, 2009)
Provocative Questions
A question students almost certainly have a wrong-but-confident answer to. Not gotcha — setup. The wrong answer, surfaced and examined, creates the dissatisfaction that makes accommodation possible.
Think-Pair-Share on Experience
“When have you encountered something like this?” Before introducing a concept, cue relevant experiences that can give the new idea an initial point of contact—then check that the analogy actually holds.
Retrieval practiceBefore a unit on evolution, a teacher gives students 10 statements and asks them to mark agree or disagree before instruction begins. What activation strategy is this — and what is it actually doing besides "reviewing what they know"?►

This is an Anticipation Guide. Beyond activating prior knowledge, it is doing two things simultaneously. First, it surfaces misconceptions — the teacher now has a map of what incorrect schemas are in the room, which shapes what instruction needs to directly confront. A student who marks "agree" to "humans and apes share a common ancestor but not a direct lineage" has told the teacher something useful about where accommodation work needs to happen.

Second, it creates investment. Students who have committed to a position before instruction are more engaged in discovering whether they were right — the schema has been activated and primed to receive new information. The dissatisfaction Posner identifies as the first condition for conceptual change is much easier to produce when the student has gone on record with what they currently believe.

Major concept 3 · Why simply explaining the answer often fails

Conceptual Change

It is often easier to plant a correct idea than to displace a bad one. In an influential 1982 model, George Posner and colleagues proposed four conditions that support conceptual change: dissatisfaction with the existing conception, and a replacement that is intelligible, plausible, and fruitful. Later research treats this as a powerful instructional framework rather than a universal four-step law; successful interventions also use bridging, refutational explanation, and changes in how a concept is categorized. 

Worth remembering

To change a schema, roll out the red carpet. Then tug on it.

Let students stand firmly on the old idea first, so they feel it give way when you pull. Then hand them a better idea to stand on.

01
Dissatisfaction
The learner needs a reason to doubt the reach or adequacy of the existing conception. A surprising result, failed prediction, or comparison can create that reason—but conflict is useful only when students can interpret it.
02
Intelligibility
The new conception has to be understandable enough to think with. Challenging an old idea without making the alternative clear can leave students confused rather than changed.
03
Plausibility
The new conception has to seem credible—not merely an arbitrary school answer. Counterintuitive ideas require evidence, mechanism, and explicit comparison with the older account.
04
Fruitfulness
The new conception becomes more attractive when it explains or predicts more than the older one. Students need opportunities to use it, not merely repeat its definition.

One widely studied route to conceptual change is cognitive conflict: a demonstration that surprises, a problem the old framework cannot solve, a comparison that exposes a contradiction, or an anomaly that does not fit the student's model. But conflict alone is not magic. Without guidance, explanation, and a credible alternative, surprise can produce confusion, dismissal, or a memorized exception. Effective conceptual-change teaching is about lasting, profound change in thinking that builds a better accounting of the world. 

Common misconception"If a student has a misconception, just show them the correct information and it will replace the wrong one."►

Simply placing a correct statement beside an incorrect belief often leaves both available. The goal is not always to erase the old idea instantly—old and new conceptions can coexist—but to make the limitation of the old model explicit and give students a more intelligible, plausible, and useful alternative.

Cognitive discomfort can help initiate revision, but it is an opening rather than the whole mechanism. The teacher still has to support explanation, comparison, practice, and retrieval of the new account.

The persistence of learning-styles beliefs among educators is a useful professional example: surveys have found the belief widespread, while systematic reviews have not supported matching instruction to a learner's alleged visual, auditory, or kinesthetic “style.” Correct information can be understood without becoming the framework a person actually uses. (Dekker et al., 2012; Pashler et al., 2008)

Connecting to Chapter 2

Chapter 2 described the mind as an elephant and a rider. Fast intuition does most of the driving, and slow reasoning mostly explains where the elephant already went. Schemas are what the elephant runs on. When a student can recite the right answer but still acts on the old belief, the rider has learned something and the elephant has not.

That is why constructivism matters so much for teachers. Every explanation, example, and practice problem is building the schemas that will drive your students' fast, automatic thinking later. We used to think the rider's job was to steer the elephant. It turns out the rider's real job is building a better elephant.

Retrieval practiceWalk through what actually has to happen for the toddler from the hook to stop calling the cat a "dog" — using Posner's four conditions.►

Dissatisfaction: Simply being told "that's a cat, not a dog" doesn't create it. What creates it is the schema visibly failing — the cat meows instead of barking, or refuses to fetch, or does something a dog reliably wouldn't. The mismatch has to be something she notices herself, not just something she's told.

Intelligibility: "Cat" has to become a graspable category, not just a new label — she needs to understand what actually distinguishes it, in terms she already has (the sound it makes, how it moves), not a technical definition she can't yet use.

Plausibility: The new category has to feel like it could be true, not like an arbitrary adult rule. If every four-legged house pet has been "dog" so far, "cat" needs enough supporting evidence — several cats, consistently different from dogs on features she can track — to feel real rather than exception-that-doesn't-matter.

Fruitfulness: The payoff is that "cat" now helps her sort future animals more accurately than "dog" alone ever could — she can predict things about cats specifically. That predictive power is what makes the new schema worth keeping instead of quietly reverting to the old one.

Notice what didn't work: just telling her the word. That's supplementing, not dislodging — exactly the failure mode named in the misconception above.

Major concept 4 · Ideas deepen through repetition and representation

Spiral Curriculum

Jerome Bruner agreed with Piaget that learners construct knowledge actively — but was more interested in what that meant for curriculum designers and teachers. His two most durable contributions are the spiral curriculum and the three modes of representation.

Rather than covering a topic once and moving on permanently, the spiral curriculum revisits core concepts repeatedly across grade levels — each time at greater depth, complexity, and abstraction. A second grader encounters fractions by dividing physical objects equally. A fifth grader works with symbolic operations on fractions. A middle schooler uses fractions in algebraic expressions. Each visit assumes and builds on the previous one. Even so, the first visit shapes the later ones. If fractions only ever mean slices of a pizza, students can struggle when fractions behave like numbers, such as 3/2 or a point between 0 and 1 on a number line. Showing fractions on a number line early helps (see “First examples stick” in Concept 2). Bruner's bold claim: any subject can be taught in some intellectually honest form to any learner at any age — the task is finding the right representation and the right level of the spiral for where the learner currently is. Coverage asks "did we teach this?" The spiral asks "can they go deeper?"

Bruner also proposed three modes through which knowledge can be represented: enactive (physical action and embodied experience), iconic (images and diagrams), and symbolic (language, numbers, formal notation). These ideas helped inspire concrete-representational-abstract and concrete-pictorial-abstract approaches in mathematics, including approaches commonly associated with Singapore-style mathematics. Evidence is promising in mathematics—especially for targeted interventions—but the literature is narrower than a claim that one sequence is universally best. (Bruner, 1966; Ebner et al., 2025)

The nuance is that representation and sequence interact with the task, learner, and domain. In elementary mathematics, moving from concrete materials toward abstract notation can improve transfer in some studies. Across broader research, however, no rigid sequence has been shown to govern every kind of learning. The strongest general principle is to connect representations explicitly—show what remains the same across object, diagram, language, and symbol—and to fade or add support in response to student understanding.

The practical takeaway

When a concept is unfamiliar and concrete or visual representations reveal its structure, use them—and connect them explicitly to the symbolic form. In some elementary mathematics contexts, moving toward abstraction supports transfer; in other contexts, simultaneous representations or a different order may work. Ask what each representation contributes for this learner and task.

Retrieval practiceA math teacher jumps straight to the symbolic notation for fractions without any physical objects or diagrams. According to Bruner's framework, what is the risk — and what nuance does the research add?►

The risk: symbolic procedures can become detached from meaning. Students may manipulate fractions correctly in familiar exercises without understanding what the numerator, denominator, or operation represents.

The research-qualified response: grounding and explicit connection matter more than ritual obedience to a universal sequence. In some elementary mathematics studies, concrete-to-abstract “fading” improves transfer; in other domains or with more knowledgeable learners, simultaneous or differently ordered representations can work. Use concrete and visual models when they clarify the structure, connect them explicitly to symbols, and remove them when they no longer add value.

Major concept 5 · Different learning goals need different guidance

Natural and Invented Learning

Here is the strongest possible case for constructivism, and it comes from ordinary development. By age three, most children can sustain short back-and-forth conversations, ask questions, and make themselves understood much of the time—without formal grammar lessons. Their language is not “complete,” and development continues for years, but the amount learned through participation, imitation, feedback, and interaction is extraordinary.

Walking offers a related but more variable example. Most children learn to walk without classroom-style lessons, yet the timing and route are not culturally uniform: opportunities to practice, caregiving routines, surfaces, clothing, and expectations can accelerate or delay particular motor milestones. Chomsky's “language acquisition device” was a historically influential way of naming humans' biological preparedness for language, not a discovered brain module that simply switches on. Looking-time studies likewise suggest that even young infants respond differently to physically possible and impossible events, although researchers debate exactly what those responses reveal about infants' representations. 

For capacities such as spoken language, locomotion, social participation, and everyday causal learning, formal lessons are often unnecessary. That does not mean the environment is irrelevant or that adults should “stay out of the way.” Rich input, responsive interaction, opportunity, feedback, and cultural practice are part of how these capacities develop. Constructivism is strongest here when it emphasizes active participation without pretending that development is isolated from teaching or culture.

One influential framework describes these capacities as biologically primary knowledge, contrasted with culturally recent or biologically secondary knowledge. This is a useful instructional heuristic, not a clean binary: preparedness varies by task, and all learning depends on experience. Still, it explains why a method that works for conversation cannot simply be assumed to work for algebra or alphabetic decoding.

Reading is culturally recent—writing systems are only several thousand years old—and the brain has no dedicated organ that evolved specifically for literacy. Learning to read reorganizes and coordinates existing visual, phonological, and language systems. Dehaene and colleagues call this neuronal recycling. The claim is not that instruction mechanically wires one universal circuit, but that literacy changes how existing systems respond and connect.

Children surrounded by print do not ordinarily infer the alphabetic code as effortlessly as they acquire conversation. Meta-analytic and national-review evidence supports systematic phonics as an important component of beginning reading instruction, especially for word identification and spelling, when it is integrated with phonemic awareness, fluency, vocabulary, oral language, and comprehension. The defensible conclusion is not “constructivism failed,” but that immersion alone is an unreliable route to decoding for many children. The domain and the instructional target matter. 

Whole Language Through Posner's Lens: An Interpretive Application

The framework from Concept 3 can also be used interpretively — not as a direct empirical test — to ask why evidence is sometimes slow to change professional practice. Many whole-language advocates reasoned that immersion in meaningful text should produce literacy as immersion in conversation produces speech. The analogy was coherent and humane, but it generalized across domains too quickly. The cards below apply Posner's questions to that professional schema while distinguishing evidence from interpretation.

Condition 1 — Dissatisfaction
Failure was easy to misread
Some children learned to read under almost any classroom approach, while others’ difficulty could be attributed to motivation, home background, or disability. Aggregate weaknesses in decoding instruction did not always create a single, unmistakable failure event for practitioners.
Condition 2 — Intelligibility
The alternative became clearer
Research increasingly specified what systematic phonics was—and what it was not. But understanding the recommendation did not automatically make it persuasive or easy to integrate with teachers’ commitments to rich literature and meaning.
Condition 3 — Plausibility
Values shaped interpretation
For educators who associated explicit code instruction with drill or decontextualized worksheets, phonics could seem inconsistent with authentic literacy. That is an interpretive and identity problem, not evidence that educators acted in bad faith.
Condition 4 — Fruitfulness
Implementation supplied the test
The alternative becomes fruitful when teachers see students use reliable decoding to gain access to meaningful text. The strongest synthesis is not phonics instead of literature, but systematic code instruction in service of fluent, knowledgeable reading.
Retrieval practiceA principal argues: "Children learn to speak without being explicitly taught, so the same approach should work for reading." What is right about this reasoning — and what crucial distinction does it miss?►

What's right: children acquire enormous amounts of spoken language through participation and interaction rather than formal grammar lessons.

What's missed: spoken language and alphabetic reading place different demands on learners. The biologically-primary/secondary distinction is a useful framework rather than a switch: conversation is a species-typical capacity supported by ordinary social life, while decoding a culturally invented symbol system generally benefits from systematic instruction.

The practical synthesis is equally important. Systematic phonics is a component of strong reading instruction, not a complete reading curriculum. It should help students reach meaningful, knowledge-rich text—not replace it.

Major concept 6 · When a theory becomes an ideology

The Inquiry Vs. Direct Instruction Debate

I was once considering writing a book called "10 debates in education." When I fed the idea to AI, it explained to me that "those are really just one debate repeated over and over again." I thought it would be fun to discuss things like extrinsic motivation vs. intrinsic; student-centered vs. teacher-centered teaching; inquiry vs. direct instruction; and a host of others. But the AI noted that there are two broad camps that take one side or the other. 

Ideological bundling is the tendency for empirical claims, moral commitments, and political identities to cluster into packaged belief systems accepted or rejected as a whole. Politically, there is no logical reason why a belief in lower corporate taxes should dictate one's stance on environmental regulations or foreign intervention—yet party alignment links them tightly. In education, these packages operate similarly: a person’s stance on reading instruction or standardized testing often gets bundled with their views on school choice or sex education. Rejecting even a single piece of the bundle feels like a betrayal of the entire tribe.

What are the two camps? For the purposes of this chapter, we will stylize them as the "progressive" bundle and the "traditionalist" bundle.

Progressive Bundle
Learning theory: Constructivism — active, self-directed exploration
Motivation: Intrinsic — curiosity and autonomy must be protected
Curriculum: Skills over content; student-designed inquiry
Teacher's role: Guide on the side; facilitator of discovery
Assessment: Authentic, portfolio-based; standardized tests are reductive
Political valence: Progressive, egalitarian, liberation framing
Traditionalist Bundle
Learning theory: Explicit instruction — systematic, teacher-led transmission
Motivation: Extrinsic — structured rewards and accountability work
Curriculum: Knowledge-rich; sequenced and cumulative
Teacher's role: Sage on the stage; expert and instructor
Assessment: Objective, standardized; accountability matters
Political valence: Traditional, orderly, academic excellence framing
How to read these columns: They are ideal types designed to expose bundling, not survey categories or descriptions of every educator. Real teachers mix commitments from both columns.

Piaget's constructivism was a theory about how learners build understanding. In some educational movements, that descriptive claim was translated into a prescriptive moral stance: because learners construct knowledge, teachers ought to minimize explanation and maximize self-directed discovery. Memorization is bad teaching, and inquiry is good teaching. But this movement doesn't restrict itself to teaching methods or educational strategies: it includes everything from favoring intrinsic motivation to emphasizing formative assessment.

To keep the distinction clear, this chapter will use the term constructivism for the descriptive theory of learning that came from Piaget and educational progressivism for a narrower ideological package that treats inquiry as morally superior, the teacher primarily as facilitator, and explicit teaching as suspect. That label does not describe every progressive educator or exhaust the progressive tradition. This is a highly stylized use of the term for the purposes of exploring the problems with ideological bundling. 

Which camp should you join?

Neither. 

In fact, I genuinely hope that you take the best of both camps, and use them in your classroom. The purpose of this part of the chapter is to show you the problems with joining an ideological camp. You have a better option: follow the evidence. If there isn't any, wait patiently, and hold your convictions loosely. 

One classroom method that is associated with progressivism is inquiry-based learning, sometimes grouped with discovery learning. The progressive camp, jumping on the Piaget bandwagon, pushed hard for less memorization, more inquiry, and stronger emphasis on higher-order thinking. But the inference does not follow: just because Piaget was right, that does not mean that direct instruction is always suspect. Learners construct understanding during lectures, worked examples, guided practice, discussion, and inquiry alike.

Instead of being a progressive or a traditionalist, your job is to be a scholar-practitioner. 

The scholar-practitioner looks to the evidence. What does it say about the inquiry debate?

Research supports an important distinction: unassisted discovery tends to underperform explicit instruction, while enhanced or guided discovery can outperform comparison conditions. In science education, inquiry studies also show better outcomes when teachers provide substantial guidance. Inquiry is therefore a method whose success depends on design—not a creed that becomes more authentic as guidance disappears. 

Neither bundle gets the evidence right when adopted wholesale. Minimally guided discovery can leave novices without the knowledge or feedback needed to learn efficiently; instruction reduced to recitation and compliance can likewise produce brittle performance without explanation or transfer. Research on inquiry repeatedly finds that guidance matters, while research on explicit teaching does not imply that students should never discuss, investigate, or solve unfamiliar problems. The defensible position is methodological pluralism: acceptance of a variety of positions so long as they are evidence-based and disciplined by learning goals instead of ideology. 

After all of that — the theory, the steelman, the complications, the bundles — the empirical question can finally be asked clearly. Not "inquiry or direct instruction?" That is the wrong question. The right questions are: when does each approach work, for which students, at which phase of learning?

John Hattie's Visible Learning database is frequently invoked as evidence in such arguments. It should not be read that way. The database is updated as meta-analyses are added, the categories aggregate different studies and implementations, and the estimates are not a randomized head-to-head comparison of “inquiry” versus “direct instruction.” The current figures are still useful, but only with their date and limitations attached. Ultimately, however, Hattie has noted that teachers tend to speak highly of inquiry despite lower scores than direct instruction, and a need for very deliberate design.

Effect-size caution: In MetaX, 0.40 is the average across listed influences and is described as roughly a year's typical growth—not a bright-line threshold separating “meaningful” from “meaningless.” The inquiry estimate has changed from the 0.31 discussed in 2016 to 0.51 in the February 2026 database; direct instruction is currently 0.56. These figures summarize different literatures and should not be interpreted as a clean causal contest. (Hattie, 2016; Visible Learning MetaX, 2026)

The broader research supports a guidance argument, not a ban on inquiry. Unassisted discovery tends to be inefficient, especially for novices; guided inquiry and enhanced discovery can improve learning when teachers structure the task, supply feedback, and ensure that students have enough knowledge to interpret what they encounter. Prior knowledge matters, but so do the quality and timing of guidance. Rote memorization can boost learning, and worked examples and direct instruction consistently rank as effective methods. Similarly, the evidence seems to suggest a phased model: inquiry during initial learning is ineffective because students have no foundation. Inquiry, when appropriately scaffolded, can be fertile ground for learning when it comes time for deep learning and transfer.

Note that some of the debate is not empirical at all. No effect size tells you how much autonomy students should have over their own learning. No meta-analysis settles whose knowledge counts as worth teaching, or what school is fundamentally for. These are normative questions. They require values clarification and democratic deliberation, not research synthesis. The scholar-practitioner knows which kind of question they are in — and responds accordingly.

Retrieval practiceA colleague says: “The current Hattie numbers prove direct instruction beats inquiry, so I never use inquiry.” What does a careful reading of the evidence require you to say?►

First, date the numbers. The 0.31 inquiry estimate in Hattie's 2016 discussion is not the current MetaX value. As of February 2026, MetaX reports 0.51 for inquiry-based teaching and 0.56 for direct instruction.

Second, do not treat the database as a head-to-head trial. The two labels summarize different collections of meta-analyses, populations, outcomes, and implementations. A difference of .05 between aggregated categories cannot establish that one method should replace the other everywhere.

Third, examine guidance and learning phase. Explicit modeling and guided practice are often efficient for building new knowledge and procedures. Inquiry can be productive when it is guided, when students have relevant knowledge, and when the goal is explanation, integration, or application. The evidence rejects “never inquire” just as surely as it rejects “never explain.”

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

Constructivism is not an argument for one teaching method. It is a theory about how learners interpret and reorganize knowledge, and its classroom implications depend on the domain, task, learner, and quality of guidance.

  • Elicit relevant prior knowledge before or early in a unit. Use it diagnostically: identify useful connections, missing foundations, and misconceptions rather than treating activation as a ritual that is always beneficial.
  • When explanation does not change a misconception, diagnose why. Cognitive conflict can help, but pair it with a clear alternative, bridging examples, evidence, guided comparison, and practice using the new model.
  • Choose opening examples with care, then vary them. Use clear central cases to establish a category and boundary cases to prevent an overly narrow prototype.
  • Use Bruner's modes flexibly and connect them explicitly. Concrete objects and diagrams are useful when they reveal structure; symbols are useful when students can map them back to meaning. Do not preserve a manipulative after it becomes a distraction.
  • Use the biologically-primary/culturally-secondary distinction as a heuristic, not a binary. Ask how much ordinary participation is likely to produce the target learning and where systematic modeling, explanation, and practice are needed.
  • When a pedagogical argument feels compelling partly because of its political valence, ask which bundle is pulling you. Then evaluate the specific empirical claim on its own evidence. The scholar-practitioner disaggregates — taking what each tradition gets right without adopting either wholesale.
Chapter synthesis
A toddler once called a cat a “dog,” and she was reasoning from the best framework she had. That is constructivism in miniature: new information is interpreted through structures already present. The instructional consequence is not that teachers should retreat. It is that teaching must work on those structures—activating what is useful, exposing what is limited, supplying intelligible alternatives, and giving students opportunities to use them. The theory became distorted when an account of learning was turned into a command to minimize guidance. Evidence on conceptual change, beginning reading, representations, and inquiry points toward a more demanding synthesis: neither transmission nor discovery is sufficient as an ideology. The scholar-practitioner's job is to choose and sequence support according to the knowledge, task, learner, and goal.
Learners construct knowledge always — even during direct instruction. The question is never whether learning should be constructive. The question is what conditions best support the construction this learner needs to do right now.
Assimilation can create an illusion of understanding. Conceptual change often requires students to notice the limits of an existing model—but conflict works best when paired with explanation, a credible alternative, and supported practice.
The biologically-primary/culturally-secondary distinction helps explain why immersion is enough for some forms of learning but unreliable for others. It is a useful continuum and design question, not a simple natural-versus-invented switch.
Research on guidance reframes the inquiry-versus-direct-instruction debate. Explicit teaching, guided inquiry, and open application are tools with different strengths; none earns universal priority from a single effect-size ranking.

  1. Your brain thinks in examples, not definitions.
  2. Your brain avoids thinking, so make the right decision once.
  3. What you already know decides what you can learn.
  4. Every mind constructs. The teacher chooses the materials.
  5. Build it right the first time.
  6. To change a schema, roll out the red carpet. Then tug on it.
