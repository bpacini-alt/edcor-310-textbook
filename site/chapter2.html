Cognitive Biases

The Biggest Problem Is You

Chapter 2~ 40 min read

In the dark middle years of the Second World War, a British university professor took to writing an essay to warn people about a menacing problem. He had been watching rational, educated human beings swept into atrocity by the systematic manipulation of their instincts.

He published his warning in a slim, dense volume called The Abolition of Man. At the time, he was primarily known as a rigorous academic and a broadcaster of Christian apologetics. It would be nearly a decade before he would use his fiction to illustrate exactly what he meant—by writing about a world where it is "always winter, but never Christmas;" about four young people in a magical land; and a great, golden lion who rules the land with majesty and love.

His name? C.S. Lewis.

Decades of experimental psychology now confirm what Lewis had already seen with unusual clarity: there are two thinking systems within us, and it is a bad path to assume that the thinking brain is somehow superior to the feeling one. Telling people to turn off their feelings is as impossible as it is counterproductive. Lewis' goal was integration: to strengthen, train, and better link both systems as a defense against the propagandist's art.

Chapter 1 told you what is right with you — that you are antifragile, capable of being formed by difficulty into something better than you are. This chapter explains what is wrong with you: you are hopelessly flawed. Your rational mind is overpowered by an emotional core; and even your rational mind has blind spots that are nearly impossible to correct.

In short, the biggest obstacle to you finding the truth is you. It is the systematic, well-documented, largely invisible ways your own mind bends what you see, distorts what you believe, and constructs confident explanations for things that never happened the way you remember them. And it will do all of this without notifying you.

The big picture
The mind operates via two systems: one fast, automatic, and largely unconscious — the other slow, deliberate, and effortful. We tend to believe the slow system is in charge. The evidence is clear that it mostly isn't. Most of what we believe, judge, and decide is produced by the fast system and then explained — post-hoc, plausibly, convincingly — by the slow one. Even our rational mind has blind spots. This has consequences for how we learn, how we teach, how we reason about evidence, and what we have to do if we want our professional intuitions to actually be trustworthy. The real key to teaching is not merely building better thinking brains, but better intuitions.

Major concept 1 · The Rider Believes it Steers

Dual-Process Theory

C.S. Lewis noticed, writing in the early 1940s, that human beings seem to be built of two systems that do not always cooperate: the stomach— the appetites, the instincts, the gut — and the head— the rational faculty, the deliberate mind, the capacity for logical analysis. His colleagues saw the stomach as the problem. Lewis saw the relationship between them as the real issue.

Decades later, psychologist Daniel Kahneman received a Nobel Prize for research that gave Lewis's intuition scientific clothing. Kahneman demonstrated dual process theory: the mind operates via two fundamentally different systems. System I is fast and automatic — it recognizes faces, reads emotional tone, finishes familiar sentences, and navigates routine situations without deliberate effort. System II is slow and deliberate — it handles hard problems, complex analysis, and careful decisions. It is also, crucially, lazy. Given the option, System II prefers to endorse whatever System I has already produced rather than doing independent analytical work.

We tend to believe System II is in charge — that we reason our way to conclusions, then act on them. Kahneman's research suggests this picture is almost backwards. System I produces the conclusion. System II constructs the justification — a post-hoc rationalization. The reasoning is not the cause of the belief. It is the cover story.

Jonathan Haidt gave this dynamic its most memorable image: the Elephant and the Rider. The elephant is System I — massive, powerful, going where it wants. The rider is System II — perched on top, holding the reins, genuinely believing it is steering. In reality, when the elephant decides to go somewhere, the rider goes along. And the rider's most consistent function, Haidt argued, is generating plausible-sounding reasons for wherever the elephant has already decided to go.

Research studyMoral Dumbfounding — Haidt, Bjorklund & Murphy (2000)

Haidt presented participants with scenarios carefully designed to produce instant moral disgust while being rationally harmless. In one famous version, an experimenter produces a cockroach — laboratory-raised, clean-environment, autoclave-sterilized. No germs, no health risk. The experimenter dips it into a glass of apple juice and asks the participant to drink. Almost no one will. When pressed — there is literally no rational reason not to, no health risk of any kind — the justifications fall apart quickly. Most participants end up saying some version of:I just can't.

The rider scrambled for reasons. The elephant had already decided. That is moral dumbfounding: the stubborn maintenance of a judgment even after every supporting reason has failed — because the judgment was never produced by the reasoning in the first place. This is a simple, clear example of just how powerful system I is: much of our morality is centered in the power of the elephant not the rider.

Haidt has since argued that much of our linguistic capacity is not there to help us reach truth, but rather, to help us justify our answers. This is certainly the case in moral dumbfounding: the conclusion came first. System II was recruited afterward to justify it. When the justifications ran out, the refusal remained. We are not, it seems, rational actors with occasional emotions; we are emotional creatures with occasional bursts of rational-sounding justification.

Important note — Teaching implication

If judgments are produced by the elephant and explained by the rider, then logical argument alone is not how minds change. This points in two directions for teachers. First: when you want students to genuinely update their thinking — not recite a correct answer but actually revise their model of the world — argument alone is insufficient. You have to reach the elephant. Second, and more personally: the affective domain that Chapter 1 described is the elephant. When Bloom said education should form values and not merely transmit knowledge, he was describing the project of elephant-training. That is what your best teaching will attempt.

Retrieval practiceA teacher hears a compelling, well-evidenced argument that contradicts a deeply held belief about how children learn. She listens carefully, acknowledges the evidence, and then continues teaching exactly as before. Using dual process theory, explain what happened.▸

The teacher's rider heard the argument and may have conceded the point intellectually. But the rider does not control behavior — the elephant does. And the elephant was not moved. It holds the intuition that the existing approach is correct, built through years of practice and professional identity. A single argument, however well-constructed, did not reach it.

This is not hypocrisy or willful ignorance. It is how minds actually work. Changing the elephant requires something argument alone cannot provide: emotional salience, repeated exposure, social reinforcement, or a concrete experience that reframes the intuition at its root. The teacher who wants to genuinely update their practice needs more than information. They need their elephant moved. That is a different project — and a harder one.

Major concept 2 · You Cannot see what you cannot see

Cognitive Biases

Complete this lyric from the Latter-day Saint children's hymn "Give Said the Little Stream":

"I'm small I know, but wherever I go, the _____ grow greener still."

If you said grass, you are in very good company. You are also wrong. The correct word is fields. Research on this specific lyric found that approximately 82 percent of respondents confidently filled in "grass" — most of them surprised to learn the correct answer. The song has been sung across decades in Primary programs and family home evenings, and most people who know it have been quietly singing the wrong word with complete confidence that they had it right.

This is not a story about a song. It is a story about the structure of human knowing. You can hold a belief with complete confidence — with no felt uncertainty whatsoever — and be wrong. The certainty is not evidence of correctness. It is simply a feature of how memory works, independent of whether the memory is accurate.


Figure 2.1.Cover your left eye. Fix your right eye on the cross. Slowly move toward the screen. At roughly 10–14 inches, the circle vanishes — and your brain fills in background as though nothing were there. You are not seeing a gap. You are seeing your brain's approximation of what it thinks should be there.

Everyone has a literal blind spot. At the back of each eye, the optic nerve connects to the retina — and at that point there are no photoreceptors. Light that falls there produces no signal. The result is a gap in your visual field large enough to swallow a fist at arm's length. You have never noticed it, because your brain fills it in seamlessly, silently, without notifying you. You do not experience a gap. You experience continuous, uninterrupted vision — which is an approximation your brain constructed and delivered as though it were reality. That approximation is called a confabulation: an image your brain constructs despite lacking the real information.

See below for more examples. These are not party tricks. They demonstrate a single principle that extends far beyond vision: your brain produces a seamless, confident picture of reality — and some of that picture is fabricated. You do not experience a gap. You do not see the seam. There is no gaping hole in your vision. Your brain fills it in, and leaves you feeling sane and confident. Cognitive blind spots work exactly the same way. They are not areas where you feel uncertain. They are areas where your brain produces confident, coherent output — and the output is partially invented.

Figure 2.3.Blue and black, or white and gold? The dress is objectively blue and black. Many people cannot stop seeing it as white and gold — even after being shown the actual colors. Knowing the truth does not change the perception. The machinery continues doing what it does regardless of what the rider now knows.


These errors cluster and repeat in predictable ways, which means they can be named. There are dozens of these, but here are several of the most consequential for educators:

Heuristics are what make rapid judgment possible. It is critical to note that these shortcuts are fundamentally good: by instantly sorting a chaotic world, they preserve massive amounts of mental resources that are critical for higher-level thinking.

View Example: First Impressions

Our brains rely heavily on these shortcuts when forming first impressions. When meeting a new class or a new colleague, you automatically assess their competence, warmth, and predictability within seconds based on tiny, surface-level cues. This rapid sorting keeps you from experiencing cognitive overload in a fast-paced school environment, even though it occasionally locks in a biased assumption before you have real data.

Confirmation bias shapes which evidence you even notice. It tricks us by transforming our internal assumptions and comforting feelings into what feels like hard, objective reality, quietly filtering out anything that might disrupt our worldview.

View Example: The Illusion of Rereading

Consider how students study: rereading a textbook chapter feels incredibly good. Because the words look familiar, the brain misinterprets this ease of processing as actual mastery, confirming the student's belief that they "know the material." The brain greedily accepts this comforting illusion of competence and completely ignores the fact that they haven't actually practiced retrieving the information from scratch. In reality, what works best feels worst: quizzing yourself, teaching the material to someone else, and deep processing.

Confabulation means that the explanations we give for our own behavior are often constructed after the fact. We are narrative engines, instantly generating a logical, honest-feeling backstory for actions we took for completely different, unconscious reasons.

View Example: The Brain Stimulation Studies

The classic demonstration of this comes from neuroscience. When researchers applied a mild electrical stimulus to a specific part of a patient's brain to physically force a sudden laugh, the patient didn't say, "My muscle twitched because you shocked me." Instead, their conscious mind instantly invented a story, with the patient smiling and saying, "Oh, you doctors are just really funny!" They genuinely believed their own fabricated explanation.

Reactance is the resistance that fires when a belief or autonomy feels threatened. Reactance deserves a concrete illustration, because abstract definitions slide off without one.

View Example: "But. I. Took. A. Quiz!"

My wife was in graduate school pursuing a doctorate in audiology. Her roommate was completing a PhD in educational psychology. One afternoon, the roommate mentioned matter-of-factly that learning styles were not supported by research — the VARK model had been comprehensively studied and found not to predict learning outcomes. My wife (who had taken a quiz in third grade identifying herself as a visual learner) replied with what she later described as righteous indignation: "But. I. Took. A. Quiz!"

Her response was superbly human. Her response was not to examine the evidence. It was to become angry. This is instructive because she is not unusually stubborn — she is, if anything, on the gracious and even-keeled side of humanity. She was not behaving unusually. She was behaving normally. The reactance was automatic and immediate, triggered before any rational evaluation could occur. The elephant heard a threat and moved toward it.

The Dunning-Kruger Effect means the people most confident they know how to teach are often the ones who know the least — because ignorance cannot accurately assess itself.

View Example: The Outside Observer

Consider a well-meaning outside observer or a brand-new volunteer walking into a chaotic school environment. They watch a highly complex classroom for ten minutes and confidently think, "This looks simple, they just need stricter rules." Because they lack training in behavioral management, they are completely blind to the intricate choreography of group dynamics, emotional safety, and timing that an expert teacher is balancing in that exact same room.

And the Curse of Knowledge means that the more expert you become, the harder it is to remember what it was like not to know what you know. This is the most structurally dangerous bias a teacher can carry, because teaching requires, as its core function, accurate access to the state of not knowing.

View Example: The Veteran Expert

This shows up when a brilliant, twenty-year veteran math teacher explains a "simple" algebraic step. When the students stare blankly, the teacher is genuinely baffled. Because the math has been hardwired into their brain for decades, the expert literally cannot remember what it feels like to look at that exact same equation and see nothing but a terrifying, meaningless wall of abstract symbols.

Important note — The Planck Principle

Max Planck observed that a new scientific truth does not triumph by convincing its opponents — it triumphs because its opponents eventually die and a new generation grows up familiar with it. "Science advances one funeral at a time." A striking data point: approximately 32 percent of university faculty continue to teach learning styles after being directly shown the evidence that it is not supported by research. Not because they are unintelligent — because the elephant is genuinely hard to move. The practical implication is that blind spots cannot be corrected by trying harder. You cannot try harder to see your visual blind spot. You can only build external structures that compensate: peer observation, student feedback, deliberate reflection practices, and checks that do not rely on the same machinery producing the gap.

There are many good answers, but here are two.

The Curse of Knowledge could be considered the most dangerous bias for teachers because it turns a virtue into a vice: the teacher who loves their content and knows their stuff actually begins to get worse at the job. The curse of knowledge attacks the core function of the job itself. The more expert you become in your subject, the harder it is to simulate genuine confusion. It slowly becomes harder to see through the students' fresh eyes: I cannot remember what it feels like to not know this.

Or, perhaps confirmation biasdeserves the top spot — it filters the evidence you would use to correct any other bias, making it a kind of meta-bias. The good news is that confirmation bias is at least partially correctable through deliberate data-gathering. The Curse of Knowledge is harder, because expertise itself is the cause. You cannot un-expertise yourself. The compensation has to be more structural: listening to how students describe their confusion rather than re-explaining, and treating wrong answers as diagnostic rather than deficient.

Major concept 3 · The need for a better Elephant

Learning Science and Intuitions

Everything in this chapter so far has been a warning about System I — about the ways fast, automatic thinking misleads you. That warning stands. But there is a complication worth sitting with before moving on, because the corrective is not simply to think more slowly.

The dual process story told so far — System I fast and error-prone, System II slow and corrective — is accurate as far as it goes. But education researcher Carl Hendrick has begun to argue that the popular version of this story may have been overlearned in one direction. Schools have absorbed the lesson that fast thinking is unreliable and slow thinking is careful. Our entire language has formed around this same idea: we give students time to apply the algorithm, we encourage them to slow down and go step-by-step, and we equate intellectual seriousness with visible deliberate effort, on the assumption that slow, careful work is good work.

A study published in Cognition complicates this picture. Researchers gave participants creative word-association problems under conditions designed to prevent deliberate thinking: a tight time limit combined with a concurrent memory task. Participants were given time to deliberate only after they had completed the original task. The result was striking: when participants eventually produced the correct answer, they had already produced it in the constrained intuitive phase approximately 70 percent of the time. System II deliberation did not drive correct responses; more often than not, deliberation functioned as justification of what the intuitive mind had already done.

We have fallen into Lewis' trap but in the sphere of education. And it seems that building up the elephant isn't just useful for the philosophers, but potentially for the educators as well.

What predicted who got it right intuitively was not how much they knew — it was how their knowledge was organized. Participants who solved problems intuitively were those whose knowledge was structured like a well-cross-referenced library: related ideas close together, concepts linked across domains, retrieval fast because the architecture made relevant associations immediately available. As Hendrick summarizes: it is not so much what you know, but the way in which you know it.

The implications are significant. We tend to think that the goal is accurate solving of problems instead of relying on intuitions; Hendrick seems to be implying that the purpose of solving these problems by algorithm is to build high-quality intuitions. A well-organized, richly interconnected knowledge base does not merely support deliberate reasoning — it produces correct intuitions in the first place. The goal of education, on this view, cannot simply be to teach students to slow down and think carefully. It must also be to build the kind of semantic architecture that makes their fast thinking reliable. And the same applies to you — as a learner in this course and as the teacher you are becoming.

While the research on academic intuitions is only preliminary, the research on how experts tend to think vs. how novices think is quite advanced. Here are five findings from that literature that broadly support the above point.

How Experts Think — Five Research Findings

FindingWhat This Looks Like
Experts see meaningful patterns; novices see isolated facts

When presented with a problem, experts quickly recognize underlying structures. Novices focus on surface features.

  • Physics experts group problems by principles (e.g., conservation of energy)
  • Novices group them by appearances (e.g., "inclined plane problem," "pulley problem")
Experts have richly organized knowledge

Experts possess large networks of interconnected knowledge rather than disconnected facts. Because of this:

  • They retrieve information more efficiently
  • They make more inferences
  • They solve problems faster and more accurately

Knowledge is not the opposite of thinking — it is what makes sophisticated thinking possible.

Experts automate routine processes

Many basic operations become automatic through practice. For example:

  • Skilled readers don't consciously decode words
  • Experienced mathematicians don't consciously work through basic arithmetic

This frees working memory for higher-level reasoning.

Experts often underestimate what novices need (Curse of Knowledge)

Once something becomes automatic, experts forget:

  • What was difficult
  • Which background knowledge is required
  • How many steps beginners must consciously manage

This is why subject-matter expertise alone does not guarantee teaching expertise.

Novices benefit from more guidance than experts

Experts can learn effectively through exploration because they already possess substantial mental models. Novices typically learn more from:

  • Explicit instruction
  • Worked examples
  • Modeling
  • Scaffolding

As knowledge grows, support can gradually fade.

How to build expert thinking—and intuitions

If the elephant runs on interconnected intuitions rather than deliberate reasoning, then the strategies that actually reach it are different from the ones we typically use. Logical argument addresses the rider. What reaches the elephant is narrative— stories that make an idea emotionally real rather than merely intellectually available; repeated exposure across varied contexts, which builds the semantic connections that make an idea feel true; relationship and trust, because we update intuitions more readily from people we are connected to than from strangers making arguments; and concrete experience — actually doing the thing, seeing the outcome, having the moment where an abstract principle becomes a felt reality.

These same mechanisms apply when the intuitions we are trying to build are academic ones — the felt sense that a historical argument needs evidence, that a mathematical pattern deserves scrutiny, that a claim and its justification are different things. Academic intuitions are no different in kind from any other: they are fast, automatic responses built slowly through experience. The difference is that they are rarely cultivated deliberately.

While the research on building academic intuitions hasn't solidified yet, it does have suggestions about some things that can be done. Note: Many of these terms are ones we have not read about yet. We will discuss them in the subsequent chapters.

Major concept 4 · The Path to a Better Elephant

The Affective Domain and Formation

We return, now, to Lewis. His colleagues' response to the problem he identified — that human beings are driven by instinct and emotion and are therefore vulnerable to manipulation — was to train young people to distrust their emotional responses. Strengthen the head. Weaken the stomach. Build analytical, detached, critical minds. Lewis thought this was catastrophically wrong, and he said so:

"The task of the modern educator is not to cut down jungles but to irrigate deserts. The right defence against false sentiments is to inculcate just sentiments. By starving the sensibility of our pupils we only make them easier prey to the propagandist when he comes. For famished nature will be avenged and a hard heart is no infallible protection against a soft head."— C.S. Lewis, The Abolition of Man(1943)

The chest, in Lewis's framework, is the mediating faculty between head and stomach — the seat of trained emotional response: sentiment directed toward the right objects, feelings that have been cultivated rather than simply inherited or suppressed. A person with a well-developed chest does not merely know that honesty matters; they feel the pull of it, recoil from the temptation to deceive, and act honestly even when no one would know.

A Man Without a Chest knows honesty matters — can argue for it, cite the philosophical literature, win a debate about it — and still lies when it is convenient, because the knowledge never reached the gut. The rider learned something. The elephant did not.

Lewis, Kahneman, and Haidt are all describing the same phenomenon from different vantage points. The elephant drives behavior. Training only the rider — building analytical capacity without cultivating the emotional core — produces people who can reason about virtue without being virtuous. The solution is not a weaker elephant. It is a better-trained one.

Consider a seventh-grade math class. Students arrive with reliable intuitions: addition makes things bigger, subtraction makes them smaller, multiplication makes them bigger. These intuitions work through third grade — and then fall apart completely when fractions and negative numbers appear. What they need is not a new algorithm so much as a new intuition — a higher-resolution picture of how numbers behave that fires automatically before deliberate calculation begins. The teacher who gives them the procedure without training the underlying intuition produces students who can follow steps but collapse when the steps don't apply. The job is not just to fill the head. It is to retrain the gut.

Catholic teachings have long held that we are given a conscience from God, but we have a duty to educate our conscience. Latter-day Saints hold a similar belief. It is not enough to simply ignore our desires, emotions, and impulses; we are required to elevate, refine, and educate them.

This is what this course is attempting — for you, in the domain of your own cognition and professional judgment. The biases named in this chapter are real. But knowing their names is the rider's work, and the rider is not enough. The goal is a different kind of knowing: the kind that changes what fires automatically when you encounter a student who is struggling, a research claim that feels convenient, or a colleague's practice that strikes you as wrong. That is what Aristotle called virtue, what Bloom called the affective domain, and what Lewis called the chest.

Important note — What the holy art of skepticism means, and does not mean

The original title of this chapter was "The Holy Art of Skepticism." I still feel strongly that one of the best ways to improve yourself as a person is to question: question your emotions, question your beliefs, question what you assume is true. The woman or man who is willing to introspect at that level of honesty will be a powerful thinker as a result.

But there are different meanings of the word skeptic, and it is worth differentiating between them. Nietzche spoke of those "who worshipped the question mark" as though it were God.

Our purpose in naming cognitive failures is not to produce question-mark worshippers — people who define their intellectual identity by tearing down others' beliefs, more comfortable destroying than building. There is a real difference between the genuine skeptic (the seeker after truth) and the cynic (who merely pokes holes for reputational points).

The cynic merely doubts. The genuine skeptic investigates. Miguel de Unamuno put it well: "Skeptic does not mean him who doubts, but him who investigates or researches as opposed to him who asserts and thinks that he has found." This chapter is an initiation into the holy art of skepticism — not as cynicism, but as inquiry. The goal is a mind that examines its own machinery with the same rigor it applies to everything else. That requires courage.

Retrieval practiceWhat is the difference between a Man Without a Chest and an antifragile person — and which one does this course aim to produce?▸

AMan Without a Chesthas knowledge without formation. He can reason about the right thing to do but does not feel pulled toward it. His head is developed; his emotional core has been trained out of him. He knows; he does not become. His rider has learned the right answers. His elephant is still going wherever it wants.

Anantifragile personis transformed by difficulty — emerging from genuine challenge with stronger intuitions, deeper values, and more capable responses than they brought into it. Their chest has grown through the ordeal. The difficulty did not leave them intact (resilience) or broken (fragility). It made them.

This course aims to produce the second. The cognitive failures described in this chapter are not merely interesting facts about human psychology. They are the stressor — the specific ordeal this chapter asks you to pass through honestly. The goal is not a student who can identify cognitive biases on an exam. It is a student whose intuitions about evidence, about students, and about their own judgment have genuinely changed.

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

Every concept in this chapter has a direct analog in the work of teaching. These are not abstract psychological findings — they are the daily mechanics of how you will read students, receive feedback, process research, and make decisions under pressure.

  • When you want a student to genuinely update their thinking — not comply, but actually revise — argument alone is unlikely to work. Design experiences that move the elephant: narrative, relationship, and concrete encounter with the idea.
  • Your blind spots are structurally invisible to you. Build external compensation: peer observation, student feedback that doesn't rely on your own interpretation, and explicit reflection practices that force you to encounter disconfirming data.
  • When presenting research findings to colleagues or parents, apply the guillotine on yourself first. Be clear about which part of your claim is empirical and which part is a value judgment. This builds credibility and prevents the conversation from going sideways.
  • Reactance means that direct contradiction is often counterproductive — with students and with colleagues. When you need someone to update a belief, narrative and relationship tend to get further than argument. Design accordingly.
  • The Curse of Knowledge is your most constant professional hazard. Get in the habit of asking students to narrate their confusion — not to explain the content back to you, but to show you where their picture of it breaks down. That is your diagnostic.
Chapter synthesis
The biggest problem is not ignorance. It is the way the mind compounds ignorance with confidence, bends perception toward what it already believes, fills in gaps without notification, and generates convincing explanations for conclusions it reached by other means entirely. Lewis saw this problem in the 1940s and argued that the rationalist solution — train the head, suppress the stomach — was making it worse. Kahneman spent a career demonstrating the mechanism. The solution is not a smarter rider. It is a better-trained elephant. That is a longer, harder, and more important project — and it is what this course has been designed to pursue.
System I produces most of what you believe, judge, and do. System II mostly constructs the justification after the fact. Knowing this changes how you approach your own learning — and how you approach persuasion with students.
Cognitive blind spots cannot be corrected by trying harder — only by building external structures that compensate for the same machinery producing the gap.
The goal is not a Man Without a Chest — not analytical detachment from feeling — but a well-trained chest: intuitions that have been genuinely formed toward the right things. That is what Bloom called the affective domain. That is what this course is for.
