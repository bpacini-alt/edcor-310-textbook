Intrinsic Motivation

Lighting the Fire

Chapter 20~ 45 min read

The quote is everywhere. Posters, coffee mugs, Instagram captions, commencement speeches. “Education is not the filling of a pail, but the lighting of a fire.” It’s attributed to W.B. Yeats, with great confidence, on most of those surfaces. The problem: there is no evidence Yeats ever said or wrote it. The actual source appears to be Plutarch, who died in the second century AD. But here is the more interesting question: why does the quote spread so reliably despite the wrong attribution?

Because it captures something people recognize as true — something that pure behaviorism cannot account for and that sticker charts cannot manufacture. The experience of learning when you genuinely want to learn. The difference between a student who is doing the minimum to get the grade and a student who is three hours past bedtime because they cannot stop thinking about a problem. That difference is not a matter of rewards and consequences. It is a matter of whether something has been lit.

This chapter is about what it means to light that fire — and, more practically, about what most reliably puts it out before it starts.

The big picture
“Education is not the filling of a pail, but the lighting of a fire.” Misattributed to Yeats, likely Plutarch — but the sentiment captures something real: the highest form of motivation is not something you do to students, but something that ignites within them. This chapter explores the psychology of intrinsic motivation — what it is, what reliably produces it, and why building it matters far more than any sticker chart ever could.

Major concept 1 · Intrinsic Motivation

The Fire, Not the Pail

Extrinsic motivation asks: “What do I get for doing this?” Intrinsic motivation asks a fundamentally different question: “What is this, and do I want to engage with it?” The first is about transactions; the second is about identity, curiosity, and meaning. Research consistently shows that intrinsically motivated learning is deeper, more durable, more creative, and more likely to continue past the end of the class period — or the school year.

Intrinsic motivation is not the same as enjoyment. Some genuinely important work is hard, frustrating, or tedious in the moment, and intrinsically motivated students engage with it anyway — because they find meaning in the challenge or care about the outcome. What intrinsic motivation is, at its core, is a sense of ownership: the feeling that I am doing this because it connects to something real in me, not because someone is watching or rewarding. That psychological ownership is what produces the kind of deep engagement — what Csikszentmihalyi called flow — that makes learning feel less like work and more like a compelling problem you cannot put down.

The bad news for anyone who teaches: you cannot directly install intrinsic motivation in another person. You cannot make someone care. What you can do — and what the research supports — is create conditions in which caring is more likely to emerge naturally. That is what Drive Theory, Self-Determination Theory, and Maslow’s hierarchy all attempt to describe: not how to manufacture motivation from the outside, but how to stop undermining the motivation that is already there.

Intrinsic vs. extrinsic — not always a binary

Deci and Ryan’s research complicates the simple opposition between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation. They identify a continuum from purely external (doing something only because of a reward or threat) to fully internalized (doing something because it is genuinely part of who you are). Between those poles is identified regulation — where a student does an assigned task not because they love it, but because they genuinely understand why it matters and have chosen to engage with it. Getting students to that level of internalized motivation is a meaningful and achievable goal — and a more realistic target than pure intrinsic motivation for every subject.

Retrieval practiceA student tells you: “I used to love science until we started doing it for grades.” Using SDT’s motivation continuum, describe what has happened — and what specifically a teacher could do to begin reversing it.►

The student has moved along the motivation continuum from something close to intrinsic motivation toward external regulation. Grades have reframed the activity as something done for evaluation rather than for its own sake — the overjustification effect operating through a formal reward system. The external incentive (the grade) has become the perceived reason for engaging, and the original internal interest has been crowded out. The student’s own framing tells you this: “until we started doing it for grades” is a precise description of the moment the psychological meaning of the activity shifted.

The goal of any reversal is not to remove grades — that’s not realistically available — but to move the student toward identified regulation: genuine endorsement of why the learning matters, independent of the grade. Concrete teacher moves: explain rationale that connects to something the student actually cares about, not “because you need this for the test” but “because this explains something you’ve probably wondered about”; create low-stakes opportunities to explore out of curiosity, where the grade is minimal or absent; use informational feedback that emphasizes what the student is learning and how their thinking is developing, rather than only where it ranks; and offer genuine choices within assignments that reintroduce a sense of authorship over the work. The interest was there before the grades arrived. The task is to make it visible alongside them again.

Major concept 2 · Drive Reduction Theory

Motivation as Drive Reduction — and Why That’s Not the Whole Story

Before the frameworks explored in the rest of this chapter, one of the dominant models of motivation in psychology was drive reduction theory. The basic idea, drawing heavily on behaviorism and early physiology, was that behavior is driven by biological tensions — drives — that organisms are motivated to reduce. Hunger is a drive; eating reduces it. Thirst is a drive; drinking reduces it. The organism’s goal, on this account, is essentially homeostasis: returning the body to a neutral, equilibrium state.

Drive reduction theory has real explanatory power for basic survival behaviors. It captures something true about how discomfort motivates action. But it fails almost immediately when applied to the full range of human motivation. Why do people seek out frightening movies? Why do children play, even when they are not hungry or cold? Why do scientists stay up late pursuing questions they cannot easily answer? Why does boredom feel aversive rather than like a comfortable neutral state? None of these behaviors reduce drives; they create or maintain arousal. Drive reduction theory has no good account of curiosity, creativity, aesthetics, meaning-seeking, or the love of learning for its own sake.

The limitations of drive reduction opened the door to the richer motivational frameworks that followed — including optimal arousal theory (which proposes that organisms seek a moderate level of stimulation, not zero), Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, and eventually the autonomy-mastery-purpose architecture of Drive Theory. Understanding where drive reduction falls short helps clarify what it means to be intrinsically motivated: not the relief of tension, but the active pursuit of engagement, growth, and meaning.

Retrieval practiceGive two examples of student behavior that drive reduction theory explains well — and two that it cannot explain. What does this limitation reveal about human motivation that any theory of teaching must account for?►

Explains well: A student who hasn’t eaten is distracted and unfocused — hunger (a biological drive) competes with attention. A student who is cold, physically uncomfortable, or exhausted struggles to focus — discomfort creates a drive that demands attention until resolved. Drive reduction correctly predicts that meeting these basic needs is a prerequisite for accessing higher-level engagement.

Explains poorly: A student who voluntarily spends hours working on a challenging project that was only assigned for a few minutes of class — no drive is being reduced; engagement is being pursued. A student who reads ahead in a textbook out of curiosity, or who keeps asking questions after the discussion ends. These behaviors increase arousal rather than reducing it. If the goal were homeostasis, these students would stop.

What this reveals: Human beings are not motivated primarily by the elimination of discomfort. They are motivated by engagement, meaning, growth, and connection — things that require active pursuit, not passive relief. Any theory of teaching that only accounts for basic needs (the floor) misses the entire upper range of what motivates students to do their best work. SDT, Drive Theory, and Maslow all start where drive reduction stops.

Major concept 3 · Maslow’s Hierarchy

The Pyramid You’ve Seen (and Its Complications)

Abraham Maslow proposed his hierarchy of needs in 1943, and it remains one of the most recognized frameworks in psychology — reproduced in introductory textbooks, corporate training slideshows, and countless classroom posters. The core idea is elegant: human beings are motivated by a hierarchy of needs, and lower-level needs must be reasonably satisfied before higher-level needs become dominant motivational forces. A person who is hungry is not thinking about self-actualization; a person who does not feel physically safe cannot focus on belonging; a person who lacks belonging cannot build self-esteem from the inside.

Self-Actualization
The realization of one’s full potential: creativity, personal growth, pursuit of meaning, and becoming who one is capable of becoming. Maslow viewed this not as a static endpoint but as a direction — a way of orienting one’s life. In education, self-actualization is the long-horizon goal: we are not just filling students with content, we are creating conditions under which they might grow into the fullest version of themselves.
Esteem
Both self-esteem (confidence in one’s own competence and worth) and the esteem of others (recognition, status, and respect). This level connects directly to Pink’s mastery and SDT’s competence need. Students who feel chronically disrespected — by peers, by teachers, or by a system that consistently tells them they are behind — struggle to access higher-level motivation.
Belonging & Love
Connection, acceptance, friendship, and a sense of belonging in one’s community. Durkheim’s research on anomie and the devastating effects of social exclusion both align with this level. For adolescents especially, peer belonging is one of the most powerful motivational forces in the school environment. This is Maslow’s structural echo of SDT’s relatedness need Ch. 18 · Belonging & Tribalism.
Safety
Physical safety, psychological safety, stability, and predictability. Students in chronically unsafe home or school environments — including those experiencing bullying, domestic instability, or community violence — cannot reliably access higher-level motivation. The ACEs (Adverse Childhood Experiences) research maps directly onto this level of the hierarchy.
Physiological
The foundation: food, water, shelter, sleep, warmth, air. Without these, nothing else in the hierarchy is accessible. A student who arrived at school without breakfast, who is cold, who did not sleep, or who is in physical discomfort is motivationally compromised at the most basic level — and no amount of engaging curriculum will fully compensate.

Maslow’s hierarchy has been enormously influential — and also significantly critiqued. The evidence does not actually support a strict hierarchical progression in which lower needs must be fully satisfied before higher ones become active. People pursue meaning and connection even in desperate circumstances; creativity and love emerge in poverty, in imprisonment, in war. The hierarchy is better understood as a set of interacting needs rather than a rigid ladder — all five levels are always present to some degree, but the urgency of each shifts with circumstances.

A sixth level: self-transcendence

In 1969, a year before his death, Maslow proposed a revision of his own hierarchy — a sixth level above self-actualization: self-transcendence. Where self-actualization is about becoming who you are most capable of being, self-transcendence moves beyond the self entirely. It describes the motivation to serve something greater than personal fulfillment: a cause, a community, a spiritual ideal, or the wellbeing of others. Maslow observed that his most psychologically healthy subjects were not primarily oriented toward their own growth — they were oriented toward contribution.

Most textbook versions still show five levels because the 1943 paper is the canonical reference and the revision never made it onto the poster. But the addition matters for teachers for a specific reason: the highest human motivation may not be individual flourishing at all, but contribution and purpose — which connects directly back to Pink’s “purpose” and SDT’s “relatedness.” A student who is genuinely motivated to help others, to add something to the world, or to serve a mission they believe in is operating at this transcendent level. It is also, not coincidentally, what the best teachers are doing.

Common misconception“I can’t change what’s happening at home, so I can’t really reach this student.”►

This reading takes Maslow too literally — and the research since 1943 does not support it. The hierarchy describes tendencies in what becomes motivationally dominant under different conditions. It was never meant to imply that teachers are powerless when lower-level needs are unmet at home.

What the evidence actually shows is nearly the opposite: the quality of teacher-student relationships is one of the most powerful predictors of academic outcomes across socioeconomic conditions. Research on resilience consistently finds that a single caring adult — a teacher who sees a student, names their potential, and holds high expectations regardless of home circumstances — is among the most protective factors available to children facing adversity. Teachers cannot eliminate food insecurity or domestic instability. But they can provide safety, belonging, esteem, and genuine purpose within the classroom — and those matter enormously, even when the levels below them are poorly met at home.

The defeatist reading of Maslow is, ironically, one of the more harmful things the hierarchy has licensed. The correct reading: knowing a student faces adversity is a signal to invest more in the relationship, not less. The hierarchy clarifies what to invest in. It is not an excuse to give up.

Retrieval practiceA student who is usually engaged and curious has suddenly become withdrawn, turned in incomplete work twice, and seems distracted during discussions. Using Maslow’s hierarchy, describe two possible explanations at different levels — and explain what teacher response each would suggest.►

Explanation 1 — Safety level: Something at home or in the school environment has become threatening or unstable — a family disruption, a bullying situation, community stress. At the safety level, cognitive resources are redirected toward threat monitoring rather than learning. Teacher response: a private, low-pressure check-in (“I’ve noticed you seem a bit off lately — is there anything I can do to help?”), connection to a school counselor if appropriate, and temporarily reducing academic pressure while the underlying situation is addressed. Pushing harder on academic demands when safety needs are unmet is counterproductive.

Explanation 2 — Belonging level: A friendship has ended, a social dynamic has shifted, or the student feels excluded or invisible in the classroom or peer group. At the belonging level, the student’s motivational attention is consumed by social monitoring. Teacher response: deliberate attention to rebuilding connection — personal check-ins, designing collaborative structures where the student is genuinely needed, creating low-stakes opportunities for social success. The academic disengagement is a symptom; the belonging deficit is the issue.

Both explanations call for relationship first, curriculum second. Maslow’s framework helps teachers see withdrawal not as attitude or laziness but as a signal that something lower in the hierarchy is calling for attention.

Major concept 4 · Drive Theory & SDT

Autonomy, Mastery, and Purpose

The most empirically grounded account of intrinsic motivation comes from Edward Deci and Richard Ryan’s decades of research in Self-Determination Theory (SDT). Their central finding is that human beings have three innate psychological needs, and when those needs are met, intrinsic motivation, well-being, and genuine learning flourish. When those needs are frustrated — regardless of what rewards are on offer — motivation withers.

In Drive: The Surprising Truth About What Motivates Us, Daniel Pink synthesized this research into a practitioner-friendly framework. He largely followed Deci and Ryan’s architecture, making one notable substitution: he replaced SDT’s third need, relatedness (feeling connected to others), with purpose (feeling connected to something larger than the self). Both dimensions belong in the classroom: students need to feel connected to their teacher and peers and to understand why their learning matters beyond the test.

Autonomy
Control over the four T’s: Task (what you work on), Time (when you do it), Technique (how you approach it), and Team (with whom you work). SDT frames this as the need to feel like the author of one’s own actions — acting from genuine choice rather than external compulsion. Even a mandatory task can feel autonomous when the student genuinely understands and endorses why it matters. Autonomy-supportive teachers explain rationale, offer options where possible, and acknowledge student perspectives even when saying no. Micromanagement kills motivation regardless of how kindly it is delivered.
Mastery
The drive to get better at something that matters. SDT calls this competence — the need to feel effective and to experience real growth. Pink reframes it as mastery, emphasizing its asymptotic quality: you can always improve, but you never fully arrive, and that endless progression is part of what makes it motivating. A growth mindset, the belief that ability develops through effort and strategy, can help. Mastery also requires optimal challenge: tasks in the zone of proximal development, informative rather than purely evaluative feedback, and opportunities to experience genuine progress. Boredom (too easy) and anxiety (too hard) undermine this need equally.
Purpose
The sense that one’s work connects to something larger than the self — a mission, a community, a value. SDT’s parallel concept, relatedness, focuses more specifically on the need to feel genuinely connected to and cared for by others — teachers, peers, the classroom community. Both dimensions belong in the classroom: students who feel neither interpersonally connected nor meaningfully purposeful are motivationally at risk. “Because it will be on the test” is not a purpose. Connecting learning to students’ own lives, to genuine questions they hold, and to problems they care about solving transforms purpose from an abstraction into fuel.

One of SDT’s most practically important findings is the overjustification effect: introducing a tangible, expected reward for an activity that participants already found interesting reliably reduced their intrinsic interest after the reward was withdrawn. The reward changed the psychological meaning of the activity. This is why SDT distinguishes carefully between controlling rewards (which signal external pressure and undermine autonomy) and informational feedback (which supports competence without damaging autonomy). The goal is not to eliminate all external structure, but to deliver it in ways that support rather than circumvent the student’s own motivation.

The candle problem

In one of the most cited studies in motivation research, participants were given a candle, a box of thumbtacks, and a book of matches, and asked to affix the candle to a wall so it would not drip. Half were offered a cash reward for fast completion; half were not. The reward group consistently took longer — because the reward narrowed their thinking, and the solution (using the tack box as a platform) required thinking outside the obvious frame. Pink’s core insight: rewards that work for simple tasks can actively impair performance on complex ones that require genuine creativity and judgment. Most of what we want students to learn requires the candle-problem kind of thinking.

Retrieval practiceA student is working on a challenging creative writing project. Describe one teacher action that would support each of Pink’s three elements — and one action that would undermine each.►

Autonomy: support — Offer a genuine choice in the project format, audience, or topic within a clear framework. Even small real choices shift the motivational experience. Undermine — Specify every structural element and check in so often that the student feels they can’t be trusted to work alone. (A model or template isn’t the problem by itself; Chapter 16, Concept 6, shows that structure helps when it guides rather than controls.)

Mastery: support — Provide specific, actionable feedback on a draft that tells the student exactly what is working and what one thing to develop — feedback that advances their craft. Undermine — Return the draft with only a grade and a single evaluative sentence; or assign a topic so far beyond the student’s current skill that they cannot succeed with reasonable effort.

Purpose: support — Ask the student why this piece matters to them — what they want to communicate or explore — and connect the craft choices (structure, voice, detail) to that goal. Undermine — Frame the project entirely as a grade exercise: “This is 20% of your quarter grade and will be graded on the rubric.” Purpose requires a reason to care that goes beyond evaluation.

Major concept 5 · Practical Framework

What Teachers Can Actually Do

A recurring theme across everything in this chapter is a note of caution: you cannot directly install intrinsic motivation in another person. What you can do is avoid destroying it — and then, with care, create conditions where it is more likely to grow. That framing suggests a natural sequence: first, do no harm; then, build actively.

“First, do no harm” — the overjustification trap

One of the most reliable findings in motivation research is that extrinsic rewards applied to intrinsically interesting activities tend to undermine that interest once the rewards are removed. Before asking “how do I motivate my students?” the more important question is often “am I currently demotivating them?” Sticker charts, grade curves, public score displays, surveillance-style grading systems, and reward structures that signal “this is the kind of thing you have to be bribed to do” can erode motivation that was already present. Remove the damage first.

Beyond avoiding harm, the research points to several categories of practice that genuinely support intrinsic motivation. The table below is a map of the underlying psychological territory. Understanding the why behind each practice helps teachers adapt it to their specific context rather than applying it mechanically.

Explaining the why
Provide a genuine rationale before assigning required workSDT research shows that even fully mandatory tasks can be experienced as autonomous when the student genuinely understands and endorses the reason for them. “Because I said so” produces compliance at best. “Here is why this matters — to you, to the community, to the questions you’ve been asking” invites internalization. The rationale has to be real; students detect manipulation quickly.

Rules understood as being in service of values the student shares — fairness, learning, safety, community — can be experienced as autonomy-compatible even when they are not negotiable. “This is the rule” and “here is why this rule serves everyone” are not the same motivationally.
Building relationships and belonging
Learn students’ names, interests, and strengths early — and use themRelatedness is not built through grand gestures; it is built through accumulated evidence that the teacher sees the student as an individual. Students who feel seen by their teacher are significantly more likely to remain motivationally engaged when the work gets hard.
Create structures for genuine peer connection, not just cooperationGroup work that is only instrumentally collaborative — divide the task, combine the parts — does not necessarily build relatedness. Structures where students must share thinking, respond to each other, and develop shared understanding build the belonging that SDT and Maslow both identify as foundational to higher-level motivation.
Shifting from external control to internal values
Appeal to students’ identity and values, not just their complianceThe deepest form of motivation internalization occurs when a behavior is experienced not as required but as an expression of who you are and what you value. Teachers who help students see themselves as curious learners, as people who care about doing good work, as members of a learning community, are cultivating motivation at its most durable level. This is slower work than a reward system, and its effects are much harder to see in the short term.
Progressively hand responsibility back to students as internal motivation developsTight external structure — detailed rubrics, frequent reminders, scaffolded steps — is often genuinely necessary early. The issue is inertia: external structure that was helpful in week two can become a ceiling in week twelve. Watching for signs that students are developing their own reasons to engage, and then loosening the scaffolding in response, is one of the most important and least discussed skills in teaching.
A caveat about limits

This framework assumes a classroom in which students’ basic needs — physiological, safety, belonging — are at least partially met. Motivation science does not override poverty, trauma, or chronic instability. Where those conditions exist, the most important motivational intervention may not be in the curriculum at all. But recognizing that limitation is different from accepting it as the final word — as the misconception box in Concept 3 argues, the teacher relationship itself is one of the most powerful buffers research has identified. The hierarchy clarifies what to invest in. It is not a ceiling on what teachers can accomplish.

Retrieval practiceA teacher uses a weekly classroom leaderboard showing which students have the most “participation points.” Analyze this practice using the “first, do no harm” principle — and propose one specific alternative that would address the same behavior without the motivational risk.►

The risks: The leaderboard is a controlling reward structure that threatens multiple SDT needs simultaneously. For students near the top: it may sustain engagement through external validation, but at the cost of shifting motivation from “I participate because I have something to contribute” to “I participate to stay on the leaderboard.” For students in the middle or bottom: chronic public ranking signals relative competence in a way that is likely to produce either withdrawal (learned helplessness) or performance-focused rather than learning-focused engagement. The leaderboard does the most motivational damage to the students who most need support — those who aren’t naturally participating aren’t choosing to participate more; they are probably shrinking further.

Possible alternative: Track participation qualitatively rather than competitively. A private check-in system where each student gets brief feedback about their own engagement pattern — “you asked two really generative questions this week” — provides informational feedback that supports competence without the ranking. This supports the same goal (increased participation) without the competitive structure that makes participation feel like a performance for the teacher rather than a genuine contribution to the group.

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

Intrinsic motivation cannot be installed — but it can be protected and cultivated. These are the practices that make the difference between a classroom that builds internal drive and one that quietly erodes it.

  • Before asking “how do I motivate my students?” ask “am I currently demotivating them?” Audit your reward structures for the overjustification effect. Sticker charts for reading, public leaderboards, and grade curves all carry motivational risk for activities you want students to genuinely care about.
  • Explain the rationale for required work before assigning it — not “because I said so” or “it will be on the test,” but because the student genuinely needs to understand why it matters to them or to the questions they hold. Internalization requires understanding, not just compliance.
  • Use informational feedback rather than controlling rewards. “Here is how your thinking is developing and what one thing would make it stronger” supports competence. “Good job — 4/5 stars” signals surveillance and approval. The valence matters less than what the message communicates about the student’s relationship to their own growth.
  • Offer genuine choices over task, time, technique, or team where possible. Even small real choices shift the motivational experience. Theatrical autonomy over inconsequential details does not.
  • When students seem unmotivated, check Maslow before blaming attitude — and remember the misconception: unmet needs at home do not make you powerless. They make the relational investment more important, not less. A single teacher who sees and believes in a student is one of the most protective factors research has identified.
  • Plan to scaffold down. External structure that was helpful in week two can become a ceiling by week twelve. Watching for signs that students are developing internal reasons to engage — and then loosening the scaffolding in response — is one of the most important timing judgments in teaching.
Chapter synthesis
The fire cannot be installed from the outside. What teachers can do — what the research consistently points toward — is stop extinguishing the fire that students often bring with them, and then create the conditions in which it is most likely to catch. The chapter moves from baseline (drive reduction and its limits) through Maslow’s hierarchy of needs to the most sophisticated account of intrinsic motivation: Self-Determination Theory and Pink’s applied synthesis. At every level, the same conclusion emerges — the teacher relationship is not peripheral to motivation but foundational to it.
Intrinsic motivation is not enjoyment — it is psychological ownership. It cannot be directly installed; conditions can only be created for it to emerge. The motivation continuum runs from pure external regulation to full internalization, with identified regulation as a realistic and meaningful classroom target.
Drive reduction theory explains survival behavior but fails on curiosity, creativity, play, and meaning-seeking. Its failure points clarify what a fuller account of motivation must include: the active pursuit of engagement and growth, not just the relief of discomfort.
Maslow’s hierarchy is better read as interacting needs than a strict ladder. His final revision added a sixth level — self-transcendence — suggesting the highest human motivation is not personal fulfillment but contribution. The teacher relationship buffers against unmet lower needs more powerfully than most people assume.
SDT’s three needs — autonomy, competence, relatedness — and Pink’s autonomy-mastery-purpose framework both predict that frustrating these needs undermines motivation regardless of rewards, and that controlling rewards can damage intrinsic motivation that was already present.
The practical sequence: first, stop demotivating; then actively support autonomy through rationale and choice, competence through informational feedback and optimal challenge, and relatedness through genuine connection and collaborative purpose. The scaffolding should be planned to come down.
