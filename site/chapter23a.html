In the spring of 1917, the United States entered World War I with a problem. It was about to draft more than a million young men, and it had no quick way to decide who should do what.

A group of psychologists offered an answer: a test. Recruits sat in rows with pencils and answered questions against the clock. They unscrambled sentences, finished number patterns, and picked the right word to complete an analogy. Men who couldn’t read English took a picture version instead. By the end of the war, about 1.75 million men had been tested. Their scores were used to help decide who went to officer training and who went to dig trenches.

A farm boy who had left school in fourth grade sat in the same room as a college graduate from Boston. They took the same test, and each walked out with a number.

What did that number actually measure? And what should anyone have done with it?

The big picture

Few words carry more weight in a school than smart. Teachers use it to decide who gets challenged, who gets help, and who gets tested for special services. Students use it to decide what they’re capable of.

This chapter asks whether smart is a number. The research gives an answer that pleases almost no one. Intelligence is real. Tests measure something that predicts school success, work, and even health, at both ends of the scale. But a score also records the life a mind has lived: the schooling, the language, and the food that shaped it. Scores have risen and fallen across whole countries within a single lifetime. So a score is a starting point. A teacher can use it without treating it as a ceiling, and the thing a teacher builds most directly, knowledge and skill, is part of what the score measures.

Major concept 1 · What is intelligence, and why is it hard to define?

Intelligence

Ask ten psychologists to define intelligence and you’ll get ten answers. Ask ten teachers and you’ll get more. That doesn’t mean the word is empty. It means intelligence is hard to pin down, and people care a great deal about how it’s pinned down.

In 1923, psychologist Edwin Boring offered a famously narrow definition.

Key aphorism

Measurable intelligence is simply what the tests of intelligence test.

Edwin G. Boring, “Intelligence as the Tests Test It” (1923), p. 35 (excerpt). This is an operational definition: it defines the measurable version of a concept through the tasks used to assess it. Evidence is needed to establish what broader ability those tasks measure.

Learn more

Boring’s sentence goes on: “until further scientific observation allows us to extend the definition.” He was offering a narrow starting point for measurement. He then turned to correlations as a way to learn how test performance relates to other abilities. Naming a test doesn’t prove it measures something real. The concepts that follow look at the evidence for a common factor, what scores predict, and the limits of reading one student’s score.

Boring wasn’t saying tests capture everything about the mind. He was saying where science has to start: with something it can measure. The harder question is whether those measurements connect to anything outside the testing room.

They do. Performance on mental tasks isn’t random. People who do well on one kind of mental task tend to do well on others. This pattern has held up across countries, cultures, and testing methods for more than a century. Something produces that consistency. What to call it, how to measure it, and what causes it are where the debates begin.

For teachers, those debates matter. What you believe about intelligence shapes what you expect from students, how you design lessons, and who you think can learn what. A teacher who sees intelligence as a fixed number will teach differently from one who sees it as real but shaped by experience. The rest of this chapter looks at which view the evidence supports.

A note on history

Intelligence testing has a troubled past. In the early 1900s, test scores were used to support forced sterilization laws and to argue that some ethnic groups were born less capable. Some testers were openly prejudiced. Others did careful work. Historians still debate how much the tests themselves shaped laws like the Immigration Act of 1924 (Snyderman & Herrnstein, 1983). The misuse of scores to rank whole groups of people is well documented. And these aren’t only old abuses. Their effects still show up in tracking systems, in who gets identified for gifted programs, and in the overrepresentation of some groups of students in special education. [Citation needed]

That history doesn’t make the science false. It means the science has never been neutral in how people use it. A well-prepared teacher takes the evidence seriously and stays alert to how it can be misused.

Retrieval practiceTwo teachers learn that a new student has an IQ score of 85. Teacher A says, “I’ll adjust my expectations.” Teacher B says, “I need to find out what this student knows and can do.” Which response fits the evidence better, and what mistake is Teacher A making?►

Teacher B. An 85 is one standard deviation below average, around the 16th percentile. It tells you something about how this student did on a set of mental tasks, compared with other people. It doesn’t tell you what the student knows about your subject, what they’ve been taught, or what they can do with strong instruction.

Teacher A is treating the score as a ceiling, a fixed limit on what the student can learn. The rest of this chapter shows why that reading goes beyond the evidence. The score is one data point. Teacher B is collecting the rest.

Major concept 2 · The case for general intelligence

General Intelligence (g)

In 1904, British psychologist Charles Spearman noticed something about schoolchildren’s test scores. Children who did well in one subject tended to do well in others, even subjects that seemed to have little in common. Later researchers found the same thing with every kind of mental test they tried. Vocabulary, puzzles, memory tasks, and math problems all rise and fall together. This pattern is called the positive manifold: the pattern in which scores on all kinds of mental tests go up and down together.

Spearman argued that one broad ability sits underneath this pattern. He called it general intelligence (g): a broad mental ability that affects how well people do on almost every kind of thinking task. He also noted that people have narrower strengths, like being especially good with words or with spatial puzzles. Two people with similar g can have very different patterns of strengths.

The positive manifold is one of the most replicated findings in psychology. It is also easy to misread. It shows that mental abilities are linked. It doesn’t show that they’re fixed, and it doesn’t say where they come from.

The most popular alternative

In 1983, psychologist Howard Gardner offered a very different picture. In Frames of Mind, he proposed that people have several separate intelligences instead of one general one. His original list had seven: linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, and intrapersonal. He added naturalist in 1999 and has called existential intelligence a possible “half” (Gardner, 1999).

The theory spread quickly through schools, and the instinct behind it is a good one. Students really do have different strengths. Schools often reward a narrow slice of human ability. Looking for talent where tests don’t look is good practice.

But the theory makes a testable claim: the intelligences are separate. If they were, a student’s musical ability would tell you nothing about their verbal or spatial ability. That isn’t what the data show. Gardner’s abilities correlate, just as the positive manifold predicts (Visser et al., 2006). Several of his categories also look more like talents or personality traits than intelligence as psychologists use the word. And the theory was built mainly from case studies and argument, not experiments that could prove it wrong.

This is where a scholar-practitioner sorts the claim from the value. Gardner’s message, to look for ability in many places and teach in varied ways, holds up. His specific map of separate intelligences doesn’t. Varied instruction helps all students because it gives everyone more ways into an idea, not because it matches each student to a type.

Retrieval practiceYour principal announces that every lesson plan must now list which of Gardner’s intelligences it targets. A colleague says, “What’s the harm? It’s about seeing every kid.” What would you say to the principal, respectfully and clearly?►

Start with what’s right: the goal of seeing every student and teaching in varied ways is a good one. Then name the problem. Gardner’s intelligences aren’t separate. Students who do well on one kind of task tend to do well on others. This is the positive manifold, one of the most replicated findings in psychology. Planning around the categories puts teacher time into a map that doesn’t match how minds work, and it can lead teachers to sort students into types.

Then offer a better version. Ask that lesson plans show varied ways into the content, like a diagram, a discussion, and a hands-on task, chosen because they fit the material. That keeps the principal’s goal and drops the part the evidence doesn’t support.

Major concept 3 · Don’t mistake what they know for what they can do

Fluid and Crystallized Intelligence

Psychologists Raymond Cattell and John Horn split general ability into two broad parts. In most current models, both sit underneath g. They are different enough that confusing them causes real mistakes in classrooms.

Fluid intelligence (Gf)Crystallized intelligence (Gc)
What it isReasoning and solving new problemsKnowledge and skills built through experience
Depends on prior knowledge?Not muchYes, that’s what it is
Over a lifetimePeaks in early adulthood, then slowly declinesKeeps growing
Classroom exampleSpotting the pattern in a brand-new kind of puzzleKnowing what photosynthesis means
What shapes itHealth, sleep, stress, early environmentSchooling, books, conversation, opportunity

Fluid intelligence (Gf) is the ability to reason and solve problems you’ve never seen before, without relying on what you already know. Crystallized intelligence (Gc) is the knowledge and skills a person has built up through experience and schooling. Most real tasks use both. Reading a history passage takes knowledge of the words and events, and it takes reasoning to connect them.

Many intelligence tests lean heavily on crystallized knowledge, through vocabulary, general information, and word problems. That matters, because crystallized knowledge depends on opportunity. A student who grew up with books, travel, and long dinner-table conversations has had more chances to build it.

Mistakes in both directions

This creates two opposite errors.

The first is mistaking a knowledge gap for a reasoning limit. A student who is new to the country, or who missed school, or who grew up with few books may score low on knowledge-heavy tasks while reasoning well. A knowledge gap calls for teaching. Treating it as a fixed limit means the student may never get the teaching that would close it.

The second error runs the other way. A student who seems gifted may mostly have a rich store of knowledge from home. And a student with strong reasoning but thin knowledge can be missed entirely, because nobody thinks to look.

A large Florida school district showed how often the second kind of miss happens. For years, students were tested for gifted programs only if a teacher or parent referred them. In 2005, the district began screening every second grader instead. The standards for qualifying didn’t change. The number of students identified as gifted rose from about 3% to about 5.5%. Black and Hispanic students, students from low-income families, and English learners were the most likely to have been missed before (Card & Giuliano, 2016).

That one study makes two points at once. The ability was real: these students met the same bar as everyone else. And the old referral system wasn’t seeing them. This is confirmation bias at the scale of a school system. Adults referred the students who already looked gifted to them, and the students who didn’t fit that picture were never tested. The fix was universal screening: testing every student, instead of only those referred by teachers or parents, so that able students aren’t missed.

Retrieval practiceA sixth grader who moved from Guatemala last year scores low on a cognitive test that includes a lot of vocabulary. In math class, she solves pattern problems faster than anyone. A colleague says, “The test shows she’s below average, so she’s in the right group.” Using fluid and crystallized intelligence, explain what the colleague may be missing. What would you do next?►

The test leans on crystallized knowledge, like English vocabulary and general information that depends on schooling and experience. A low score there may mostly reflect a year of English, not her reasoning. Her speed with pattern problems suggests strong fluid reasoning. The colleague is treating a knowledge gap as a capacity limit.

Next steps: look at measures that depend less on English, such as nonverbal reasoning tasks. Build her academic English and background knowledge directly. And ask whether your school screens every student for advanced programs or relies on referrals, since referrals are where students like her tend to get missed.

Major concept 4 · What IQ can measure, and what it cannot

IQ

An IQ is a score from a standardized intelligence test, set so that the average is 100 and most people score between 85 and 115. Tests like the Wechsler scales combine several kinds of tasks: vocabulary, reasoning puzzles, working memory, and processing speed. The scores are set so that they fall on a normal distribution, with a standard deviation of 15 points.

What IQ predicts

IQ predicts real outcomes, and it’s one of the most studied predictors in psychology.

  • School grades: a strong link. A review of 240 samples found a corrected correlation of about .54 (Roth et al., 2015).
  • Years of education: a correlation of about .55 (Neisser et al., 1996).
  • Standardized tests like the SAT: a correlation of about .80 or higher with general intelligence (Frey & Detterman, 2004).
  • Work: a moderate link with job performance. A 2022 reanalysis found the classic estimate had been too high, and placed it around .31 (Sackett et al., 2022).
  • Health: in a review of 16 long-term studies, children who scored one standard deviation higher had about a 24% lower risk of dying over the following decades (Calvin et al., 2011).

To see how strong these numbers are, it helps to know that in most psychology research, a correlation of .20 is typical and .30 is large (Gignac & Szodorai, 2016). Correlations of .50 and above are unusual.

These links also hold at the top of the range. The Study of Mathematically Precocious Youth has followed thousands of students who scored in the top 1% before age 13. Even within that group, the students with the highest scores were more likely to earn doctorates, publish, or patent inventions as adults (Lubinski & Benbow, 2006). For a gifted student, more challenge doesn’t stop mattering at some ceiling.

What it doesn’t decide

A correlation describes a pattern across many people. It doesn’t decide what happens to one person. At any IQ score, the range of outcomes is wide. Effort, teaching, opportunity, and support all shape where a person lands, and the score doesn’t measure them.

Key aphorism

People who boast about their I.Q. are losers.

Stephen Hawking, interview with Deborah Solomon, The New York Times Magazine, December 12, 2004. A score can inform a teacher’s decisions without becoming a badge of personal superiority.

Learn more

Asked about his own IQ, Hawking said he didn’t know it, and then he made this joke. The humor targets turning a test score into a status symbol. This chapter’s account of what intelligence tests measure and predict rests on research. Reading a student’s score also requires attention to knowledge, effort, opportunity, and teaching. Two mistakes are tempting here. One is to dismiss IQ as meaningless, which the evidence doesn’t support. The other is to treat it as destiny, which the evidence doesn’t support either. The position the evidence supports holds both: IQ predicts real outcomes, and the score itself is shaped by conditions that schools can influence.

That position fits something the gospel teaches plainly: “Remember the worth of souls is great in the sight of God” (Doctrine and Covenants 18:10). A score describes performance on a set of tasks. It says nothing about a student’s worth.

How schools use IQ scores

What teachers should know about IQ scores

If you teach students who have been formally evaluated, you’ll see IQ scores in eligibility reports and IEP paperwork.

  • 100 is exactly average. 85 is one standard deviation below, about the 16th percentile. 115 is one standard deviation above, about the 84th percentile.
  • Intellectual disability requires significant limits in both intellectual functioning and adaptive behavior, the everyday social and practical skills of daily life, beginning before age 22. An IQ score around 70, or as high as 75, is one part of that picture. A low score alone is not enough (American Association on Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities [AAIDD], n.d.).
  • Gifted identification often uses a cutoff around 130, usually along with other evidence. Because referrals miss many able students, more schools now screen everyone.
  • What the score doesn’t tell you: how hard a student works, what they know about your subject, what they’ve been taught, or what they can do with strong instruction.

Use the number as one piece of a larger picture, never as the picture itself. Every child deserves dignity, high expectations, and high-quality teaching, regardless of their IQ score.

Retrieval practiceA ninth grader’s IQ score is 125. What does the score predict, and how confident should you be? What does it tell you almost nothing about?►

What it predicts: above-average grades and a better-than-average chance of finishing college. These are statements about groups of people with similar scores, not promises about this student.

How confident: moderately. A correlation around .54 with grades is strong for psychology, but it leaves plenty of room for students to do much better or much worse than predicted. The SMPY findings also suggest that a high-scoring student keeps benefiting from more challenge, so the score is a reason to stretch this student, not to relax.

What it tells you almost nothing about: how much this student knows about your subject, whether they’ve learned the prerequisites, how hard they work, how they respond to feedback, and what they could become with excellent teaching.

Major concept 5 · Intelligence responds to environment

Heritability and the Flynn Effect

If IQ predicts so much, the next question is where it comes from. Two kinds of evidence seem at first to point in opposite directions.

Genes matter

Twin and adoption studies find that genes play a large role in why people differ in IQ. Heritability is how much of the variation in a trait, within a particular group and setting, is linked to genetic differences. For IQ, heritability rises with age. In a study of 11,000 pairs of twins, it was 41% at age 9, 55% at age 12, and 66% at age 17 (Haworth et al., 2010).

Heritability is one of the most misunderstood ideas in psychology, and it trips up classroom decisions in three ways.

  • It describes groups, not individuals. It doesn’t tell you how much of one student’s score came from genes.
  • It doesn’t mean fixed. Height is highly heritable, and average height still rose a lot during the 20th century as nutrition improved. A trait can be heritable and still change when conditions change.
  • It doesn’t explain differences between groups. Heritability within a group tells you nothing about why two groups differ on average.

A useful way to hold this together is the reaction range: the idea that genes set a range of possible outcomes and environment decides where in that range a person ends up. Two children with similar genetic potential can end up with very different scores depending on their food, their stress, the language around them, and their schooling. For teachers, the environment is the part you can do something about.

Environment matters too

In the 1980s, researcher James Flynn noticed something strange. IQ test makers kept having to make their tests harder, because each generation scored higher than the one before. Across much of the world, average scores rose about 3 points per decade during the 20th century (Pietschnig & Voracek, 2015). This is the Flynn effect: the rise in average IQ scores across much of the world during the 20th century. The gains came too fast to be genetic, which shows that environment changes measured intelligence.

The gains were huge. Scored against today’s norms, the average person from around 1900 would land near 70. Flynn didn’t believe our great-grandparents were mostly intellectually disabled. He concluded that modern life had trained new habits of mind, like abstract reasoning and sorting things into categories, that the tests happen to measure (Flynn, 2007). Better nutrition, more schooling, smaller families, and less disease probably all contributed.

So genes matter, and environment matters. Both findings are true at once. Heritability describes why people differ within a setting. The Flynn effect shows what happens when the setting itself changes.

For the first time, scores are falling

Then the trend did something it had never done in the first century of testing. In the 1990s, average scores began to fall. Declines have now been documented in at least seven countries, including Norway, Denmark, Finland, the United Kingdom, and France (Dutton et al., 2016).

In the United States, a study of about 400,000 adults who took the same online test between 2006 and 2018 found lower scores in verbal reasoning, matrix reasoning, and number series. Spatial reasoning went up (Dworak et al., 2023). So the picture is a shift, not a simple collapse, and the test came from volunteers, not a random sample.

The strongest evidence comes from Norway, where nearly every young man took the same test when he entered military service. Researchers compared brothers born years apart. The decline showed up within families: younger brothers scored lower than their older brothers once the trend turned (Bratsberg & Rogeberg, 2018). That rules out the idea that the decline came from changes in which kinds of families were having children. Like the rise, the fall is environmental.

What changed is not settled. Researchers have suggested changes in schooling, in reading habits, in screen use, and in how hard people try on tests. None of these has been proven. What we can say is this: if the gains came from the environment and the losses come from the environment, then neither is locked in.

Group differences

Average scores differ between some racial and ethnic groups in the United States. The gaps are real in the sense that they show up consistently on tests. Where they come from has been debated for decades, sometimes bitterly.

Here is what the evidence supports. A 2012 review by leading intelligence researchers concluded that group differences in IQ “are best understood as environmental in origin” (Nisbett et al., 2012). No genetic evidence has explained the gaps, and some of them narrowed in the late 20th century. Not every researcher accepts that conclusion. But heritability within groups can’t settle the question, for the reason above, and the Flynn effect shows that environments can move scores by amounts this large.

For a teacher, one point holds regardless: a group average tells you nothing about the student in front of you.

What this means in your classroom

Group data matter for teachers because they say something about the systems students have moved through, not about any one student. A student who has grown up in a high-poverty area, attended under-resourced schools, and lived with chronic stress hasn’t had the same chances to build the skills tests measure as a student who hasn’t faced those things. That gap is real. It reflects circumstance, not capacity. A teacher’s job is not to lower expectations based on group membership. It’s to recognize that measured gaps in knowledge and skill reflect a student’s learning history, and to teach accordingly.

Retrieval practiceNorwegian researchers found that the drop in IQ scores showed up even when comparing brothers in the same family. Why is a within-family comparison so useful here, and what explanation does it rule out?►

Brothers grow up in the same home, with the same parents, the same income, and the same neighborhood. If younger brothers score lower than older brothers once the trend turns, the decline can’t come from a change in which families are having children, an idea some researchers had proposed. It has to come from something that changed in the world between the older brother’s childhood and the younger brother’s.

What the study doesn’t tell us is what changed. Schooling, reading, screens, and test motivation are all candidates, and none has been proven. Notice the difference between the finding (the cause is environmental) and the guesses about which part of the environment.

Major concept 6 · What raises IQ

Schooling and Intelligence

If environment can raise and lower scores across whole countries, the practical question is which parts of the environment matter. Some are outside a teacher’s control. One of the most important isn’t.

Schooling raises intelligence

The schooling finding deserves a closer look, because it’s the one teachers control most directly. It comes from studies that get close to experiments. When countries raised the school-leaving age, the students who had to stay longer scored higher later. When birthday cutoffs gave some children an extra year of school by the luck of the calendar, those children scored higher too. Across 42 data sets and more than 600,000 people, the authors called education “the most consistent, robust, and durable method yet to be identified for raising intelligence” (Ritchie & Tucker-Drob, 2018).

Why would school raise IQ? Because school asks students to do, all day, the kinds of thinking IQ tests measure. Students reason about abstract ideas, hold information in mind while working with it, and solve problems they haven’t seen before. School also builds vocabulary, background knowledge, and habits of careful thinking. That last part is crystallized intelligence.

That means the quality of each school day matters for more than test scores. A day of weak instruction is, in a small but real way, a lost chance for students’ minds to grow. A day of demanding, well-taught work is part of how they grow.

What lasts is knowledge and skill

The preschool studies add an important twist. Early programs raised IQ scores, and the boost faded. What lasted were the things those programs built along the way: skills, habits, and knowledge that kept paying off.

That points to the most useful way for a teacher to think about intelligence. You won’t usually see a student’s IQ rise in your class, and that isn’t the goal. The goal is the knowledge and skill that school is built to grow. Those are a large part of what intelligence tests measure, they keep growing across life, and they’re the part of a mind that teaching changes most directly.

This applies to every student in the room, including those far ahead. A student who already knows most of what the class is learning isn’t gaining knowledge while she waits. The next chapter’s question, how to match teaching to where each student actually is, starts here.

A gospel lens: intelligence that rises with us

The scriptures use the word intelligence in a larger sense than psychologists do. “The glory of God is intelligence, or, in other words, light and truth” (Doctrine and Covenants 93:36). That is not a statement about IQ scores, and it would be a mistake to read it as one.

Another passage speaks to how that intelligence is gained: “Whatever principle of intelligence we attain unto in this life, it will rise with us in the resurrection. And if a person gains more knowledge and intelligence in this life through his diligence and obedience than another, he will have so much the advantage in the world to come” (Doctrine and Covenants 130:18–19).

These verses don’t explain twin studies or the Flynn effect, and the research doesn’t explain them. But they share a premise with this concept. Knowledge and intelligence, in the scriptural sense, are gained over time through effort. For a teacher, that means no student’s starting point is the end of the story. [DOCTRINE CHECK? Retrieval practiceAt a staff meeting, a veteran teacher says, “I can’t change how smart my students are. I can only help them make the most of what they’ve got.” Several new teachers nod. Using this chapter’s evidence, what’s right about that statement, and what would you add?►

What’s right: You probably won’t see dramatic IQ changes in one year, and early IQ boosts tend to fade. Focusing on teaching well, instead of waiting for “smarter” students, is the right instinct.

What I’d add:

  • “What they’ve got” isn’t fixed. Each extra year of school raises IQ scores by about 1 to 5 points, so the schooling we provide is part of what shapes students’ measured ability.
  • The knowledge and skill we teach are a large part of intelligence. Building vocabulary, background knowledge, and fluent skills grows the crystallized side of every student’s mind, and it keeps paying off long after IQ gains fade.
  • This applies to students who are ahead too. If our strongest students spend the year reviewing what they already know, we’re not making the most of what they’ve got.

As a leader, I’d frame it as encouragement, not correction: “We do more than you’re giving us credit for.”

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice
  • Treat a score as a starting point, not a ceiling. Find out what the student knows and can do, and teach from there.
  • Tell knowledge gaps apart from reasoning limits, in both directions. A student who lacks background knowledge needs teaching. A student who has a lot of it may not be as far ahead in reasoning as they look.
  • Ask how your school finds advanced students. Referral systems miss able students, especially those from low-income families, students of color, and English learners. Universal screening finds more of them.
  • Teach as if Gardner’s instinct is right, without his categories. Use varied ways into the content because they fit the material, not to match students to types.
  • Build knowledge on purpose. Vocabulary, reading across subjects, and rich discussion grow the part of intelligence that teaching changes most directly.
  • Stretch the students who are ahead. High-scoring students keep benefiting from more challenge. Waiting is not learning.
  • Make your classroom predictably safe. Chronic stress interferes with working memory and attention. A warm, stable, low-threat classroom is a condition for the thinking you’re asking students to do.
  • Remember what group gaps reflect. Measured gaps between groups reflect students’ circumstances and schooling, not a limit on what any student can learn. The same factors behind the Flynn effect, like richer language, better nutrition, and stronger schooling, are the ones that close gaps. You are one of those factors.
Chapter synthesis
Is smart a number? Partly. Tests measure something real. Scores on all kinds of mental tasks rise and fall together, and IQ predicts grades, work, and health, even among the most able students. But a score also records a history. Heritability is real and doesn’t mean fixed. Whole countries gained about 30 points in a century, and scores are now falling for the first time, because environments changed. The most powerful environment a teacher controls is school, and what school builds most directly is knowledge and skill.
General intelligence is real. The positive manifold is one of the most replicated findings in psychology, and it’s the main reason Gardner’s separate intelligences don’t hold up.
Knowing isn’t the same as reasoning. Confusing crystallized knowledge with fluid reasoning causes mistakes in both directions: missed potential and mistaken giftedness.
IQ predicts, but it doesn’t decide. The links to grades, work, and health are real at both ends of the scale. Outcomes for any one student vary widely.
Heritable doesn’t mean fixed. The Flynn effect, and its recent reversal within families, show that environment moves measured intelligence.
School builds minds. Schooling raises IQ, and what lasts is the knowledge and skill teaching builds.
