In the spring of 1917, the United States entered World War I with a problem. It was about to draft more than a million young men, and it had no quick way to decide who should do what.

A group of psychologists offered an answer: a test. Recruits sat in rows with pencils and answered questions against the clock. They unscrambled sentences, finished number patterns, and picked the right word to complete an analogy. Men who couldn’t read English took a picture version instead. By the end of the war, about 1.75 million men had been tested. Their scores helped decide who went to officer training and who went to dig trenches.

Picture two of them. A farm boy who left school after fourth grade sits in the same room as a college graduate from Boston. They take the same test, and each walks out with a number.

What did that number actually measure? And what should anyone have done with it?

The big picture

Few words carry more weight in a school than smart. Teachers use it to decide who gets challenged, who gets help, and who gets tested for special services. Students use it to decide what they’re capable of.

IQ has a shaky reputation, and some of that reputation was earned. But by the standards of psychology, it is one of the sturdiest measures we have. Few things psychologists measure predict as much: grades, years of school, job performance, and even how long people live. A score also records the life a mind has lived, including its schooling, its language, and the food that fed it. Scores have risen and fallen across whole countries within a single lifetime.

This chapter answers the two questions from the 1917 story in order. The first four concepts ask what an intelligence score measures. The last asks what schools should do with one.

Major concept 1 · The case for g

Intelligence

Ask ten psychologists to define intelligence and you’ll get ten answers. Ask ten teachers and you’ll get more. In 1923, Harvard psychologist Edwin Boring cut the argument short with a deliberately narrow definition. Put plainly, it comes to this:

Key aphorism

Intelligence is whatever intelligence tests are testing.

Paraphrased from Edwin G. Boring, “Intelligence as the Tests Test It” (1923), p. 35

Learn more

The line above is our plain-language paraphrase. Boring’s own sentence reads: “Measurable intelligence is simply what the tests of intelligence test, until further scientific observation allows us to extend the definition.” He was offering a starting point for measurement, not claiming that tests capture the whole mind. The rest of his essay turned to correlations: how scores on different tests relate to one another. That question is where this concept goes next.

Boring’s definition can sound like a dodge, but it describes how the science actually started. The field that measures mental abilities is called psychometrics. Psychometricians don’t begin by deciding what intelligence really is. They build tests, give them to many people, and look at what the scores do. If the scores were random, Boring’s definition would be the end of the story. They aren’t.

The positive manifold

In 1904, British psychologist Charles Spearman noticed something about schoolchildren’s grades and test scores. Children who did well in one subject tended to do well in others, even subjects that seemed to have little in common. To study the pattern, he developed an early form of factor analysis (Spearman, 1904). Later researchers found the same thing with every kind of mental test they tried. Vocabulary, puzzles, memory tasks, and math problems all rise and fall together. This pattern is called the positive manifold: the pattern in which scores on all kinds of mental tests go up and down together.

Spearman argued that one broad ability sits underneath this pattern. He called it general intelligence (g): a broad mental ability that affects how well people do on almost every kind of thinking task. He also saw that g doesn’t explain everything. Each test draws on some narrower skill of its own, like working with words or rotating shapes in your head. He called these specific abilities (s). Two people with similar g can still have very different strengths.

The positive manifold is one of the most replicated findings in psychology. It is also easy to misread. It shows that mental abilities are linked. It doesn’t show that they’re fixed, and it doesn’t say where they come from.

Fluid and crystallized intelligence

Psychologists Raymond Cattell and John Horn later split general ability into two broad parts. In most current models, both sit underneath g.

Fluid intelligence (Gf) is the ability to reason and solve problems you’ve never seen before, without relying on what you already know. Crystallized intelligence (Gc) is the knowledge and skills a person has built up through experience and schooling. Most real tasks use both. Reading a history passage takes knowledge of the words and events, and it takes reasoning to connect them.

Many intelligence tests lean heavily on crystallized knowledge, through vocabulary, general information, and word problems. The 1917 Army test did too. One item asked whether Crisco was a patent medicine, a disinfectant, a toothpaste, or a food product. Another asked whether Christy Mathewson was famous as a writer, an artist, a baseball player, or a comedian (Gould, 1981). The farm boy and the college graduate both drew on g when they took that test. They also drew on everything they had been taught and everything they had happened to see, and those were not the same.

Why psychologists take g seriously

None of this would matter much if g were only a pattern in test scores. It matters because measures of g predict life outside the testing room better than almost anything else psychologists measure. Concept 2 gives the numbers. That track record is why most researchers see IQ as one of psychology’s strongest ideas, even as they argue about what causes it.

A note on history

Intelligence testing has a troubled past. In the early 1900s, eugenicists used test scores to argue that some people, and some ethnic groups, were born less capable. States passed laws to sterilize people judged “feebleminded,” and the Supreme Court upheld one of them in Buck v. Bell (1927). Some testers were openly prejudiced. Others did careful work. Historians still debate how much the tests themselves shaped laws like the Immigration Act of 1924 (Snyderman & Herrnstein, 1983).

The arguments aren’t only about the past. Black students with high test scores are less likely to be placed in gifted programs than white students with the same scores (Grissom & Redding, 2016). Whether students of color are placed in special education too often or too rarely is still disputed, and the answer depends on which students are compared (Morgan et al., 2015).

That history doesn’t make the science false. It means the science has never been neutral in how people use it.

Retrieval practiceA friend says, “IQ is meaningless. Even the psychologists admit intelligence is just whatever the test measures.” Using Boring’s full sentence and the positive manifold, explain what your friend has right and what they’re missing.►

What they have right: Boring did define measurable intelligence as what the tests test. A test score is a starting point, not a full picture of a mind.

What they’re missing: Boring’s sentence goes on: “until further scientific observation allows us to extend the definition.” That observation came. Scores on very different mental tests rise and fall together, a pattern called the positive manifold. It’s one of the most replicated findings in psychology, and it’s the evidence for a general ability, g. Those scores also predict grades, schooling, work, and health. A meaningless number wouldn’t do that.

Major concept 2 · What IQ Can Measure—and What It Cannot

Intelligence Quotient

An IQ is a score from a standardized intelligence test, set so that the average is 100 and most people score between 85 and 115. Tests like the Wechsler scales combine several kinds of tasks: vocabulary, reasoning puzzles, working memory, and processing speed. The scores are set so that they fall on a normal distribution, with a standard deviation of 15 points.

What IQ predicts

IQ has strong predictive validity: its scores predict real outcomes, and it’s one of the most studied predictors in psychology.

  • School grades: a strong link. A review of 240 samples found a corrected correlation of about .54 (Roth et al., 2015).
  • Years of education: a correlation of about .55 (Neisser et al., 1996).
  • Standardized tests like the SAT: a correlation of about .82 with general intelligence (Frey & Detterman, 2004).
  • Work: a moderate link with job performance. A 2022 reanalysis found the classic estimate had been too high, and placed it around .31 (Sackett et al., 2022).
  • Health: in a review of 16 long-term studies, children who scored one standard deviation higher had about a 24% lower risk of dying over the following decades (Calvin et al., 2011).

To see how strong these numbers are, it helps to know what’s normal. Across hundreds of findings about individual differences, a correlation of .20 is typical and .30 is large. Fewer than 3% reach .50 (Gignac & Szodorai, 2016).

These links also hold at the top of the range. The Study of Mathematically Precocious Youth has followed thousands of students who scored in the top 1% before age 13. Even within that group, the students with the highest scores were more likely to earn doctorates, publish, or patent inventions as adults (Lubinski & Benbow, 2006). Differences in ability keep mattering even near the very top.

What it doesn’t decide

A correlation describes a pattern across many people. It doesn’t decide what happens to one person. At any IQ score, the range of outcomes is wide. Effort, teaching, opportunity, and support all shape where a person lands, and the score doesn’t measure them.

Think back to the two recruits. Across thousands of men like them, the Army’s scores would line up with later schooling and work in the ways the list above describes. For the farm boy himself, the number left almost everything open.

Two mistakes are tempting here. One is to dismiss IQ as meaningless, which the evidence doesn’t support. The other is to treat it as destiny, which the evidence doesn’t support either. The evidence supports holding both ideas at once: IQ predicts real outcomes, and the score itself is shaped by conditions that can change.

People who boast about their I.Q. are losers.

Asked about his own IQ, Hawking said he didn’t know it, and then he made this joke. The humor targets turning a test score into a status symbol. This chapter’s account of what intelligence tests measure and predict rests on research. Reading a student’s score also requires attention to knowledge, effort, opportunity, and teaching.

Retrieval practiceA ninth grader’s IQ score is 125. What does the score predict, and how confident should you be? What does it tell you almost nothing about?►

What it predicts: above-average grades and a better-than-average chance of finishing college. These are statements about groups of people with similar scores, not promises about this student.

How confident: moderately. A correlation around .54 with grades is strong for psychology, but it leaves plenty of room for students to do much better or much worse than predicted. The SMPY findings also suggest that ability differences keep mattering near the top, so a high score is a reason to stretch this student, not to relax.

What it tells you almost nothing about: how much this student knows about your subject, whether they’ve learned the prerequisites, how hard they work, how they respond to feedback, and what they could become with excellent teaching.

Major concept 3 · Intelligence responds to environment

The Flynn Effect

If IQ predicts so much, the next question is where it comes from. Two kinds of evidence seem at first to point in opposite directions.

Genes matter

Twin and adoption studies find that genes play a large role in why people differ in IQ. Heritability is how much of the variation in a trait, within a particular group and setting, is linked to genetic differences. For IQ, heritability rises with age. In a study of 11,000 pairs of twins, it was 41% at age 9, 55% at age 12, and 66% at age 17 (Haworth et al., 2010).

Heritability is one of the most misunderstood ideas in psychology. Three points keep it straight.

  • It describes groups, not individuals. It doesn’t tell you how much of one person’s score came from genes.
  • It doesn’t mean fixed. Height is highly heritable, and average height still rose a lot during the 20th century as nutrition improved. A trait can be heritable and still change when conditions change.
  • It doesn’t explain differences between groups. Heritability within a group tells you nothing about why two groups differ on average.

A useful way to hold this together is the reaction range: the idea that genes set a range of possible outcomes and environment decides where in that range a person ends up. Two children with similar genetic potential can end up with very different scores depending on their food, their stress, the language around them, and their schooling.

Environment matters too

In the 1980s, researcher James Flynn noticed something strange. IQ test makers kept having to make their tests harder, because each generation scored higher than the one before. Across much of the world, average scores rose about 3 points per decade during the 20th century (Pietschnig & Voracek, 2015). This is the Flynn effect: the rise in average IQ scores across much of the world during the 20th century. The gains came too fast to be genetic, which shows that environment changes measured intelligence.

The gains were huge. Scored against today’s norms, the average person from around 1900 would land near 70. That’s the generation the 1917 recruits belonged to. Flynn didn’t believe our great-grandparents were mostly intellectually disabled. He concluded that modern life had trained new habits of mind, like abstract reasoning and sorting things into categories, that the tests happen to measure (Flynn, 2007). Better nutrition, more schooling, smaller families, and less disease probably all contributed.

So genes matter, and environment matters. Both findings are true at once. Heritability describes why people differ within a setting. The Flynn effect shows what happens when the setting itself changes. In the 1990s, the trend did something new: in several countries, average scores began to fall.

Go deeperFor the first time, scores are falling

Nothing like it showed up in the first century of testing. Declines have now been documented in at least seven countries, including Norway, Denmark, Finland, the United Kingdom, and France (Dutton et al., 2016). One of that review’s authors, Richard Lynn, is known for claims about race and IQ that have been widely discredited. This review of falling scores hasn’t been, but his name is a reason to read it with care.

In the United States, a study of about 400,000 adults who took the same online test between 2006 and 2018 found lower scores in verbal reasoning, matrix reasoning, and number series. Spatial reasoning went up (Dworak et al., 2023). So the picture is a shift, not a simple collapse, and the test takers were volunteers, not a random sample.

The strongest evidence comes from Norway, where nearly every young man took the same test when he entered military service. Researchers compared brothers born years apart. The decline showed up within families: once the trend turned, younger brothers scored lower than their older brothers (Bratsberg & Rogeberg, 2018). That rules out the idea that the decline came from changes in which kinds of families were having children. Like the rise, the fall is environmental.

What changed is not settled. Researchers have suggested changes in schooling, in reading habits, in screen use, and in how hard people try on tests. None of these has been proven. If the gains came from the environment and the losses come from the environment, then neither is locked in.

Apply: Why is a comparison between brothers so useful here, and what explanation does it rule out?

Answer: Brothers grow up with the same parents, in the same home, usually in the same neighborhood. If younger brothers score lower than older brothers once the trend turns, the decline can’t come from a change in which families are having children. It has to come from something that changed in the world between the older brother’s childhood and the younger brother’s. The study doesn’t say what changed. Notice the difference between the finding (the cause is environmental) and the guesses about which part of the environment.

Group differences

The distribution of scores differs between groups in the United States. The word distribution matters, because groups can differ in more than their averages.

Sex is the clearest example. Men’s and women’s average scores on general intelligence are about the same. But men’s scores are more spread out, so men are somewhat more common at both the low and high ends of the range (Johnson et al., 2008). Average scores also differ between some racial and ethnic groups. These differences show up consistently on tests. Where they come from has been argued over for more than a century, often bitterly.

Some of the ugliest ideas in modern history grew out of a focus on group differences, and they have names: eugenics, scientific racism, and sexism. Each took a difference in group scores and turned it into a claim about who deserved schooling, citizenship, or children. That history is a reason for care. It isn’t a reason to stop asking. Scientific questions deserve honest answers, and refusing to look at the evidence leaves the question to the people with an agenda.

Here is what the evidence supports. A group of leading intelligence researchers concluded that group differences in IQ “are best understood as environmental in origin” (Nisbett et al., 2012). No genetic evidence has explained the racial and ethnic gaps, and some of them narrowed in the late 20th century. Not every researcher accepts that conclusion. But heritability within groups can’t settle the question, for the reason above, and the Flynn effect shows that environments can move scores by amounts this large. One more point holds no matter where a difference comes from: a group’s distribution says nothing about where any one person falls in it.

Retrieval practiceA classmate says, “IQ is about 66% heritable by age 17, so there’s not much room for anything else to matter.” Using height and the Flynn effect, explain what’s wrong with that reasoning.►

Heritability describes how much of the variation among people in one setting is linked to genetic differences. It doesn’t say how much a trait can change when the setting changes. Height is highly heritable, and average height still rose a lot when nutrition improved. IQ is the same: average scores rose about 30 points in a century, far too fast for genes to explain. A trait can be highly heritable and still respond strongly to environment.

Major concept 4 · What raises IQ

Environmental Levers

If environment can raise and lower scores across whole countries, the next question is which parts of the environment matter. Researchers have identified several.

FactorWhat the evidence shows
SchoolingEach extra year of school raises IQ scores by about 1 to 5 points. Children who get an extra year of school because of a birthday cutoff score higher than children of the same age who don’t (Ritchie & Tucker-Drob, 2018). Whether skills slip over the summer is now debated, because older findings often fail to replicate in newer data (Workman et al., 2023).
LeadChildhood exposure lowers IQ. Leaded gasoline cost Americans born in the 1960s and 70s up to about 6 points each (McFarland et al., 2022).
NutritionIodine-deficient populations score about 13.5 points lower on average (Bleichrodt & Born, 1994). Children with iron-deficiency anemia tend to score lower, though proof that iron is the cause is still incomplete (Grantham-McGregor & Ani, 2001). Early malnutrition holds back development for millions of children worldwide (Grantham-McGregor et al., 2007).
Early childhood programsIQ gains from famous preschool programs faded within a few years. Perry Preschool’s gain fell from 0.75 SD at age 5 to almost nothing by age 8 (Bailey et al., 2017). Life outcomes, like finishing school and earnings, improved for decades.
Chronic stressChildren who grew up in poverty had weaker working memory as young adults, and higher chronic stress in childhood helped explain why (Evans & Schamberg, 2009).
Language environmentThe amount of back-and-forth conversation toddlers had at 18 to 24 months predicted their IQ and vocabulary ten years later, even after accounting for family income and education (Gilkerson et al., 2018).

Schooling raises intelligence

The schooling finding deserves a closer look. It comes from studies that get close to experiments. When countries raised the school-leaving age, the students who had to stay longer scored higher later. When birthday cutoffs gave some children an extra year of school by the luck of the calendar, those children scored higher too. Across 42 data sets and more than 600,000 people, the authors called education “the most consistent, robust, and durable method yet to be identified for raising intelligence” (Ritchie & Tucker-Drob, 2018).

Why would school raise IQ? Because school asks students to do, all day, the kinds of thinking IQ tests measure. Students reason about abstract ideas, hold information in mind while working with it, and solve problems they haven’t seen before. School also builds vocabulary, background knowledge, and habits of careful thinking, which is crystallized intelligence. The farm boy who left after fourth grade lost more than a diploma. He lost years of exactly the practice the Army’s test was measuring.

What lasts is knowledge and skill

The preschool studies add an important twist. Early programs raised IQ scores, and the boost faded. What lasted were the things those programs built along the way: skills, habits, and knowledge that kept paying off in school and work for decades (Bailey et al., 2017).

Retrieval practiceA preschool program raised children’s IQ by three-quarters of a standard deviation at age 5, but the gain was nearly gone by age 8. A critic says, “The program failed.” Using the evidence in this concept, how would you respond?►

The critic is judging the program only by IQ. The IQ gain did fade. But the children who went through Perry Preschool did better for decades afterward: more of them finished school, and they earned more as adults. The program built skills, habits, and knowledge that kept paying off even after the IQ difference disappeared. A program can matter a great deal without leaving a permanent mark on an IQ score.

Major concept 5 · What do we do with IQ?

Intelligence and Schools

In 1917, the Army had a number for every recruit and had to decide what to do with it. Schools face the same question every day, only more quietly. It comes up in eligibility reports, gifted referrals, and the guesses teachers make about who can do what. The science in the first four concepts answers it in three parts: how schools use scores, how to avoid misreading students, and what to believe about whether intelligence can change.

How schools use IQ scores

What teachers should know about IQ scores

If you teach students who have been formally evaluated, you’ll see IQ scores in eligibility reports and IEP paperwork.

  • 100 is exactly average. 85 is one standard deviation below, about the 16th percentile. 115 is one standard deviation above, about the 84th percentile.
  • Intellectual disability requires significant limits in both intellectual functioning and adaptive behavior, the everyday social and practical skills of daily life, beginning before age 22. An IQ score around 70, or as high as 75, is one part of that picture. A low score alone is not enough (American Association on Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities [AAIDD], n.d.).
  • Gifted identification often uses a cutoff around 130, usually along with other evidence. Because referrals miss many able students, more schools now screen everyone.
  • What the score doesn’t tell you: how hard a student works, what they know about your subject, what they’ve been taught, or what they can do with strong instruction.

Use the number as one piece of a larger picture, never as the picture itself. A score describes performance on a set of tasks. It says nothing about a student’s worth: “Remember the worth of souls is great in the sight of God” (Doctrine and Covenants 18:10). Every child deserves dignity, high expectations, and strong teaching, whatever their score.

Retrieval practiceTwo teachers learn that a new student has an IQ score of 85. Teacher A says, “I’ll adjust my expectations.” Teacher B says, “I need to find out what this student knows and can do.” Which response fits the evidence better, and what mistake is Teacher A making?►

Teacher B. An 85 is one standard deviation below average, around the 16th percentile. It tells you something about how this student did on a set of mental tasks, compared with other people. It doesn’t tell you what the student knows about your subject, what they’ve been taught, or what they can do with strong instruction.

Teacher A is treating the score as a ceiling, a fixed limit on what the student can learn. That reading goes beyond the evidence. The score is one data point. Teacher B is collecting the rest.

Two ways to misread a student

Because so many tests and classroom tasks lean on crystallized knowledge, a student’s performance can mislead you in two opposite directions.

The first is a knowledge gap: the student lacks the background knowledge a task assumes. A student who is new to the country, or who missed school, or who grew up with few books may do poorly on knowledge-heavy tasks while reasoning well. The farm boy in 1917 had a knowledge gap. The mistake is reading a gap as a limit on reasoning. A gap calls for teaching, and treating it as a fixed limit can mean the student never gets the teaching that would close it.

The second is a knowledge head start: the student arrives with a rich store of knowledge from home, through books, travel, and long dinner-table conversations. The mistake is reading a head start as high ability. A student with a head start may look far ahead in reasoning when they’re mostly ahead in what they’ve had the chance to learn. The same mistake hides students with strong reasoning and thin knowledge, because nobody thinks to look.

A large Florida school district showed how often students get missed. For years, students were tested for gifted programs only if a teacher or parent referred them. Starting in 2005, the district screened every second grader instead. The standards for qualifying didn’t change. Among third graders, the share identified as gifted rose from about 3% to 5.5%. The share of Black students identified more than doubled, and the share of Hispanic students nearly tripled. Students from low-income families and English learners were also among those most likely to have been missed (Card & Giuliano, 2016).

That one study makes two points at once. The ability was real: these students met the same bar as everyone else. And the old referral system wasn’t seeing them. This is confirmation bias at the scale of a school system. Adults referred the students who already looked gifted to them, and the students who didn’t fit that picture were never tested. The fix was universal screening: testing every student, instead of only those referred by teachers or parents, so that able students aren’t missed.

Group gaps and the student in front of you

Group data say something about the systems students have moved through, not about any one student. A student who has grown up in a high-poverty area, attended under-resourced schools, and lived with chronic stress hasn’t had the same chances to build the skills tests measure as a student who hasn’t faced those things. That gap is real. It reflects circumstance, not capacity. A teacher’s job isn’t to lower expectations based on group membership. It’s to recognize that measured gaps in knowledge and skill reflect a student’s learning history, and to teach from there.

Retrieval practiceA sixth grader who moved from Guatemala last year scores low on a cognitive test that includes a lot of vocabulary. In math class, she solves pattern problems faster than anyone. A colleague says, “The test shows she’s below average, so she’s in the right group.” Using fluid and crystallized intelligence, explain what the colleague may be missing. What would you do next?►

The test leans on crystallized knowledge, like English vocabulary and general information that depends on schooling and experience. A low score there may mostly reflect one year of English, not her reasoning. Her speed with pattern problems suggests strong fluid reasoning. The colleague is reading a knowledge gap as a limit on reasoning.

Next steps: look at measures that depend less on English, such as nonverbal reasoning tasks. Build her academic English and background knowledge directly. And ask whether your school screens every student for advanced programs or relies on referrals, since referrals are where students like her tend to get missed.

What to believe about intelligence

What teachers believe about intelligence shapes what they expect from students, how they design lessons, and who they think can learn what. So it’s worth being precise about what the evidence supports.

Intelligence can change a little in a lifetime, but tremendously across generations. Psychologists call this intelligence malleability: how much intelligence can change with experience. Within one life, the changes are modest. Each year of school adds a few IQ points, and the boosts from early programs tend to fade. Across generations, the changes are huge: whole countries gained about 30 points in a century. You might see a little of this in your own classroom, as a student’s reasoning sharpens over a year of strong teaching. But a higher score isn’t the goal. The goal is the knowledge and skill school is built to grow. Those are a large part of what intelligence tests measure, they keep growing across life, and they’re the part of a mind that teaching changes most directly.

Expectations matter, but less than the legend says. You may have heard of the classic study in which teachers were told that certain students were about to “bloom,” and those students’ IQs supposedly rose. Teacher expectations do affect students, but the effects are usually small, and the claim that they raise IQ is weakly supported (Jussim & Harber, 2005). The more useful point is practical. A teacher who believes a student can learn is more likely to give that student the teaching that makes learning possible.

Students’ own beliefs matter a little. Chapter 16 covered growth mindset. Teaching students that ability can grow helps a little, for some students, in some classrooms. This chapter adds the reason the belief is true: schooling really does raise measured intelligence.

That makes the quality of each school day matter for more than test scores. A day of weak teaching is, in a small but real way, a lost chance for students’ minds to grow. A day of demanding, well-taught work is part of how they grow. That holds for every student in the room, including the ones far ahead. A student who already knows most of what the class is learning isn’t gaining much while she waits. The next chapter’s question, how to match teaching to where each student actually is, starts here.

A gospel lens: intelligence that rises with us

The scriptures use the word intelligence in a larger sense than psychologists do. “The glory of God is intelligence, or, in other words, light and truth” (Doctrine and Covenants 93:36). That is not a statement about IQ scores, and it would be a mistake to read it as one.

Another passage speaks to how that intelligence is gained: “Whatever principle of intelligence we attain unto in this life, it will rise with us in the resurrection. And if a person gains more knowledge and intelligence in this life through his diligence and obedience than another, he will have so much the advantage in the world to come” (Doctrine and Covenants 130:18–19).

These verses don’t explain twin studies or the Flynn effect, and the research doesn’t explain them. But they share a premise with this concept. Knowledge and intelligence, in the scriptural sense, are gained over time through effort. For a teacher, that means no student’s starting point is the end of the story.

Retrieval practiceAt a staff meeting, a veteran teacher says, “I can’t change how smart my students are. I can only help them make the most of what they’ve got.” Several new teachers nod. Using this chapter’s evidence, what’s right about that statement, and what would you add?►

What’s right: You probably won’t see dramatic IQ changes in one year, and early IQ boosts tend to fade. Focusing on teaching well, instead of waiting for “smarter” students, is the right instinct.

What I’d add:

  • “What they’ve got” isn’t fixed. Each extra year of school raises IQ scores by about 1 to 5 points, so the schooling we provide is part of what shapes students’ measured ability.
  • The knowledge and skill we teach are a large part of intelligence. Building vocabulary, background knowledge, and fluent skills grows the crystallized side of every student’s mind, and it keeps paying off long after IQ gains fade.
  • This applies to students who are ahead too. If our strongest students spend the year reviewing what they already know, we’re not making the most of what they’ve got.

As a leader, I’d frame it as encouragement, not correction: “We do more than you’re giving us credit for.”

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice
  • Treat a score as a starting point, not a ceiling. Find out what the student knows and can do, and teach from there.
  • Watch for knowledge gaps and knowledge head starts. A student who lacks background knowledge needs teaching. A student who has a lot of it may not be as far ahead in reasoning as they look.
  • Ask how your school finds advanced students. Referral systems miss able students, especially those from low-income families, students of color, and English learners. Universal screening finds more of them.
  • Build knowledge on purpose. Vocabulary, reading across subjects, and rich discussion grow the part of intelligence that teaching changes most directly.
  • Stretch the students who are ahead. Ability differences keep mattering near the top. Waiting is not learning.
  • Make your classroom predictably safe. Chronic stress interferes with working memory and attention. A warm, stable, low-threat classroom is a condition for the thinking you’re asking students to do.
  • Remember what group gaps reflect. Measured gaps between groups reflect students’ circumstances and schooling, not a limit on what any student can learn. The same factors behind the Flynn effect, like richer language, better nutrition, and stronger schooling, are the ones that close gaps. You are one of those factors.
Chapter synthesis
In 1917, a farm boy and a college graduate walked out of the same testing room, each with a number. This chapter has asked what that number measured and what anyone should have done with it. It measured something real. Scores on all kinds of mental tasks rise and fall together, and they predict grades, work, and health, even among the most able people. But the number also measured a history: the graduate’s years of school, and the farm boy’s lack of them. Whole countries gained about 30 points in a century, and in some countries scores are now falling, because environments changed. So what should anyone have done with it? Both men deserved dignity, and work that suited them. Intelligence testing hasn’t always given people that. But intelligence is one part of a person that we can measure, even if imperfectly. You’ll meet numbers like theirs in your classroom, in eligibility reports, gifted referrals, and IEP meetings. Treat each one as one piece of evidence. Look past it to what the student knows and can do. Then build the knowledge and skill that school grows best.
General intelligence is real. The positive manifold is one of the most replicated findings in psychology, and measures of g are among the strongest predictors psychology has.
IQ predicts, but it doesn’t decide. The links to grades, work, and health are real at both ends of the scale. Outcomes for any one person vary widely.
Heritable doesn’t mean fixed. The Flynn effect, and its recent reversal within families, show that environment moves measured intelligence.
School builds minds. Schooling raises IQ, and what lasts is the knowledge and skill teaching builds.
Read the student, not just the score. A knowledge gap isn’t a reasoning limit, and a knowledge head start isn’t the same as high ability.
