Learner Differences

Every student is unique. Most of the frameworks built to capture that uniqueness aren’t. Here’s how to tell the difference.

Chapter 24~ 50 min read

In 2008, a research team published one of the most comprehensive reviews ever conducted on a widely used education framework. They were looking at learning styles — the idea that students are visual, auditory, or kinesthetic learners, and that matching instruction to preferred modality improves learning. After reviewing hundreds of studies, their conclusion was terse and uncomfortable: “the meshing hypothesis lacks empirical support.” Not weak support. Not mixed results. Not support. The hypothesis that animated thousands of lesson plans, professional development days, and curriculum redesigns simply hadn’t held up.

What made this finding complicated wasn’t the science. It was the education system’s response to it: largely, nothing changed.

But learning styles are just the tip of a much larger, more expensive iceberg. The reality is that most of the ways our education system conceptualizes learner differences are functionally fake. We have spent decades chasing complex taxonomies, custom-tailored profile matrices, and hyper-individualized lesson plans under the assumption that the finest nuances of student psychology demand radically different teaching methods.

This is a tragic misdirection of teacher energy. Are learner differences real? Of course they are. Students arrive with varying background knowledge, distinct working memory capacities, and unique personal interests. But the systemic fixation on diagnosing and treating these infinite individual differences has blinded us to a foundational truth: the vast majority of students learn the vast majority of things in the exact same way.

When we pull teachers away from the core architecture of learning to chase boutique frameworks, we weaken the single most powerful tool in the building: high-quality, evidence-based Tier 1 instruction. If a teacher cannot clearly model a concept, guide practice, check for understanding, and manage working memory load, no amount of differentiation can save the lesson. Conversely, when robust Tier 1 instruction is happening, it naturally accommodates the vast majority of real learner differences without a single color-coded worksheet.

This chapter is about stopping the chase. It is about what actually helps. Not the frameworks that feel true. The ones that are.

The big picture
The moral imperative to see students as individuals has been co-opted by a multi-million dollar industry of boutique diagnostic frameworks. True equity does not come from exhaustively profiling every student's preferences; it comes from establishing an unshakeable Tier 1 foundation rooted in cognitive science, paired with clear legal structures for when differences require formalized boundaries. Students with IEPs, 504 plans, and who are language learners all deserve precise supports, and there are meaningful differences between students in terms of interests and readiness; this chapter will give you ways to address them. But ultimately, because most brains process information through the same cognitive architecture, excellent teaching is simply about honoring that architecture.

Why a good instinct became a bad framework

Learning Styles and the Meshing Myth

The desire to differentiate comes from somewhere real and largely good. Teachers watch students struggle and succeed in different ways, and it feels important to honor that. It feels reductive to treat a classroom of thirty human beings as interchangeable. Education has a long and uncomfortable history of doing exactly that — ignoring cultural context, punishing difference, treating the student who didn’t fit the mold as the problem. The instinct to push back against that history is healthy. The problem is not the instinct. The problem is what the field built to satisfy it.

Beginning in the 1980s and accelerating through the 1990s, a wave of frameworks promised to finally capture the diversity of human learners in usable form. The most famous was Howard Gardner’s theory of Multiple Intelligences, which proposed that every student could be “smart” in their own way. The appeal was immediate. The evidence, after decades of scrutiny, was not. The proposed intelligences are not genuinely distinct — they correlate in ways that are far more consistent with general cognitive capacity than with nine independent systems. Studies testing whether teaching to a student’s dominant intelligence improves outcomes have consistently come up empty.

Learning styles fared no better. The meshing hypothesis — the claim that matching instruction to learning style improves outcomes — has been examined repeatedly and not supported. Students do have preferences. Those preferences do not predict learning gains when you design instruction around them. Personality typing instruments follow a similar pattern: intuitive appeal, widespread use, and a research base that doesn’t support the practical conclusions drawn from them.

Why these frameworks feel true

One reason learning styles and MI survive is that they feel like they’re working. Teachers who start thinking about visual vs. auditory learners often vary instruction — using diagrams, discussion, and different formats. Purposeful variation can help when a representation clarifies an idea or an activity gives students useful practice; a learning-style label is not evidence that matching caused the benefit. The framework can get credit for what good instructional design would have done anyway. When you test the specific claim — does the visual learner outperform when taught visually compared to auditorily? — the effect disappears. Feeling like something works is not the same as evidence that it does.

Strip away the frameworks and what remains is something worth holding onto: a conviction that every student deserves to be seen as a person, not a profile. Not as a kinesthetic learner, not as an ENTJ, not as a “creative type.” As a person — with a history, a set of things they care about, a level of trust (or distrust) in institutions like school, a body of prior knowledge, and a particular moment in their development. That is not reducible to a category. It also does not require one.

What it requires is the quality of attention that good teachers have always brought to their work: noticing students, taking them seriously, adjusting in response to what you actually observe rather than what a framework predicts. The rest of this chapter takes that observation seriously and replaces the frameworks that didn’t hold up with the ones that do.

Retrieval practiceA teacher uses a learning styles inventory to categorize students as visual, auditory, or kinesthetic learners, then designs different materials for each group. What specifically is wrong with this practice — and what does the research show actually explains why the practice sometimes appears to help?►

The specific wrong: the teacher is acting on the meshing hypothesis — the claim that matching instruction to preferred modality improves learning for that student. This hypothesis has been tested repeatedly and not supported. Students who are labeled “visual learners” do not consistently outperform when taught visually rather than auditorily. The label does not predict learning gains.

Why it appears to help: the teacher who starts thinking about learning styles begins varying instruction — using diagrams, discussion, and different formats. Those choices can help when they fit the topic and prompt useful thinking; any benefit does not show that instruction needed to match each student’s preferred style. As Chapter 4 explains, dual coding pairs words with useful visuals as a design choice, not a prescription based on a learner label. Choose representations and activities that fit the learning goal, then use them with the whole class. The framework was the wrong vehicle for the right instinct.

Research studyMovement can help some tasks without defining a learner

In a virtual-reality experiment, participants learned visual categories using either button presses or large arm movements. Larger movements improved learning when a category required integrating several cues, but added little when participants could judge features independently. The advantage remained when participants later responded with a button, suggesting that action helped shape learning rather than only the immediate response.

This is task-specific evidence, not a rescue of learning styles: the study changed the action used for a task, not each learner’s preferred modality. It also tested something different from dual coding, which pairs verbal and visual representations. The design question is what activity fits the learning goal.

Source: Luke Rosedahl, Yuka Sasaki, and Takeo Watanabe (2026), “Learning by moving: Physical motion accelerates category learning,” Science Advances, 12(43), eaed4003. Read the study.

ApplyDoes this study show that some students are “kinesthetic learners”? Explain the difference between a learner-matching claim and the effect tested here.►

No. The study suggests that large movements can help with some cue-integration tasks. It changed the action used in the task; it did not test whether students benefit from matching instruction to a stable preferred style.

Dual coding is for the whole class

Everyone can benefit from a useful visual. In that limited sense, everyone is a “visual learner”—but that does not make “visual learner” a fixed type, and it does not mean every image helps. Chapter 4’s discussion of dual coding connects verbal and visual representations; reviews of multimedia research support pairing relevant words and graphics and placing related information together (Noetel et al., 2022; Ginns, 2006). The point is to make the same idea easier to understand—not to decorate a slide or assign students a type.

Tier 1, MTSS, and Universal Design for Learning

Start With Strong Teaching

Before reaching for a specialized intervention, ask whether you’ve done the universal thing well. The best response to human diversity in a classroom is not thirty individualized plans. It is excellent universal practice — and there is a name for it: Tier 1 instruction.

A principle worth keeping
Good differentiated teaching starts with good teaching.

Tier 1 comes from the Response to Intervention (RTI) and Multi-Tiered Systems of Support (MTSS) frameworks, which organize instructional support in three layers. The logic of the system depends entirely on Tier 1 being genuinely strong. When it isn’t, more students appear to need Tier 2 and Tier 3 supports — and the system collapses under its own referral volume.

1Universal Instruction
High-quality, research-based teaching for all students. Should meet the needs of roughly 80% of learners without additional support.
2Targeted Support
Supplemental, small-group intervention for students who need more than Tier 1 provides. Typically 10–15% of students.
3Intensive Intervention
Individualized, intensive support for students with the most significant needs. Typically 3–5% of students.
High-quality Tier 1 in practice

Clear learning objectives that students understand — not just written on the board, but explained in language that gives students a target. Explicit instruction — modeling, guided practice, and independent practice in sequence, with worked examples and immediate error correction. Frequent low-stakes checks for understanding that give the teacher real information rather than performance of compliance. Consistent routines that reduce cognitive overhead. Deliberate questioning — structured retrieval that requires all students to think, not just the few who volunteer. Immediate, specific feedback that tells students what they actually got right and wrong. And belonging infrastructure: the relational and community conditions under which students are willing to take risks and learn.

None of that requires knowing a student’s learning style. It requires knowing the evidence base for teaching and executing it consistently.

These are whole-class practices, not supports reserved for a labeled group. Clear models, purposeful diagrams, manageable steps, guided practice, and specific feedback can benefit students across the class, including students with learning disabilities. For some, those practices will make the core lesson more accessible; they do not replace accommodations, IEP or 504 supports, specially designed instruction, or other targeted services. A strong Tier 1 gives everyone a stronger shared foundation and makes it easier to identify who needs more.

Universal Design for Learning

UDL, developed by CAST, is a framework for designing instruction that works for the widest possible range of learners from the start — rather than retrofitting accommodations after the fact. It is built on three core principles: multiple means of representation (how content is presented), multiple means of action and expression (how students demonstrate learning), and multiple means of engagement (how students are motivated and sustained).

In the context of Tier 1, UDL is most precisely understood as a systematic approach to removing unnecessary barriers. When a student cannot access content because of format, language, or presentation — not because of the content’s inherent difficulty — that is a design problem, not a learning problem. The barrier belongs to the instruction, not the learner.

UDL and cognitive load

In CLT terms, UDL earns its place by reducing extraneous cognitive load — the load created by how content is presented, not by the content itself. If a student is spending working memory decoding a format rather than processing the idea, that’s extraneous load, and removing it is a legitimate instructional goal. UDL loses its justification when it simply multiplies options without purpose — when “choice” becomes a substitute for design rather than an extension of it. The test is always: does this remove a barrier that doesn’t belong to the learning goal, or does it just add noise? Ch. 22 · Cognitive Load Theory

Retrieval practiceA school implements UDL by giving all students a choice of three formats for every assignment — written, visual, or oral. A colleague says this is “removing barriers.” Using CLT and the definition of extraneous load, evaluate this practice: what has it gotten right, and where might it go wrong?►

What it has gotten right: The instinct is correct. UDL’s purpose is to remove format barriers that are not intrinsic to the learning goal. If a student understands the content but cannot access a text-only format due to a reading difference, offering an oral option removes a barrier that was extraneous to what’s being assessed. That is a legitimate cognitive load reduction.

Where it might go wrong: Offering three choices on every assignment is not the same as removing barriers — it may introduce new ones. The Paradox of Choice is real: the decision-making process itself consumes working memory that should be available for learning. Students now have to decide how to demonstrate knowledge before they start demonstrating it. If the choice is not meaningful (i.e., if any format genuinely works for the goal), the overhead is pure extraneous load, not barrier removal.

The CLT test is clean: does this format option remove a barrier that doesn’t belong to the learning goal, or does it add complexity without purpose? If it’s the former, it earns its place. If it’s the latter, it’s design noise dressed up as inclusion.

A map of real differences

Three Kinds of Student Differences

Students genuinely differ. The claim that MI and learning styles don’t hold up is a claim about categorical frameworks, not about human variation. Variation is real; the labels are what failed. Understanding what kinds of variation actually matter — and what to do about each — is the practical goal.

Three categories cover most of what teachers encounter:

Readiness Gaps
Differences in prior knowledge and skill. Addressable through instruction. The most actionable category for classroom teachers. Handled by the Instructional Hierarchy. Not a fixed property of the student — a gap in learning history.
Clinical Differences
Diagnosed neurological or developmental conditions with legal and instructional implications. Require targeted support beyond Tier 1. Formalized in 504 plans and IEPs. Your role is bounded: implement the plan, collaborate with the team, do not diagnose.
Individual Differences
Interests, cultural contexts, experiential backgrounds, and ways of engaging with the world. Resist categorization. Responded to through deliberate knowing of students — relational knowing, not categorical labeling.
A note on scope

This chapter is about Tier 1. Clinical differences are real, important, and deserve serious treatment — but that treatment belongs in a course on special education law, disability studies, or specialized intervention. What you’ll find in Concept 5 is enough to understand your role as a general education teacher: implement the plan, collaborate with the team, and do not diagnose. A book that tries to be everything to every reader tends to be insufficient to all of them.

Retrieval practiceA student is consistently performing below grade level. Using the three categories of difference, describe three completely different explanations — one from each category — and the entirely different teacher response each would call for.►

Category 1 — Readiness Gap: The student hasn’t learned the prerequisite skills. Example: performing below grade level in algebra because single-digit multiplication was never automatized. Response: locate the student’s actual stage on the Instructional Hierarchy, back up to where the gap is, and teach forward from there. This is a curriculum and instruction problem with a curriculum and instruction solution. No referral needed unless Tier 1 adjustment fails.

Category 2 — Clinical Difference: The student has a documented learning disability (e.g., dyslexia, dyscalculia) that affects how they process the content. Response: check for an existing IEP or 504 plan and implement it faithfully. If none exists but the pattern is persistent and cross-setting, document your observations and bring them to the student support team for evaluation. Do not diagnose; do notice and refer.

Category 3 — Individual Difference: The student finds the content alienating because every example, context, and reference assumes a cultural or experiential background they don’t have. The content is accessible in principle; the delivery is creating unnecessary distance. Response: learn what the student does care about, what background knowledge they bring, and make deliberate choices at the margins of instruction to build bridges between the curriculum and their actual world. This requires relational knowing, not a referral.

When the problem is a skill, not a style

The Instructional Hierarchy

The most common and most actionable form of student difference is also the least dramatic: some students simply haven’t learned what they need to know yet. A student who hasn’t mastered single-digit multiplication will struggle with algebra regardless of their personality type. A student who reads below grade level will struggle with science content regardless of their motivation or intelligence. These are readiness gaps, and they are addressed not by labeling the student but by locating where they actually are in their learning — and teaching from there.

The Instructional Hierarchy, developed by Haring and Eaton, solves this by describing where a student is in their learning journey and what type of instruction is actually appropriate at each stage. Providing the wrong support at the wrong stage isn’t just inefficient — it can actively impede progress. Drilling fluency practice with a student who hasn’t acquired the basic skill yet frustrates without teaching. Providing step-by-step modeling to a student who is ready to generalize holds them back unnecessarily.

StageWhat the Student Can DoTeacher Moves
Stage 1
Acquisition		Learning the skill for the first time. Accuracy is the goal — speed and fluency come later.Explicit instruction, clear modeling, worked examples, guided practice step by step, immediate error correction.
Stage 2
Fluency		Performs accurately but not yet automatically. Speed and automaticity are the goal.Repeated practice, immediate feedback, gradual increases in pace. Massed practice is appropriate here.
Stage 3
Generalization		Applies the skill across different contexts and materials without relearning.Vary contexts, interleave practice, use real-world applications, prompt transfer to new situations.
Stage 4

Stage 5

The most commonly skipped stage

Traditional schooling frequently rushes past Stage 2 — fluency. Students learn a skill, demonstrate basic accuracy, and move on before that skill is automatic. The cost compounds over time: skills that never reach fluency consume working memory whenever they are used, leaving less capacity available for the more complex thinking that depends on them. Reading fluency is the clearest example, but the pattern applies across subjects. Automaticity is not a luxury. It is the foundation that frees up cognitive resources for higher-order work. When a student who should be analyzing a text is instead spending working memory on decoding words, the bottleneck isn’t their intelligence — it’s an unfinished earlier stage. Background knowledge as a readiness gap

Readiness gaps are not only about skills — they’re also about knowledge that instruction assumes students already have. A word problem about a yacht means something different to a student who has seen one than to a student who hasn’t. A history lesson that references “middle class anxieties” assumes students understand what the middle class is. A science unit that uses “household chemicals” assumes those households look a certain way. None of these is a failure of intelligence. All of them are failures to account for the background knowledge the lesson was designed around. Background knowledge functions the same way prior skill does in the Instructional Hierarchy: without it, new content has nothing to attach to. A student at Stage 1 on a concept they’ve never encountered isn’t behind — they just need the instruction to start from where they actually are. Ch. 1 · Willingham & Domain Knowledge

Retrieval practiceA teacher is frustrated: “I’ve explained this three times and practiced with the whole class, but students still can’t do it independently.” Using the Instructional Hierarchy, identify two different things that might be happening — and name the stage most commonly skipped and why its absence matters.►

Possibility 1 — Stage 1 mismatch: Students haven’t fully acquired the skill yet, but instruction has moved to independent practice as if they have. “Explaining three times” is not the same as Stage 1 instruction: explicit modeling, worked examples, guided practice step by step, and immediate error correction. If the teacher is explaining and then expecting independent execution, they may be skipping the gradual release that Stage 1 requires.

Possibility 2 — Fluency gap: Students have achieved basic accuracy (Stage 1) but never reached automaticity (Stage 2). They can perform the skill correctly when prompted, but it hasn’t become automatic enough to execute under load — and independent work is high-load by definition. Explaining again won’t fix a fluency gap. What fixes a fluency gap is repeated practice with immediate feedback and increasing pace.

The most commonly skipped stage is Stage 2 — Fluency. Its absence matters because skills that don’t reach automaticity never stop consuming working memory when used. Every future task that builds on that skill carries the overhead of the unautomated foundation. The problem doesn’t go away — it compounds, becoming harder to diagnose as the curriculum moves forward and the foundational gap gets buried under new material.

When a student has a documented need

IEPs, 504 Plans, and Your Role

Some differences among students are not gaps in prior knowledge, and they are not simply expressions of individuality. They are clinically significant neurological or developmental differences that affect how a student learns, behaves, or accesses the curriculum — and they carry legal weight. Understanding two key legal frameworks is part of your job as a general education teacher.

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act

Section 504 is a civil rights law that prohibits discrimination against individuals with disabilities in any program receiving federal funding. In schools, it protects students whose disability substantially limits a major life activity — including learning. A student with a Section 504 plan may not need specialized instruction, but they do need accommodations: extended time on assessments, preferential seating, reduced-distraction environments, permission to use assistive technology, printed copies of class notes. The 504 plan documents those accommodations and creates legal accountability for providing them.

Your role: implement the plan consistently and document when you do. If something in the plan isn’t working or isn’t feasible, that’s a conversation for the 504 coordinator — not a unilateral decision to skip it.

Individualized Education Programs (IEPs)

An IEP is a more intensive document, governed by the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA). Unlike a 504, an IEP implies eligibility under one of 13 disability categories and includes not just accommodations but specialized instruction — modified curriculum, specific related services (speech-language therapy, occupational therapy), and measurable annual goals. IEPs are developed by a team that includes the student’s parents or guardians, their general education teacher, a special education teacher, and usually a school administrator.

504 vs. IEP at a glance

Section 504: Civil rights framework (Rehabilitation Act). Disability substantially limits a major life activity. Accommodations only — no specialized instruction. No specific disability category required. Managed by a 504 coordinator.

IEP: Special education services framework (IDEA). Eligibility under one of 13 categories. Includes accommodations and specialized instruction, measurable annual goals, and related services. Developed and reviewed annually by an IEP team including the general ed teacher.

A Note on English Language Learners

English Language Learners (ELLs) are students developing proficiency in English while being educated in English. Being an ELL is not a disability, and treating it as one is both factually wrong and potentially harmful. The more accurate framing is that language acquisition is a readiness gap of a specific kind — a gap in a specific tool (English academic language), not in the student’s capacity to learn. An ELL student may understand content fully in their home language and demonstrate genuine academic ability while being unable to access a lesson delivered only in English, or demonstrate what they know on an English-only assessment. That gap is real and instructionally significant. It is a gap in a tool, not a limit on capacity.

What this means practically

Strong Tier 1 instruction matters enormously for ELL students: clear objectives, visual supports, consistent routines, explicit vocabulary instruction, and checking for understanding in multiple ways all reduce the language barrier without reducing the cognitive demand. Treat the student’s home language as an asset, not a problem. A student who thinks in two languages is doing something cognitively demanding that most of their teachers cannot do. They are not behind — they are operating across a wider linguistic range than monolingual peers.

Your role as a general education teacher is to implement the plan — faithfully and consistently — and to communicate with the team when something isn’t working. You are not expected to diagnose. You are not expected to design the IEP. You are expected to know what it says, do what it requires, and show up to meetings prepared to speak to the student’s academic performance in your class. Most students with IEPs spend the majority of their school day in general education classrooms. Strong Tier 1 instruction is not irrelevant for students with clinical differences. It is the floor they stand on while receiving more targeted support.

Retrieval practiceA student with a documented disability has no IEP — only a 504 plan with extended time and preferential seating. You think the student might need more. Walk through your role: what you should do, what you should not do, and what the difference between those two things actually is.►

What you should do: Implement the existing 504 plan consistently and document that you are doing so. Observe the student carefully and document specific, concrete behaviors — not diagnostic conclusions, but factual observations: “Across the past three weeks, student X has had difficulty initiating tasks in every class period; produced below-grade-level written work on four consecutive assessments; demonstrated understanding verbally that does not appear in written products.” Bring these observations to your school’s student support team or counselor and ask whether a referral for evaluation is appropriate.

What you should not do: Diagnose. Modify the 504 plan unilaterally. Tell the family you think the student has ADHD, a learning disability, or any other specific condition. Skip accommodations because you think something different would help more. These actions exceed your professional role and may have legal implications.

The distinction: Your role is observation, documentation, and referral — not diagnosis or plan modification. The clinical specialists, the family, and the evaluation process determine what the student needs. Your observations are a critical input into that process; they are not the process itself. Notice and refer. Implement and document. Those are your lanes.

Some differences resist neat boxes

Knowing the Student in Front of You

After readiness gaps and clinical differences, there remains a vast and important category of ways students differ that resist neat classification: their interests, the cultural worlds they inhabit, the experiences they bring to the content, what they find worth caring about, what they’ve been asked to distrust. These differences are real. They affect learning. And they don’t fit into a box.

Interest as the Most Actionable Variable

The most teachable individual difference — the one that classroom practice can most directly engage — is interest. Not personality type. Not learning modality. Interest: what a student cares about, what they find worth thinking about, what they’d choose to read if given the option.

Interest matters for a clear cognitive reason: people process information more deeply when it connects to something they already care about. Prior knowledge activates more readily. Attention sustains longer. Germane load — the effortful processing that produces real learning — increases. Interest is one of the most reliable triggers for activating prior knowledge — which is the scaffold that makes new information stick. Ch. 22 · Cognitive Load Theory

Interest in practice

The practical implication isn’t that every lesson needs to be personalized to thirty different interests — that’s neither possible nor necessary. It’s that a teacher who knows their students can make choices at the margins that change engagement meaningfully. A math problem set in a context students recognize works better than one in an alien context, holding everything else constant. A writing prompt that leaves room for personal connection produces better work. An example drawn from something students are currently interested in lands differently than one from a context they have no relationship with. These are small choices with real cumulative effects. They don’t require a learning styles theory. They require knowing what students care about.

Cultural Context and Background Knowledge

Students don’t arrive as blank slates — they arrive as people, with histories that shape what they already know, what questions they’re primed to ask, what they find obvious and what they find surprising. Cultural context is part of this. A student whose household runs a small business brings economic intuitions to a lesson on supply and demand that another student doesn’t. A student who has navigated between two languages has linguistic awareness that is a genuine cognitive asset. A student who has had to be responsible at home in ways their peers haven’t has a kind of practical intelligence that doesn’t show up in standardized assessments. None of this is captured by a learning styles inventory. It is captured by attention.

Categorical vs. Relational Knowing

The alternative to pseudoscientific frameworks isn’t indifference — it’s a better kind of attention. The field has sometimes offered teachers a choice between elaborate categorization systems and treating everyone identically. That’s a false choice. What you’re actually choosing between is categorical knowing — labeling students by type — and relational knowing — understanding this student, in this class, at this point in their life.

Relational knowing is not systemizable. It accumulates through the ordinary work of paying attention: noticing what lights students up, what shuts them down, what they bring to the classroom that the curriculum hasn’t accounted for, where they are on the skills you’re trying to build. It requires treating every student in front of you as someone whose particularity precedes and exceeds any category you could put them in. Whatever conviction gets you there — whatever framework, experience, or belief gives you access to that quality of attention — is worth holding onto. The research supports it. So does something more basic: it’s what treating people with dignity actually looks like in a classroom.

Why it’s actionable: Interest is teachable — teachers can discover it, respond to it, and build on it through ordinary professional attention. It doesn’t require a diagnostic instrument, a specialized intervention, or a clinical referral. It requires knowing students and making choices at the margins of instruction: choosing contexts, examples, and prompts that connect to what students actually care about.

Mechanism 1 — Prior knowledge activation: Interest is a reliable trigger for activating prior knowledge. When a student cares about a topic, the prior knowledge they already have about adjacent content becomes more available — more connections activate, new information has more existing structure to attach to. This is precisely the mechanism Willingham describes as the scaffold that makes new information stick. Instruction that connects to student interest leverages this mechanism without any additional effort.

Mechanism 2 — Germane cognitive load: In CLT terms, interest increases the proportion of working memory devoted to germane load — the effortful processing that produces actual schema-building — at the expense of distraction and off-task thinking. A student who is genuinely engaged occupies perceptual resources on the content itself (perceptual load theory, Chapter 22) and directs cognitive effort toward the kind of deep processing that produces durable memory (Willingham: “memory is the residue of thought”). Interest doesn’t make hard work easy; it makes the hard work more likely to happen.

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

The framework question — which categorization system should I use? — is less important than the attention question: what do I actually know about the students in front of me, and is my instruction meeting them where they are?

  • Stop using learning styles inventories to sort students into groups. The meshing hypothesis doesn’t hold. What does hold: vary modalities and formats for all students, because varied presentation benefits everyone — not because it matches individual “types.”
  • When a student struggles, ask which category the difference belongs to before deciding what to do. A readiness gap calls for instruction. A clinical difference calls for implementing the plan and collaborating with the team. An individual difference calls for relational attention. These are very different responses to what can look like the same problem.
  • Diagnose the Instructional Hierarchy stage before prescribing a remedy. Drilling a student who hasn’t acquired the skill is frustrating for both of you. Modeling step-by-step for a student ready to generalize is holding them back. The stage mismatch is the problem; matching instruction to stage is the fix.
  • Treat fluency as non-negotiable, not a bonus. Skills that never reach automaticity keep consuming working memory for as long as the student uses them. Build fluency practice into your sequence before moving on.
  • Know what your students’ IEPs and 504 plans actually say — and implement them consistently. If something isn’t working, bring it to the team. If something isn’t feasible, bring it to the coordinator. Skipping accommodations is not an option.
  • Cultivate interest. Learn what your students care about. Use that knowledge to make choices at the margins: contexts, examples, prompts, topics. These are small moves with real cumulative effects on engagement and learning.
Chapter synthesis
Every student is unique. Most of the frameworks built to capture that uniqueness aren’t — and the evidence for this is clear enough that continuing to use them is a choice, not a default. What replaces them is not indifference to human variation. It is better tools: the Instructional Hierarchy for readiness gaps, the legal frameworks of 504 and IEP for clinical differences, and relational knowing — the accumulated attention of a teacher who treats every student as a person before a profile — for everything else. Strong Tier 1 instruction, built on what the evidence actually supports, serves more students more effectively than any amount of style-matching or intelligence-typing ever did.
Learning styles and Multiple Intelligences capture a real instinct — that students differ in ways that matter — but not a real mechanism. The meshing hypothesis has been tested and not supported. The framework gets credit for what good instructional variety does anyway.
Strong Tier 1 instruction is the best universal response to human diversity. It clarifies who needs more targeted support by making the signal legible. When Tier 1 is weak, the noise obscures who actually needs intervention.
UDL earns its place by reducing extraneous cognitive load — removing barriers that belong to the instruction, not the content. It loses its justification when it multiplies options without purpose.
Readiness gaps are the most actionable category. The Instructional Hierarchy names where a student is and what instruction they need. Fluency is the most commonly skipped stage, with compounding consequences for higher-order work.
Interest is the most teachable individual difference. Through prior knowledge activation and increased germane load, it leverages the cognitive science directly. Knowing what students care about is professional knowledge, not sentiment.
