Practical Significance

How Guilty? How Innocent? Is the Juice Worth the Squeeze?

Chapter 6~ 40 min read

The jury has delivered its verdict. Guilty.

But here is what that verdict does not tell you: guilty of what? Was this jaywalking or armed robbery? A parking ticket or securities fraud? The verdict establishes that something happened — that the evidence was strong enough to rule out innocence. But establishing guilt and innocence is completely different from how serious the crime is.

This is where statistical significance stops and practical significance begins. Chapter 5 was about reading the verdict. Chapter 6 is about reading the sentence. The two questions are related but distinct — a study can establish that an effect is real and still tell you almost nothing about whether the effect is large enough to justify changing policy to adopt an intervention.

Suppose a new tutoring program produces a statistically significant improvement in reading fluency. It clears the statistical threshold, p < .05, the jury is convinced. Now suppose the improvement is one word per minute. On a measure where typical readers vary by thirty or forty words per minute, a difference of a single is statistical noise — it is smaller than the natural day-to-day variation of individual readers. Should your district spend $200,000 implementing this program? You can't answer that question with statistical significance alone. It was never designed to answer that question.

Practical significance has the tools for it, and it has many. This chapter introduces two of the most prominent of them: Cohen’s D for measuring the size of differences between groups and Pearson’s R for measuring the strength of relationships — plus a powerful application: Hattie’s synthesis of educational research for situating both in the context of what actually moves student outcomes.

Armed with both statistical and practical significance, you are now empowered to answer the series of questions that will eventually be put to you as an educator: is this effect real? If so, does it really help students?

In short, does the juice justify the squeeze?

The big picture
Chapter 5 answered whether an effect is real. Chapter 6 asks whether it matters. The same data — even the same finding — can look very different depending on which question you’re asking. The tools in this chapter are what turn “something happened” into “here is how large a thing happened.” Learning to use them will change how you read every piece of research you encounter — and how you make decisions based on it.

Major Concept 1 · How Guilty?

Statistical vs. Practical Significance

To understand why statistical significance isn’t enough, you need to understand what it was designed to do — and what it was never designed to do.

Statistical significance answers one question: is this result real? Is the pattern in the data something that happened in the world, or something that happened by chance in this particular sample? That is a genuinely important question. It is also a limited one. The answer “yes, this is probably real” tells you nothing about the magnitude of the thing that is real. A real effect can be enormous. It can also be vanishingly small.

Imagine a cancer drug that prolongs the life of sufferers consistently, every single time; but only by a few days. Statistically significant? Yes. Practically significant? Likely not. The importance of practical significance becomes even more complicated when we include the cost of an expensive medication and the impact of side effects. Practical significance allows us to weigh all of these variables.

There is a direct connection between sample size, practical significance, and statistical significance. Large samples make significant p-values easier to find. They also make weaker effects easier to spot. A single person brought back from the dead would be a sufficient sample size to prove that something real is happening and that what is happening is important. But if the effect is weaker, the only way to spot it is with a larger sample: if a medicine relieves cancer, but only for a few days, you won't pick up the effect without tens of thousands of participants in a study. A study with 50,000 participants can detect a difference of one-tenth of a point on a 100-point scale — reliably, consistently, with a p-value far below any threshold you might set.

Now note: you can find a difference of one-tenth of one percent on a 100-point scale with 50,000 participants. That effect is real. It is also, in almost any practical context, completely meaningless. No intervention that makes such a small difference is worth putting into practice, especially if it costs money or precious classroom time to implement.

The statistically significant finding tells you the prosecution won the verdict. It does not tell you anyone should go to prison. Statistical significance says "the effect from the intervention we're studying probably is not just noise." Practical significance tells you if it's worth caring about.

Practical significance asks "how large is the effect?" The statistical term we use is effect sizes. An effect size is a standardized measure of magnitude — it answers "how much?" in a way that allows comparison across studies, across outcome measures, and across populations. Researchers have developed dozens of effect size measures for different research contexts. This chapter focuses on two that appear most frequently in educational research: Cohen's D for comparing groups, and Pearson's R for measuring relationships between variables. These help us answer "is the effect large enough to matter, to act on, to justify the cost of implementation?"

The standardization is important. An improvement of 8 points on one test and an improvement of 8 points on a different test may represent very different achievements, depending on how variable each test is. For example, imagine a 10 point test vs. a one-thousand point test. Similarly, many academic papers ask questions about test scores, others about graduation rates, some about college completion. What we need is a common unit, a way to compare apples to oranges in a way that retains some level of meaning and coherence. Fortunately, we already have such a standard measure: the standard deviation.The standard deviation makes comparisons across different tests and contexts meaningful.

Previously, we applied standard deviation to individuals: a person that is one-sigma above the mean might be a little tall, but nothing crazy. Now, we apply it to groups. When we talk about effect sizes in research, we are usually asking how far apart two group averages are. A person can be five sigmas from the average, but that would barely move the average of a group of 10,000 a tiny bit. While a person who is one sigma above the mean in IQ is intelligent, that isn't anything to write home about; but an intervention that raises the IQ of a population by one standard deviation is likely a very big deal.

Think of it this way: if the typical variation in reading fluency among third-graders is 30 words per minute, then an improvement of 3 words per minute is one-tenth of a standard deviation — a trivially small effect. An improvement of 30 words per minute is a full standard deviation — a transformative one. Same units (words per minute), completely different stories.

Common misconception“The study found a significant effect, so the program must be worth implementing.”►

Statistical significance gives evidence that an effect probably is not just noise. It says nothing about whether the effect is large enough to justify the cost, disruption, and opportunity cost of implementation. A program that produces a statistically significant but tiny improvement has cleared one bar — the results aren’t a fluke. It has not cleared the more important bar: is this worth doing?

The “juice and squeeze” question is what practical significance was designed to answer. What is the effect size? How does it compare to alternative uses of the same resources? How does it compare to other interventions targeting the same outcome? These are the questions that follow from “real” — and they are the ones that determine whether a school actually adopts something.

Major Concept 2 · Measuring Differences

Cohen’s D

When a researcher wants to know how different two groups are — how much a treatment moved outcomes relative to a control — the standard tool is Cohen's D, introduced by statistician Jacob Cohen in 1969. The formula is conceptually simple: the difference between the two group means, divided by the pooled standard deviation. The result expresses how far apart the groups are in standard deviation units.

A Cohen’s D of 1.0 means the treatment group’s mean is one full standard deviation above the control group — roughly equivalent to moving a student from the 50th percentile to the 84th. A Cohen’s D of 0.5 moves a student from the 50th to the 69th. A Cohen’s D of 0.2 is a modest shift — real, but small.

Small · d = 0.2
The groups overlap substantially. The effect is real but modest — visible in large datasets, unlikely to be noticeable in an individual classroom. Worth knowing about; not worth reorganizing a school around.
Medium · d = 0.5
A meaningful difference, noticeable to a careful observer. Roughly equivalent to a student moving from the 50th to the 69th percentile. A reasonable benchmark for interventions worth serious consideration.
Large · d = 0.8
A substantial difference, easily observable without statistics. Roughly equivalent to moving from the 50th to the 79th percentile. In educational research, effects this large are relatively rare and worth significant attention.

Cohen himself described these benchmarks as rough conventions — not natural laws. A d = 0.2 effect might be trivial in the context of a cheap universal intervention but impressive in the context of an expensive one. The benchmarks are starting points for judgment, not substitutes for it. The scholar-practitioner question is always: how large is this effect relative to what it costs to produce it?

A note on Hattie: when John Hattie performed his research on the variety of effect sizes in education research, he also took an average of the effects that show up in the research literature. The average effect was a 0.4 standard-deviation effect size. As such, he named 0.4 his "hinge point;" interventions below 0.4 standard deviations deserve extra scrutiny, while interventions above 0.4 may deserve more serious attention (e.g. interventions with a Cohen's d above 1.0 standard deviations). This is what you've learned in previous chapters, but now you understand enough that it should make a lot more sense to you.

Worked exampleComputing and interpreting Cohen’s D

The study: A district tests a new vocabulary program. After 8 weeks, the treatment group (n = 120) has a mean score of 74 on a vocabulary assessment; the control group (n = 118) has a mean of 68. The pooled standard deviation is 15 points.

Computing D: (74 − 68) ÷ 15 = 6 ÷ 15 = d = 0.40

Interpretation: The treatment group is 0.40 standard deviations above the control — a small-to-medium effect. Using Cohen’s benchmarks, this sits between small and medium; in Hattie's educational frame, it is right at the hinge point.

The practical question: Is d = 0.40 worth it? That depends entirely on what the program costs, how it compares to other vocabulary interventions, and what else the district could do with the same resources. The effect size tells you the size of the sentence — the rest of the judgment belongs to the practitioner.

Retrieval practiceA new reading intervention reports a Cohen’s D of 0.18 and a p-value of .002. The district is excited — “it’s highly significant!” How would you respond?►

The district is conflating statistical significance with practical significance. The p-value of .002 tells us the effect is almost certainly real — with high confidence, this is not a fluke. But d = 0.18 is a small effect by any benchmark. The result being “highly significant” statistically likely reflects a large sample size, which makes even tiny effects detectable, rather than a meaningful intervention effect.

The right question is: what does a d = 0.18 improvement actually look like for a student? In most reading contexts, it’s a modest shift — likely noticeable only in aggregate data, not in individual classrooms. Before recommending adoption, a scholar-practitioner would want to know the program’s cost, implementation burden, and how d = 0.18 compares to other reading interventions available to the district. “Statistically significant” is the beginning of the analysis, not the end of it.

Major Concept 3 · Measuring Relationships

Pearson’s R

Cohen’s D measures the size of a difference between two groups. But not all research questions are about differences between groups. Some are about relationships between variables — does attendance predict achievement? Does socioeconomic status relate to graduation rates? Does teacher experience correlate with student outcomes? For these questions, the standard tool is Pearson’s R, commonly referred to simply as R. 

Pearson’s R measures two things simultaneously: the strength of a linear relationship between two variables, and its direction. The value always falls between −1 and +1. A value of +1 indicates a perfect positive relationship — as one variable increases, the other increases proportionally, without exception. A value of −1 indicates a perfect negative relationship — as one increases, the other decreases proportionally. A value of 0 indicates no linear relationship at all.

In practice, correlations of +1 or −1 essentially never appear in social science data. What we see instead are values that tell us how reliably one variable predicts another. By convention: R values between 0 and ±.3 are considered weak; ±.3 to ±.7 are moderate; ±.7 to ±1 are strong. These, like Cohen’s benchmarks, are starting points — the strength that matters in practice depends on what is being measured and what decision depends on the result.

One critical clarification: Pearson's R measures the strength of a relationship, not the existence of a cause — the distinction Ch. 3 established in full. The coefficient quantifies how reliably two variables move together. The causal story requires a different kind of evidence entirely.

Retrieval practiceResearchers find R = .91 between monthly ice cream sales and drowning deaths. A public health official proposes banning ice cream to reduce drowning. What does the R value actually tell you — and what doesn’t it?►

R = .91 tells you that ice cream sales and drowning deaths move together with very high reliability — as one goes up, so does the other, consistently. That is a genuinely strong relationship. It does not tell you that ice cream causes drowning. The far more plausible explanation is a confounding variable: summer temperature. Hot weather increases both ice cream consumption and swimming; more swimming means more drowning risk. Both variables are effects of the same cause — they are correlated because of what drives both, not because one produces the other.

Banning ice cream would not reduce drowning deaths, because ice cream is not in the causal chain. The R value, however high, cannot tell you this — only experimental design or careful theoretical reasoning can. This is why the transition from “these things are correlated” to “therefore we should intervene on X” requires far more than a strong R. The coefficient measures the relationship. Everything else is inference that needs independent justification.

Retrieval practiceA study finds R = −.52 between student absences and end-of-year GPA. Interpret this result: what does the sign mean, what does the magnitude mean, and what can and cannot be concluded?►

The sign (negative):As absences increase, GPA tends to decrease. The relationship moves in opposite directions — more of one predicts less of the other.

The magnitude (.52):This is a moderate relationship. Not a perfect predictor, but reliably present. About 27% of the variance in GPA (R² = .52² ≈ .27) is accounted for by absence rates — which means roughly 73% is explained by other factors entirely.

What can be concluded:Attendance and GPA are meaningfully related. Students who miss more school tend to earn lower GPAs. This is strong enough to justify attendance as a focus for intervention.

What cannot be concluded:That absences cause lower GPAs — or that improving attendance will necessarily improve GPA. Some absences reflect illness or family instability that also affects academic performance independently. The correlation may be capturing the effects of shared causes rather than a direct causal relationship. Caution is warranted before assuming the intervention target is obvious.

Major Concept 4 · Hattie's Hinge Point — The Full Picture

Applied Effect Sizes

Chapter 4 introduced John Hattie's synthesis and the hinge point as a provocation: when someone tells you an intervention works, ask how much — and whether it clears d = 0.40, the average effect of simply attending school. At that point, you had the story and the motivation but not the technical tools to fully evaluate what the numbers mean. Now you do. Cohen's D is the unit Hattie's entire synthesis is built on. His synthesis translates many findings into d values — differences expressed in standard deviation units. The hinge point is not an arbitrary threshold; it is Hattie's benchmark for the average effect found across the Visible Learning research base. What sits above it clears that benchmark. What sits below it does not.

The reduced table below illustrates the range. Notice where teacher collective efficacy sits — a factor whose motivational significance you encountered in Chapter 4 can now be read technically: the treated group's mean achievement was 1.57 standard deviations above the control group's. In Cohen's terms, that is nearly twice a "large" effect. At the other end of the table, class size reduction at d = 0.21 is a real effect — but it sits well below Hattie's benchmark, which puts its cost-benefit ratio in a different light than it might first appear. This is the “is the juice worth the squeeze” question from earlier, now with a benchmark attached: the hinge point gives you a rough line to measure the juice against, so the judgment isn’t just “is this real” but “is this worth more than the ordinary year of schooling it’s competing with.”

Critiques of Hattie"The synthesis compares apples to aircraft carriers — the effect sizes aren't really comparable."▸

This critique is serious and worth engaging now that you have the tools to evaluate it. The core problem iscomparability. Hattie aggregates d values from studies measuring extremely different outcomes — reading fluency, math achievement, self-concept, graduation rates — across different populations, grade levels, and contexts. A d = 0.4 from a tightly controlled reading RCT with second-graders tells a different story than a d = 0.4 from a large observational study of class size effects in high schools. Cohen's D is standardized within a study; it is not automatically comparable across studies measuring different things with different instruments.

A second problem isbaseline quality. Some of the meta-analyses Hattie drew from compared interventions against inadequate control conditions, which artificially inflates the effect size for that intervention. A d value is only as meaningful as the comparison group is appropriate.

A third problem isstudy quality aggregation. Meta-analysis is only as good as the studies it synthesizes. Including low-quality studies with high-quality ones does not average out to medium-quality evidence — it contaminates the estimate. The "garbage in, garbage out" principle applies at the meta-meta-analysis level just as it does at the study level.

None of this means Hattie is useless. It means the hinge point is a rough guide for prioritization, not a precise measurement instrument. The correct use of the synthesis is as a starting point for deliberation — "this effect size suggests class size reduction is not the highest-leverage investment" — not as a final verdict. The critiques don't undermine the hinge point; they calibrate how confidently you should hold any specific number within it.

And as Hattie’s critics would note, these effect sizes come from different populations and study designs — treat the ranking as a starting point for deliberation, not a final answer.

Note the table of critique of Hattie, above.

Even a synthesis of more than a thousand studies has to be held with open hands — some of its numbers rest on weak comparisons, mismatched outcomes, and studies that might not replicate if anyone re-ran them. And that caution doesn’t stop at Hattie. A great deal of what gets published in education and psychology turns out to be smaller, shakier, or less repeatable than its headline suggested. The reason we share Hattie's research is because it is one of the premiere, evidence-based, accessible research endeavors that have reached public consciousness. We anticipate that his findings will be challenged, revised, and replicated over the coming years. But this raises the question the final concept is really about: once you know that even peer-reviewed findings fail at an uncomfortable rate, how much evidence should it take to move you?

Major Concept 5 · Beyond a Reasonable Doubt

Scientism and Life-Alpha

Two people can read about the replication crisis and walk away with opposite lessons. One decides that if even peer-reviewed science can't be trusted, nothing can, and stops updating on evidence at all. The other decides science basically works, ignores the failure rate, and keeps trusting the next study that confirms what they already believed. Neither is doing this well. Somewhere between them is a threshold — how much evidence do you personally require before you'll change your mind? — and that threshold has a name: your life-alpha.

The term borrows directly from Chapter 5. Statistical alpha is a threshold a researcher sets in advance: how much evidence is required before rejecting the null hypothesis. Life-alpha is the same idea applied to you personally: how much evidence you require before rejecting a belief you already hold. Set it too low — easily moved by a single study, a compelling anecdote, a persuasive voice — and you become the credulous mark. Set it too high — refusing to update no matter what accumulates — and you become the cynic, mistaking stubbornness for rigor.

The credulous face real risks: they buy bogus programs for their students, they spend money on medical scams, they believe hokum, and they lose out for it all. It is not good to be gullible. But you should be skeptical of skepticism too: "doubting" Thomas, the apostle was reproved precisely because there are some things that require believing, not mere seeing.

One particular danger is scientism. The scientistic view trusts all conclusions arriving with a p-value, a regression coefficient, or an effect size — and ignores anything else. A values claim, a piece of qualitative evidence, a student's own account of their experience — these get waved away as "just anecdote," regardless of their actual quality. What's more, many who embrace scientism are less interested in arriving at the right conclusions through rigorous inquiry and are more interested in wielding dogmatic answers as weapons to put the less-enlightened in their place.

This isn't rigor. It's cynicism with a lab coat on, dismissing entire categories of legitimate evidence because they didn't arrive in the right format. Recall Hume's Guillotine: knowing what's true doesn't tell you what to value or what to do. Scientism is the mistake of thinking the guillotine doesn't apply to you — that if you measure hard enough, the ought will fall out of theis. It doesn't. It never does. Science is a wonderful, miraculous tool; but it is only a tool.

The scholar-practitioner's life-alpha sits between the cynic and the zealot — high enough not to be swayed by one underpowered study, low enough to actually update when well-replicated evidence says to. An underpowered study is, in the courtroom terms this chapter opened with, a hung jury. It can't convict and it can't acquit — it doesn't establish that the effect is real, but it doesn't clear it either.

These tools will make you better as a person, a scholar, and a practitioner. But there is nothing in these chapters that allows you an epistemological insurance policy. You will still get things wrong.

If you're careful, however, and work very hard to apply what you've learned in these last two chapters, you might be able to get a few things less wrong.

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

Practical significance is the bridge between research and decision. These tools don’t just help you read studies better — they help you ask better questions when someone is trying to convince you to adopt something.

  • When someone presents research in favor of a program, ask for the effect size alongside the p-value. ““Statistically significant” is an important signal, not a sufficient one. The effect size tells you the size of the sentence.
  • Use d = 0.4 as a rough filter. Interventions that consistently fall below the hinge point — especially expensive or disruptive ones — deserve serious scrutiny. Interventions consistently above it deserve serious attention. Neither judgment is automatic, but the hinge point helps you ask the right question.
  • When you see a correlation reported (R = something), separate strength from causation. A strong R shows a strong observed association. It does not tell you what is causing what — that question requires design, not statistics.
  • Watch for effect sizes that are real but trivially small. The larger the study, the easier it is to detect tiny effects. Always ask: even if this is real, is it large enough to matter at the classroom level?
  • Use the “juice and squeeze” question as a professional habit. Before supporting a new initiative, ask: what is the effect size relative to what this costs in time, money, and opportunity? The squeeze is always real. The juice should be too.
Chapter synthesis
Two numbers sit at the heart of every piece of educational research you will ever read. One tells you whether the result is real. The other tells you whether it matters. You now have both. Chapter 5 gave you the verdict. Chapter 6 gave you the sentence. Together, they give you what the scholar-practitioner actually needs: not just confidence that something happened, but a calibrated sense of how much happened, and whether it is worth acting on.
Statistical significance and practical significance answer different questions. Real effects can be trivially small. Large effects can fail to reach significance in underpowered studies. Both numbers matter.
Cohen’s D measures the size of a difference in standard deviation units. Benchmarks of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 are starting points — the right threshold depends on cost, context, and what else is available.
Pearson’s R measures the strength and direction of a relationship. A strong R is evidence of a strong observed association — not evidence of causation. The causal story requires design, not statistics.
Hattie’s 0.4 hinge point is a rough guide for prioritization, not a definitive ranking. Its critiques are real. Used with appropriate humility, it remains one of the most useful tools in the educator’s evidence toolkit.
Your life-alpha is your personal evidentiary threshold. Set it too low and you're credulous; too high and you're a cynic. Scientism is a specific version of the high setting — trusting only what arrives with a p-value, and forgetting that Hume's Guillotine still applies.
