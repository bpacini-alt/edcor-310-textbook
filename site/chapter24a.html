One of the authors of this book, Mayleigh, teaches special education. Here is how she describes her class.

“I teach a special education resource English class. It’s a modified English course. Its job is to help students close gaps in their reading and writing, and to help them succeed in the rest of their classes.

“If you looked at my class list, you might guess that every student in it has the same problem. You’d be wrong.

“Some of my students are there because they have intense dyslexia or fluency-based reading issues. Others have trouble with comprehension, and some have both. Some are there because they have significant needs in their writing, and others have a combination of reading and writing needs. Some have intense intellectual needs. Others have language impairments that make it hard to understand spoken and written language. Some have anxiety so severe that getting access to what they read, understanding it, and making connections is incredibly difficult. And some are exceptionally bright and just need extra help.

“They’re all in one class. Being in that class doesn’t define what they can do.”

So if the label on a student’s schedule doesn’t tell a teacher what that student needs, what does?

The big picture

One evening in the early 1970s, a few wheelchair users in Berkeley, California, rolled up to a curb they couldn’t get past. Activists there had been pushing the city for ramps, and when the official ones came slowly, some built rough asphalt ramps themselves. Over the following decades, curb cuts spread across the country. Then something unexpected happened. People pushing strollers used them, and so did kids on bikes, travelers with suitcases, and workers with delivery carts. A change made for the people who needed it most turned out to help nearly everyone. Angela Glover Blackwell (2017) called this the curb-cut effect.

Classrooms work the same way. Teachers are told to meet every student’s individual needs, and that’s right. But students are more alike than different in how they learn (Willingham, 2009). The best place to start is with teaching that helps everyone, then add what particular students need on top of it.

This chapter follows that order. It starts with strong teaching for the whole class, then looks at where students differ in readiness, clears away two popular theories that don’t hold up, and turns to the interests and cultures students bring. It ends with students who need more than a curb cut.

Major concept 1 · What all students share

Tier 1 Instruction

A curb cut is built once, at the corner, for everyone who will ever cross there. The classroom version is the teaching every student gets every day. Before reaching for a specialized intervention, ask whether the everyday teaching is strong.

Key aphorism

Good differentiated teaching starts with good teaching.

Schools usually organize support in three tiers, through systems called MTSS or RTI. Tier 1 is what every student gets, Tier 2 adds small-group help, and Tier 3 adds intensive, individual help. Tier 1 instruction is the high-quality, research-based teaching that every student in a general education classroom receives.

Tier 1 instruction works alongside Tier 1 supports: the things a teacher deliberately builds into the room to support learning, like routines, posted schedules, and written directions. The two are connected, but they aren’t the same. Instruction is the teaching. Supports make it easier for every student to take that teaching in.

1Universal instruction
High-quality, research-based teaching for all students. Common target: meets the needs of about 80% of students without additional support.
2Targeted support
Small-group help for students who need more than Tier 1 provides. Common target: about 10–15% of students.
3Intensive intervention
Individualized help for students with the most significant needs. Common target: about 3–5% of students.

These percentages are planning targets, not research findings. The whole system depends on Tier 1 being strong. When it’s weak, many students look like they need extra help when what they really need is clearer teaching. When it’s strong, the students who truly need more stand out.

What strong Tier 1 looks like

PracticeWhat it means
Clear goalsStudents know what they’re learning and what success looks like, in words that give them a target.
Explicit instructionModeling, then guided practice, then independent practice, with worked examples and quick error correction
Frequent checksShort, low-stakes checks that show the teacher who has it and who doesn’t

None of this requires sorting students into types. It requires knowing what the evidence says about teaching and doing it consistently.

These are whole-class practices, not supports saved for a labeled group. Clear models, purposeful diagrams, manageable steps, guided practice, and specific feedback help students across the class, including students with learning disabilities. For some students, they make the core lesson reachable. They don’t replace accommodations, IEP or 504 services, or specialized instruction. They’re the floor those supports stand on.

Strong Tier 1 also has room above grade level. Tier systems are usually described from the bottom up, as a net for students who fall behind. But a student who has already mastered today’s skill and gets twenty more problems of the same kind is mostly waiting. A strong lesson plans for students who finish early, with work that moves them to the next stage of the skill. The next concept describes those stages.

Universal Design for Learning

UDL borrows its name from architecture, where universal design means building spaces that everyone can use from the start. Curb cuts are the classic example. Universal Design for Learning (UDL), developed by the organization CAST, is a framework for planning lessons that remove unnecessary barriers for all learners from the start, instead of adding fixes later. It rests on three principles: more than one way to stay engaged, more than one way to take in content, and more than one way to show learning (CAST, 2024).

UDL is about unnecessary barriers. If a student can’t reach the content because of the format, the language, or the setup, and not because of the content itself, that’s a design problem. The barrier belongs to the lesson, not the learner.

Putting UDL to work takes three steps:

  1. Name the learning goal. Be specific about what students should know or be able to do by the end.
  2. Find the barriers that aren’t part of the goal. Ask what could keep a student from reaching the goal for reasons that have nothing to do with it.
  3. Remove those barriers for everyone, from the start. Build the fix into the lesson for the whole class instead of waiting for a student to struggle.

UDL in practice

BarrierFix built into the lessonWho else it helps
Directions given only out loudPost them in writing too, with numbered stepsStudents who missed a step, English learners, students who were absent

Each fix keeps the thinking the lesson asks for. If the goal is reading, the reading stays. If the goal is writing, the writing stays. UDL removes the obstacles around the goal, not the goal itself.

UDL and cognitive load

UDL earns its place when it cuts extraneous load. If a student spends working memory decoding a format instead of thinking about the idea, removing that barrier is worth doing. UDL loses its value when it piles up options with no purpose. Choosing among options takes working memory too, especially for students who are new to the material. Before adding an option, ask whether it removes a barrier that isn’t part of the learning goal or just adds noise.

Retrieval practiceA school puts UDL into practice by giving every student a choice of three formats for every assignment: written, visual, or oral. A colleague says this is “removing barriers.” Using cognitive load theory and the idea of extraneous load, what has the school gotten right, and where might it go wrong?►

What it gets right: UDL’s purpose is to remove barriers that aren’t part of the learning goal. If a student understands the content but can’t show it in writing because of a reading or writing disability, an oral option removes a barrier that had nothing to do with what’s being assessed. That’s a real reduction in extraneous load.

Where it might go wrong: Three choices on every assignment isn’t the same as removing barriers, and it can add new ones. Choosing takes working memory, especially for students new to the material. Students now have to decide how to show what they know before they start showing it. If the choice doesn’t remove a real barrier, that decision is just extra load. And if the goal of an assignment is writing, an oral option removes the goal along with the barrier.

The test is whether each option removes a barrier that isn’t part of the learning goal. If it does, it earns its place. If it doesn’t, it’s noise that only looks like inclusion.

Major concept 2 · Differences in readiness

Instructional Hierarchy

Even on a sidewalk everyone can use, people are at different points along the route. In a classroom, the most common difference between students is also the least dramatic. Some students haven’t yet learned what a lesson assumes. Others learned it long ago. A student who hasn’t mastered multiplication facts will struggle with algebra no matter how motivated they are. A student who mastered the unit’s skills over the summer will learn little from a unit spent practicing them.

Both are readiness gaps, differences between what a student already knows or can do and what a lesson assumes. Neither is solved by labeling the student. Both are solved by finding out where the student is with the skill and teaching from there.

The Instructional Hierarchy, first described by Haring and Eaton (1978), describes where a student is with a particular skill and what kind of instruction fits that point. The wrong support at the wrong time does more than waste time. It can slow learning down. Drilling for speed with a student who hasn’t learned the skill frustrates without teaching. Step-by-step modeling for a student who’s ready to use the skill in new places holds them back.

StageWhat the student can doTeacher moves
Stage 1 · AcquisitionLearning the skill. Accuracy is the goal.Explicit instruction, modeling, worked examples, guided practice, immediate error correction.
Stage 2 · FluencyAccurate, but slow or effortful. Speed and automaticity are the goal.Repeated practice, immediate feedback, a gradually faster pace. Massed practice is appropriate here.
Stage 3 · Generalization and adaptationFluent, but needs to use the skill in new settings and on new kinds of problems.Vary contexts and materials, mix in related problems, ask when and why the skill applies, pose unfamiliar problems, have students explain their strategy.

Haring and Eaton split the third stage in two: generalization (using the skill in new settings) and adaptation (changing it to solve new problems). Many researchers who use the model today, including Amanda VanDerHeyden and Matthew Burns, combine them into one stage, and so does this course.

A stage belongs to a skill, not a student. The same student can be fluent with multiplication and still learning fractions.

How strong is the evidence, and which stage gets skipped?
  • Best tested: the first step. Studies in reading and math find that students who are still making errors gain more from modeling and guided practice, while students who are accurate but slow gain more from repeated practice (Burns et al., 2010; Szadokierski et al., 2017).
  • Most often skipped: fluency. Schools often move on once students show basic accuracy, before the skill is automatic. A skill that never reaches fluency uses up working memory every time it’s used, which leaves less room for harder thinking. A student who should be analyzing a story but is still sounding out words is stuck on an earlier stage. Building fluency also helps students keep a skill months later (VanDerHeyden & Burns, 2009).
  • Least tested: Stage 3. The third stage has much less research behind it, mostly small studies of elementary reading and math. Students also don’t need perfect fluency before they practice using a skill in new places, because mixing problem types helps them learn when a skill applies.

Treat the hierarchy as a guide for choosing instruction, not a law.

When a student is ahead

The hierarchy works in both directions. A student who is already fluent with a skill doesn’t need Stage 1 or Stage 2 teaching on it. They need Stage 3: new settings, harder problems, and questions about when and why the skill applies. Giving them more practice at a stage they’ve passed is the same mismatch in the other direction.

The research on moving advanced students forward is stronger than many teachers expect. A review of about a century of studies found clear academic gains from acceleration, which lets a student move through material faster than usual, such as skipping a grade or taking a subject with an older class (g = 0.42). Grouping advanced students together for instruction also helped (g = 0.37 for special grouping of gifted students), and so did grouping within a regular classroom (g = 0.19 to 0.30) (Steenbergen-Hu et al., 2016).

In a regular classroom, that can look like:

  • A pretest before a unit. Students who already show mastery skip the practice they don’t need.
  • Stage 3 work on the same skill: unfamiliar problems, real applications, or explaining a strategy to someone else
  • Acceleration in a single subject: taking math with an older class while staying with classmates for everything else

Advanced students also need to struggle sometimes. Effort that feels hard in the moment, the kind researchers call desirable difficulties, is part of how memories and skills grow strong. A student who coasts through school without ever being stretched may meet real difficulty for the first time much later, with no practice at handling it.

Background knowledge

Readiness isn’t only about skills. It’s also about the knowledge a lesson assumes. A word problem about a yacht means something different to a student who has seen one than to a student who hasn’t. A history lesson about “middle-class anxieties” assumes students know what the middle class is. A science unit about “household chemicals” assumes households look a certain way.

Chapter 23 named the two ways this can mislead a teacher. A knowledge gap can look like weak reasoning when the student simply hasn’t met the material. A knowledge head start can look like high ability when the student has mostly had more chances to learn. Either way, new learning attaches to what students already know. A student who has never met a concept isn’t behind in ability. They need the teaching to start where they actually are.

A gospel lens: according to his several ability

In the parable of the talents, a man leaving on a journey trusts his servants with his goods: “unto one he gave five talents, to another two, and to another one; to every man according to his several ability” (Matthew 25:15). When he returns, the servant who had five has gained five more, and the servant who had two has gained two more. Each hears the same words: “Well done, thou good and faithful servant” (Matthew 25:21).

The parable is about stewardship, what we do with what God gives us, not about classroom instruction. But it names something this concept depends on. The servants started in different places, and each was expected to grow from where he started. A teacher who matches instruction to each student’s readiness, behind or ahead, asks every student the same question: what did you gain from what you were given?

Retrieval practiceA teacher is frustrated: “I’ve explained this three times and practiced with the whole class, but students still can’t do it on their own.” Using the Instructional Hierarchy, name two different things that might be happening. Then name the stage most often skipped and explain why skipping it matters.►

Possibility 1, a Stage 1 mismatch: Students haven’t fully learned the skill, but instruction has moved to independent practice as if they had. Explaining three times isn’t the same as Stage 1 teaching, which means modeling, worked examples, guided practice step by step, and quick error correction. The teacher may be skipping the gradual handoff that Stage 1 needs.

Possibility 2, a fluency gap: Students can do the skill accurately when prompted (Stage 1), but it isn’t automatic yet (Stage 2). Independent work puts a heavy load on working memory, and a skill that isn’t automatic falls apart under that load. Explaining again won’t fix a fluency gap. Repeated practice with quick feedback and a gradually faster pace will.

The most skipped stage is Stage 2, fluency. Skills that never become automatic keep using up working memory every time students use them. Every later skill built on that one carries the extra load, so the problem grows and gets harder to spot as the curriculum moves on.

Retrieval practiceA fourth grader finishes her math practice in five minutes, every time, with no errors. Her teacher has started giving her twenty more problems of the same kind when she’s done. She has begun rushing and making careless mistakes, and she says math is boring. Using the Instructional Hierarchy, explain what’s going on and what the teacher could do instead.►

She’s fluent with this skill, so she’s ready for Stage 3. Twenty more problems of the same kind are Stage 2 work, more fluency practice, for a student who has already reached fluency. Getting more of the same as a reward for finishing teaches her that careful work earns more busywork, which may explain the rushing.

Instead, the teacher could pretest at the start of each unit and let her skip practice she doesn’t need. When she finishes, she could get Stage 3 tasks on the same skill: unfamiliar word problems, a problem she has to explain to a classmate, or a real situation where she decides whether the skill applies. If she’s far ahead across the whole subject, the teacher could raise subject acceleration with the school.

Major concept 3 · Differences that don’t hold up

Learning Styles and Multiple Intelligences

Many popular ideas about the brain are neuromyths. Most of them didn’t start as nonsense. They usually carry a good teaching habit wrapped inside a theory that doesn’t hold up. A teacher’s job is to keep the habit and drop the theory, and that means learning to tell the two apart.

The two most popular examples in schools grew out of a good impulse. Teachers watch students struggle and succeed in different ways, and treating a class of thirty people as interchangeable feels wrong. Education also has a long history of ignoring culture and treating the student who didn’t fit the mold as the problem. Starting in the 1980s, a wave of frameworks promised to honor students’ differences in a usable form. Two of them are still everywhere.

Both get the curb cut backward. A curb cut is one design that serves everyone. These frameworks build a separate entrance for each “type” of learner, around differences that don’t hold up.

Learning styles

The idea is that some students are visual learners, some are auditory, and some are kinesthetic, so teaching should match each student’s style. Teachers learn it in workshops, parents mention it at conferences, and students use it to describe themselves. The claim that matching instruction to a style improves learning is called the meshing hypothesis.

The claim makes a clear prediction. Sort students into visual and auditory learners. Teach half of each group with pictures and half with spoken explanations. Then test everyone the same way. If learning styles are real, visual learners should do best with pictures, and auditory learners should do best with spoken explanations.

In 2008, four learning scientists searched the research for studies with that design. They found very few, and the ones that existed mostly didn’t show the predicted pattern. They concluded there was no adequate evidence for using learning-style assessments in schools (Pashler et al., 2008). Later studies built to test the prediction directly also found nothing. In one, students labeled as visual or auditory learners learned no better when the format matched their label (Rogowsky et al., 2015).

Students do have preferences. Some people would rather look at a diagram than hear a lecture. But preferring something isn’t the same as learning better from it.

Key aphorism

We are all visual learners.

Visuals can help all students learn. That does not mean students have fixed learning styles or that instruction should be matched to them.

Learn more
  • Source: Hattie and Yates (2014, p. 115), Visible Learning and the Science of How We Learn. The same passage also says that we are all auditory learners.
  • Meaning: Learning from visuals is a shared human capacity, not a permanent student type.
  • Why it matters: Choose visuals when they make an idea clearer for learners, not to match a student to a learning-style label.

Why it lasts

Across 37 studies of more than 15,000 educators, about 89% said they believed in matching instruction to learning styles (Newton & Salvi, 2020). Many of those teachers had been taught otherwise.

Part of the reason is the elephant and the rider. Learning styles feels right. It matches what teachers see, because students do differ, and it sounds kind. Knowing the research lives with the rider. The habit lives with the elephant.

The bigger reason is the good habit inside it. A teacher who starts thinking about visual and kinesthetic learners usually starts using more diagrams, more movement, and more discussion. Some of those changes help, because a good diagram makes some ideas clearer for everyone. The theory gets credit for what good lesson design was doing anyway. Keep the diagrams and drop the labels.

Where it hides

Learning styles rarely shows up by name anymore. It hides in everyday phrases and forms:

  • “He’s a hands-on learner, so reading isn’t really his thing.”
  • A first-day survey that sorts students into visual, auditory, and kinesthetic groups
  • Choice boards labeled by style instead of by learning goal
  • An IEP accommodation that reads “kinesthetic learner: provide hands-on instruction”

That last one matters most for future special educators. An accommodation should remove a documented barrier. A student with dyslexia might get audiobooks because decoding print is a barrier to reaching grade-level content. That’s an accommodation for a barrier, not a match for a learning style, and it doesn’t replace teaching the student to read. A style label in an IEP sounds like support, but it can quietly lower what’s expected of a student. It can also hurt students directly. A student who decides “I’m not a reading person” may stop practicing the very skill that would help most (Newton & Salvi, 2020).

Match the method to the content and the goal, not to the student. Geography is best taught with maps. Pronunciation is best taught with sound. A fractions lesson may need a number line, and a persuasive essay needs examples of persuasive writing. Choose the representation that makes the idea clearest, and use it with the whole class.

Dual coding is for the whole class

Everyone can benefit from a useful visual. In that limited sense, everyone is a “visual learner.” That doesn’t make “visual learner” a fixed type, and it doesn’t mean every image helps. Dual coding pairs words with useful visuals as a design choice for everyone. Reviews of multimedia research support pairing relevant words and graphics and placing related information close together (Noetel et al., 2022; Ginns, 2006). The point is to make the same idea easier to understand, not to decorate a slide or assign students a type.

Go deeperMovement can help some tasks without defining a learner

In a virtual-reality experiment, participants learned visual categories using either button presses or large arm movements. Larger movements improved learning when a category required combining several cues, but added little when participants could judge features one at a time. The advantage remained when participants later responded with a button, which suggests the movement helped shape the learning itself, not just the response.

This is evidence about a specific task, not a rescue of learning styles. The study changed the action used for a task, not each learner’s preferred style. It also tested something different from dual coding, which pairs words with visuals. The design question is which activity fits the learning goal.

Source: Luke Rosedahl, Yuka Sasaki, and Takeo Watanabe (2026), “Learning by moving: Physical motion accelerates category learning,” Science Advances, 12(43), eaed4003. Read the study.

Apply: Does this study show that some students are “kinesthetic learners”? Explain the difference between a learner-matching claim and the effect tested here.

Answer: No. The study suggests that large movements can help with some tasks that require combining several cues. It changed the action used in the task. It didn’t test whether students benefit from matching instruction to a stable preferred style.

Retrieval practiceYou’re the special education teacher at an IEP meeting for a seventh grader who reads two years below grade level. His mother and his science teacher both say he’s “a hands-on learner.” They propose adding this accommodation: “Kinesthetic learner: provide hands-on instruction in all classes.” What would you say, and what would you propose instead?►

Start by agreeing with what’s true. He may enjoy hands-on work, and both adults clearly want him to succeed.

Then separate preference from need. Matching instruction to a learning style hasn’t been shown to improve learning, and a style label in an IEP can lower expectations for reading, the skill he most needs to build. Ask what the actual barrier is. Here, the data point to reading: he can’t yet read grade-level text on his own.

Propose accommodations tied to that barrier, such as audio versions of science texts so he can reach the content, and pre-taught key vocabulary. Pair them with direct reading instruction, so the accommodation opens access while his reading grows. Hands-on activities can still be used wherever they fit the science content, for every student in the class.

Multiple intelligences

In 1983, psychologist Howard Gardner proposed that people have several separate intelligences instead of one general one. His original list had seven: linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, and intrapersonal. He added naturalist in 1999 and has called existential intelligence a possible “half” (Gardner, 1983, 1999).

The good habit inside the theory is real. Students do have different strengths. Schools often reward a narrow slice of human ability, and looking for talent where tests don’t look is good practice.

But the theory makes a testable claim: the intelligences are separate. If they were, a student’s musical ability would tell you nothing about their verbal or spatial ability. That isn’t what the data show. Chapter 23 described the positive manifold, the pattern in which scores on all kinds of mental tests rise and fall together. Gardner’s abilities follow the same pattern (Visser et al., 2006). Several of his categories also look more like talents or personality traits than intelligence as psychologists use the word. And the theory was built mainly from case studies and argument, not experiments that could prove it wrong.

So keep Gardner’s message: look for ability in many places, and teach in varied ways. Drop his map of separate intelligences. Varied teaching helps all students because it gives everyone more ways into an idea, not because it matches each student to a type.

Retrieval practiceYour principal announces that every lesson plan must now list which of Gardner’s intelligences it targets. A colleague says, “What’s the harm? It’s about seeing every kid.” What would you say to the principal, respectfully and clearly?►

Start with what’s right: the goal of seeing every student and teaching in varied ways is a good one. Then name the problem. Gardner’s intelligences aren’t separate. Students who do well on one kind of task tend to do well on others. This is the positive manifold, one of the most replicated findings in psychology. Planning around the categories puts teacher time into a map that doesn’t match how minds work, and it can lead teachers to sort students into types.

Then offer a better version. Ask that lesson plans show varied ways into the content, like a diagram, a discussion, and a hands-on task, chosen because they fit the material. That keeps the principal’s goal and drops the part the evidence doesn’t support.

Major concept 4 · Differences in interest, culture, and experience

Contextual Differences

The Berkeley activists knew which corners mattered because they crossed them every day. Teachers learn about their students the same way, by paying attention. Students differ in ways that don’t sort neatly: their interests, the cultures they live in, the experiences they bring, what they care about, and what they’ve learned to distrust. These differences are real, they affect learning, and they don’t fit in a box.

Interest

The difference a teacher can most directly work with is interest: what a student cares about, what they find worth thinking about, and what they’d choose to read if given the choice. Personality types and learning styles don’t help a teacher here. Knowing what students care about does.

Interest matters for a clear reason. People think more deeply about information that connects to something they already care about. Their prior knowledge comes to mind more easily, and their attention lasts longer. When students care about a topic, more of their limited working memory goes to the content instead of to distraction. And because interest brings prior knowledge to mind, it gives new information something to attach to.

This doesn’t mean every lesson has to be personalized to thirty different interests. That’s neither possible nor necessary. A teacher who knows their students can make small choices that change how much students engage:

  • A math problem set in a context students recognize works better than one set somewhere unfamiliar, with everything else held the same.
  • A writing prompt that leaves room for a personal connection tends to produce better work.
  • An example drawn from something students care about right now lands differently than one from a world they have no connection to.

These choices are small, but their effects add up.

Culture and experience

Students don’t arrive as blank slates. They arrive with histories that shape what they already know, what questions they’re ready to ask, and what they find obvious or surprising. A student whose family runs a small business brings real economic sense to a lesson on supply and demand. A student who moves between two languages every day has an awareness of language that is a real asset, and the home language is something to build on. A student who carries a lot of responsibility at home may have a kind of practical skill that never shows up on a standardized test. A learning-styles inventory captures none of this. Attention does.

Know the student, not just the file

There are two ways to know a student. One is through the file: test scores, labels, plans, and categories. The file matters, and later in this chapter it will matter a great deal. The other is through attention over time: noticing what lights a student up and what shuts them down, what they bring that the curriculum hasn’t accounted for, and where they are with the skills you’re teaching.

Teachers are sometimes offered a false choice between elaborate sorting systems and treating everyone the same. The better choice is to read the file and then keep watching the person. That kind of attention can’t be turned into a checklist. It builds up through the everyday work of teaching. The research supports it, and so does something more basic: it’s what treating people with dignity looks like in a classroom.

President Boyd K. Packer put it this way: “The superb teacher also studies the students—studies them seriously and intently” (“Study Your Students,” 1963, p. 17, quoted in a Church teaching manual). The manual that reprints the line connects close observation of students with compassion in teaching. Here it names the practical alternative to learner profiles: notice the actual person, their readiness, and what they bring to class.

Retrieval practiceWhy is interest described as the individual difference a classroom teacher can most directly work with? Connect your answer to at least two ideas from the cognitive science in this course.►

Why it’s workable: Teachers can discover interest, respond to it, and build on it through ordinary attention to their students. It doesn’t take a diagnostic test, a special program, or a referral. It takes knowing students and making small choices about contexts, examples, and prompts.

Idea 1, prior knowledge: When a student cares about a topic, what they already know about it comes to mind more easily, so new information has more to attach to. New learning sticks to what students already know.

Idea 2, working memory and attention: Interest means more of a student’s limited working memory goes to the content and less to distraction. A task that holds a student’s interest leaves less attention free to wander, and it pushes them to think hard about the material. And since “memory is the residue of thought” (Willingham, 2009), what students think hard about is what they remember. Interest doesn’t make hard work easy. It makes the hard work more likely to happen.

Major concept 5 · Differences that call for a plan

Exceptional Learners

Curb cuts didn’t make every place reachable for everyone. Some people still need an elevator, a ramp built to fit, or a ride. Some students are the same. Strong Tier 1 teaching helps them, and they still need more.

A note on scope

If you’re preparing to teach special education or gifted education, you’ll get much more training in this, in courses on special education law, gifted education, and specialized intervention. This concept covers what every teacher needs: know the plan, carry it out, work with the team, and notice and refer without diagnosing.

Legal duties, and not all of them special education

Every kind of support in this concept comes with a legal duty. When a student qualifies, the school, and you as one of the student’s teachers, must provide what the law requires. But these supports aren’t all the same kind of thing, and most of them aren’t special education.

English learners show the difference most clearly. Learning English is not a disability, and a student doesn’t need a disability, or a gift, to receive English learner services. In Lau v. Nichols (1974), the Supreme Court ruled that a San Francisco district had denied about 1,800 Chinese-speaking students a meaningful education by teaching them only in English with no language support. Since then, schools have been required to identify students who are learning English and give them help with the language.

Most of this concept is about exceptional learners: students whose needs differ enough from their peers’ that they qualify for special services, including students with disabilities and students who are gifted. A disability may change how a student learns or reaches the curriculum. Giftedness may put a student so far ahead that ordinary classroom adjustments aren’t enough. English learners aren’t exceptional learners, but their support is required by law too, so they close the concept.

A label like special education or gifted makes a student eligible for services, but it doesn’t tell you what the student needs. That’s why Mayleigh’s class can’t be taught as one group. Every student in it has the same label and a different plan: one for decoding, one for writing, one for spoken language, one for anxiety. The plan is where you find out what each student needs.

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act

Section 504 is a civil rights law. It prohibits discrimination against people with disabilities in any program that receives federal funding. In schools, it protects students whose disability substantially limits a major life activity, including learning. A student with a 504 plan may not need specialized instruction, but they do need accommodations, such as extended time on tests, preferential seating, a low-distraction setting, assistive technology, or printed copies of class notes. The 504 plan lists those accommodations and makes the school legally responsible for providing them.

Your role is to carry out the plan consistently and to document that you do. If something in the plan isn’t working or isn’t possible, take it to the 504 coordinator. Don’t decide on your own to skip it.

Individualized Education Programs (IEPs)

An IEP is a more intensive plan, governed by the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA). A student qualifies under one of 13 disability categories. Unlike a 504 plan, an IEP includes specialized instruction as well as accommodations. It may include a modified curriculum, related services like speech-language therapy or occupational therapy, and measurable annual goals. An IEP is written by a team that includes the student’s parents or guardians, a general education teacher, a special education teacher, and usually a school administrator.

Your role as a general education teacher is to carry out the plan faithfully and consistently, and to tell the team when something isn’t working. You aren’t expected to diagnose, and you aren’t expected to design the IEP. You are expected to know what it says, do what it requires, and come to meetings ready to describe how the student is doing in your class. In 2023, about two-thirds of students served under IDEA spent at least 80% of the school day in regular classrooms (U.S. Department of Education, 2025). For many of these students, the general education teacher is the person who teaches toward their IEP goals most of the day. The special education teacher plans with you, supports you, and may co-teach, but the student’s progress in your class is your responsibility. Take it as fully as you take responsibility for every other student. Strong Tier 1 instruction is the floor they stand on while they get more targeted support.

Go deeperHow are learning disabilities identified?

For decades, schools identified a specific learning disability mainly by looking for a gap between a student’s IQ and their achievement. A student with an average IQ who read far below grade level might qualify. Critics pointed out that this often meant waiting for a student to fail badly enough to show a large gap. It also missed students whose IQ scores were pulled down by the same reading problem. In 2004, federal law changed. States can no longer require that gap, and they may use a student’s response to strong, research-based intervention instead (34 C.F.R. § 300.307). This is one reason strong Tier 1 and Tier 2 teaching matters so much. In many schools, it’s part of how disabilities are identified.

Gifted students

Gifted education isn’t covered by federal special education law, so each state sets its own rules. Idaho requires every school district to identify gifted and talented students and to provide for their special instructional needs (Idaho Code § 33-2003). Idaho’s education department describes gifted education as a required service, not a required program. It recognizes five areas of giftedness: intellectual, specific academic, creative, leadership, and visual and performing arts.

Requiring something doesn’t make it happen. In 2024, 45% of Idaho’s traditional school districts reported identifying zero gifted students (Idaho Department of Education, 2024). Across the country, many able students are never identified, especially when schools wait for referrals instead of screening everyone. For a classroom teacher, that means two things. Notice students who may be far ahead, even when they don’t look like the stereotype of a gifted kid. And know how your district identifies gifted students, so you can refer them.

Twice-exceptional students

Some students are both gifted and have a disability. These twice-exceptional students are easy to miss, because each label can hide the other. A gifted student with dyslexia may work hard enough to read at grade level, so both the disability and the giftedness go unnoticed. Or the disability gets all the attention, and nobody looks for the strengths. The exceptionally bright students in Mayleigh’s class may be twice-exceptional. A twice-exceptional student may need an IEP and advanced work at the same time.

Retrieval practiceA student with a documented disability has a 504 plan with extended time and preferential seating, but no IEP. You think the student may need more. Walk through your role: what you should do, what you shouldn’t do, and what separates the two.►

What you should do: Carry out the 504 plan consistently and document that you’re doing it. Watch the student closely and write down specific, factual observations, not diagnoses. For example: “Over the past three weeks, the student had trouble starting tasks every class period, turned in below-grade-level writing on four assessments in a row, and explained ideas aloud that didn’t appear in the written work.” Bring these to your school’s student support team or counselor and ask whether a referral for evaluation makes sense.

What you shouldn’t do: Diagnose. Change the 504 plan on your own. Tell the family you think the student has ADHD, a learning disability, or any other condition. Skip accommodations because you think something else would help more. These go beyond your role and can have legal consequences.

What separates them: Your role is to observe, document, and refer. Specialists, the family, and the evaluation process decide what the student needs. Your observations are an important part of that process, but they aren’t the process.

Retrieval practiceAn eighth grader has an IEP for a specific learning disability in written expression. In class discussions, she makes connections nobody else sees, and her science fair project won the district competition. Her teachers mostly talk about her missing assignments. What might be happening, and what would you do?►

She may be twice-exceptional. Her disability in writing is real and documented, and it may be hiding strong ability that shows up when writing isn’t required, like in discussion and hands-on projects.

I would keep carrying out her IEP accommodations for writing, and document what I see: specific examples of her advanced reasoning, along with her work samples. Then I’d bring it to her IEP team and ask whether she has been screened for gifted services. She may need support for writing and more challenge in content at the same time. One doesn’t cancel the other.

English learners

As the table at the start of this concept shows, English learner services are their own kind of support, separate from special education and 504 plans.

Schools use many names for these students: English language learners (ELL), English learners (EL), English as a second language students (ESL), second language learners (SLL), multilingual learners, and emergent bilinguals. The names change by state, district, and decade, but they describe the same students. This book uses English learner: a student who is still developing English while being taught in English.

The difference is one of readiness in a single tool: academic English. A student may understand a concept fully in their home language and still be unable to follow a lesson taught only in English, or to show what they know on an English-only test. That gap is real, and it calls for teaching, not lowered expectations.

Strong Tier 1 helps a great deal here: clear goals, visuals, consistent routines, explicit vocabulary teaching, and checking for understanding in more than one way. Many of the UDL fixes in Concept 1 were made for exactly this. They lower the language barrier without lowering the thinking. Treat the home language as an asset. A student who thinks in two languages is doing something demanding that many of their teachers can’t do.

A student can be an English learner and have a disability. Mixing the two up causes harm in both directions. An English learner can be referred for special education they don’t need, and a student with a real disability can be told “it’s just the language” for years. Telling them apart takes evidence from the student’s home language, their progress over time, and specialists who know both.

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

What do you actually know about the students in front of you, and is your teaching meeting them where they are?

  • Start with what helps everyone. Strong Tier 1 instruction and supports help most students and make it clear who needs more.
  • Build the curb cuts into the lesson. Name the goal, find the barriers that aren’t part of it, and remove them for the whole class from the start.
  • Find each student’s stage, in both directions. Back up for a student who’s behind. Move a student who’s ahead to harder work on the same skill, not more of the same.
  • Treat fluency as non-negotiable, not a bonus. Skills that never become automatic keep using up working memory for as long as students use them. Build fluency practice into the sequence before moving on.
  • Plan for the students who finish early. Pretest, skip what they’ve already mastered, and have Stage 3 work ready.
  • Keep the habit, drop the myth. Use diagrams, movement, and varied tasks because they fit the content, not to match a student “type.” In an IEP, tie each accommodation to a documented barrier.
  • Study your students. Learn what they care about and use it to make small, steady choices about examples and contexts.
  • Know each student’s plan, and own their progress. Carry out IEPs and 504 plans faithfully, and teach toward IEP goals as part of your everyday work. If something isn’t working, bring it to the team. Notice and refer students who may be gifted, including students who don’t fit the stereotype.
  • Don’t confuse language with disability. Build English and keep high expectations for English learners, and remember that a student can be both an English learner and have a disability.
Chapter synthesis
The curb cuts in Berkeley were built for people in wheelchairs, and they ended up serving nearly everyone. Teaching works the same way. Students are more alike than different in how they learn, so strong teaching for the whole class comes first, with barriers removed from the start. On top of that floor, students differ in ways that matter. Some are behind with a skill and some are ahead, and the Instructional Hierarchy tells a teacher what each needs next. All of them bring interests, cultures, and languages a teacher can build on. Some are exceptional, at either end or both, and their plans describe needs a label can’t. Learning styles and multiple intelligences don’t belong on that list. They carry good habits worth keeping, but the theories have been tested and not supported, so continuing to use them is a choice. The curb cuts in Berkeley are a good model for a new teacher. Some people will always need more than a curb cut, and some of your students will too. Those specialized services are covered in other courses and majors. This course is about effective teaching for all students, and what you can build now is the classroom’s curb cut: strong Tier 1 teaching, planned with your students who need the most in mind. Nearly every student will use it, the students who need more will be easier to see, and when those students are in your class, their progress is still yours to own.
