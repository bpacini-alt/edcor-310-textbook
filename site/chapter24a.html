Learner Differences

Every student is unique. Most of the frameworks built to capture that uniqueness aren’t. Here’s how to tell the difference.

Draft note: First person is Mayleigh.

I teach a special education resource English class. It’s a modified English course. Its job is to help students close gaps in their reading and writing, and to help them succeed in the rest of their classes.

If you looked at my class list, you might guess that every student in it has the same problem. You’d be wrong.

Some of my students are there because they have intense dyslexia or fluency-based reading issues. Others have trouble with comprehension, and some have both. Some are there because they have significant needs in their writing, and others have a combination of reading and writing needs. Some have intense intellectual needs. Others have language impairments that make it hard to understand spoken and written language. Some have anxiety so severe that getting access to what they read, understanding it, and making connections is incredibly difficult. And some are exceptionally bright and just need extra help.

They’re all in one class. Being in that class doesn’t define what they can do.

So if the label on their schedule doesn’t tell me what a student needs, what does?

The big picture

Teachers are told, again and again, to meet every student’s individual needs. That advice is right, but it leaves out the hard part: knowing which differences between students actually matter.

Some popular frameworks answer that question with labels: visual learner, kinesthetic learner, resource kid, gifted kid. A label can be useful, but it can also hide the need it’s supposed to describe. Students are more alike than different in how they learn (Willingham, 2009). Where they differ is in where they are with a skill, what support the law entitles them to, and who they are. Those differences run in every direction. Some students are behind, some are far ahead, and some are simply different.

Kind of differenceWhat it looks likeWhat it calls for
ReadinessPrior knowledge and skill, behind or ahead of the lessonTeaching matched to where the student is with that skill
ExceptionalityA disability, giftedness, or bothA formal plan or service, carried out faithfully
ContextInterests, culture, language, experienceKnowing the student well enough to build bridges

This chapter starts by clearing away the most popular label that doesn’t work. Then it builds from strong teaching for everyone toward each kind of difference that does.

Major concept 1 · Good advice hiding inside bad theories

Learning Styles

The desire to differentiate comes from somewhere real and mostly good. Teachers watch students struggle and succeed in different ways, and it feels important to honor that. Treating a class of thirty people as interchangeable feels wrong. Education also has a long, uncomfortable history of doing exactly that: ignoring culture, punishing difference, and treating the student who didn’t fit the mold as the problem. Pushing back against that history is healthy. The trouble is what the field built to do it.

Starting in the 1980s, a wave of frameworks promised to capture the variety of human learners in a usable form. The most famous was Howard Gardner’s theory of multiple intelligences, which proposed that every student could be smart in their own way. It fails because people’s abilities turn out to be linked, not separate. Personality-typing tools follow a similar pattern: an intuitive appeal, wide use, and a research base that doesn’t support the classroom conclusions people draw from them. The most popular of these frameworks is learning styles.

The idea is everywhere. Some students are visual learners, some are auditory, and some are kinesthetic, so teaching should match each student’s style. Teachers learn it in workshops, parents mention it at conferences, and students use it to describe themselves.

It’s also a neuromyth. The claim that matching instruction to a style improves learning is called the meshing hypothesis, and it has been tested many times and not supported.

How you’d test it, and what happened

The claim makes a clear prediction. Sort students into visual and auditory learners. Teach half of each group with pictures and half with spoken explanations. Then test everyone the same way. If learning styles are real, visual learners should do best with pictures, and auditory learners should do best with spoken explanations.

In 2008, four learning scientists searched the research for studies with that design. They found very few, and the ones that existed mostly didn’t show the predicted pattern. They concluded there was no adequate evidence for using learning-style assessments in schools (Pashler et al., 2008). Later studies built to test the prediction directly also found nothing. In one, students labeled as visual or auditory learners learned no better when the format matched their label (Rogowsky et al., 2015).

Students do have preferences. Some people would rather look at a diagram than hear a lecture. But preferring something isn’t the same as learning better from it.

Key aphorism

We are all visual learners.

Visuals can help all students learn. That does not mean students have fixed learning styles or that instruction should be matched to them.

Learn more
  • Source: Hattie and Yates (2014, p. 115), Visible Learning and the Science of How We Learn. The same passage also says that we are all auditory learners.
  • Meaning: Learning from visuals is a shared human capacity, not a permanent student type.
  • Why it matters: Choose visuals when they make an idea clearer for learners, not to match a student to a learning-style label.

Why it survives, even among teachers who know better

Here’s the puzzle. Across 37 studies of more than 15,000 educators, about 89% said they believed in matching instruction to learning styles (Newton & Salvi, 2020). Many of those teachers had been taught otherwise. So why does the idea last?

Part of the answer is the elephant and the rider. The fast, emotional elephant goes where it wants, and the slow, reasoning rider mostly explains the trip afterward. Learning styles feels right. It matches what teachers see, because students do differ, and it sounds kind. Knowing the research lives with the rider. The habit lives with the elephant.

Learning styles also seems to work. A teacher who starts thinking about visual and kinesthetic learners usually starts using more diagrams, more movement, and more discussion. Some of those changes help, because a good diagram makes some ideas clearer for everyone. The framework gets credit for what good lesson design was doing anyway.

Where it hides

Learning styles rarely shows up by name anymore. It hides in everyday phrases:

  • “He’s a hands-on learner, so reading isn’t really his thing.”
  • A first-day survey that sorts students into visual, auditory, and kinesthetic groups
  • Choice boards labeled by style instead of by learning goal
  • Accommodations in an IEP that say “kinesthetic learner: provide hands-on instruction”

That last one matters most for future special educators. An accommodation should remove a documented barrier. A student with dyslexia might get audiobooks because decoding print is a barrier to reaching grade-level content. That’s an access accommodation. It isn’t matching a style, and it doesn’t replace teaching the student to read. A style label in an IEP sounds like support, but it can quietly lower what’s expected of a student.

The label can also hurt students directly. A student who decides “I’m not a reading person” may stop practicing the very skill that would help most (Newton & Salvi, 2020).

What to do instead

Match the method to the content and the goal, not to the student. Geography is best taught with maps. Pronunciation is best taught with sound. A fractions lesson may need a number line, and a persuasive essay needs examples of persuasive writing. Choose the representation that makes the idea clearest, and use it with the whole class.

Dual coding is for the whole class

Everyone can benefit from a useful visual. In that limited sense, everyone is a “visual learner.” That doesn’t make “visual learner” a fixed type, and it doesn’t mean every image helps. Dual coding pairs words with useful visuals as a design choice, not a prescription for a type of student. Reviews of multimedia research support pairing relevant words and graphics and placing related information close together (Noetel et al., 2022; Ginns, 2006). The point is to make the same idea easier to understand, not to decorate a slide or assign students a type.

Strip away the frameworks and something worth keeping remains: every student deserves to be seen as a person, not a profile. Not as a kinesthetic learner, an ENTJ, or a “creative type,” but as a person with a history, things they care about, a level of trust (or distrust) in school, a body of prior knowledge, and a particular moment in their development. That isn’t reducible to a category, and it doesn’t need one.

What it needs is the kind of attention good teachers have always brought to their work: noticing students, taking them seriously, and adjusting to what you actually see instead of what a framework predicts. The rest of this chapter replaces the frameworks that didn’t hold up with tools that do.

Go deeperMovement can help some tasks without defining a learner

In a virtual-reality experiment, participants learned visual categories using either button presses or large arm movements. Larger movements improved learning when a category required combining several cues, but added little when participants could judge features one at a time. The advantage remained when participants later responded with a button, which suggests the movement helped shape the learning itself, not just the response.

This is evidence about a specific task, not a rescue of learning styles. The study changed the action used for a task, not each learner’s preferred style. It also tested something different from dual coding, which pairs words with visuals. The design question is which activity fits the learning goal.

Source: Luke Rosedahl, Yuka Sasaki, and Takeo Watanabe (2026), “Learning by moving: Physical motion accelerates category learning,” Science Advances, 12(43), eaed4003. Read the study.

Apply: Does this study show that some students are “kinesthetic learners”? Explain the difference between a learner-matching claim and the effect tested here.

Answer: No. The study suggests that large movements can help with some tasks that require combining several cues. It changed the action used in the task. It didn’t test whether students benefit from matching instruction to a stable preferred style.

Retrieval practiceYou’re the special education teacher at an IEP meeting for a seventh grader who reads two years below grade level. His mother and his science teacher both say he’s “a hands-on learner.” They propose adding this accommodation: “Kinesthetic learner: provide hands-on instruction in all classes.” What would you say, and what would you propose instead?►

Start by agreeing with what’s true. He may enjoy hands-on work, and both adults clearly want him to succeed.

Then separate preference from need. Matching instruction to a learning style hasn’t been shown to improve learning, and a style label in an IEP can lower expectations for reading, the skill he most needs to build. Ask what the actual barrier is. Here, the data point to reading: he can’t yet read grade-level text on his own.

Propose accommodations tied to that barrier, such as audio versions of science texts so he can reach the content, and pre-taught key vocabulary. Pair them with direct reading instruction, so the accommodation opens access while his reading grows. Hands-on activities can still be used wherever they fit the science content, for every student in the class.

Major concept 2 · Start with strong teaching

Tier 1 Instruction

If labels like learning styles don’t tell a teacher what to do, the first answer is the least dramatic one: teach the whole class well. Before reaching for a specialized intervention, ask whether you’ve done the universal thing well.

Key aphorism

Good differentiated teaching starts with good teaching.

Schools usually organize support in three tiers, through systems called MTSS or RTI. Tier 1 is what every student gets, Tier 2 adds small-group help, and Tier 3 adds intensive, individual help. Tier 1 instruction is the high-quality, research-based teaching that every student in a general education classroom receives.

Tier 1 instruction works alongside Tier 1 supports, the things a teacher deliberately builds into the room to support learning, like routines, posted schedules, and written directions. The two are connected, but they aren’t the same. Instruction is the teaching. Supports are what make it easier for every student to take that teaching in.

1Universal instruction
High-quality, research-based teaching for all students. Common target: meets the needs of about 80% of students without additional support.
2Targeted support
Small-group help for students who need more than Tier 1 provides. Common target: about 10–15% of students.
3Intensive intervention
Individualized help for students with the most significant needs. Common target: about 3–5% of students.

The whole system depends on Tier 1 being strong. When it’s weak, many students look like they need extra help when what they really need is clearer teaching. When it’s strong, the students who truly need more stand out.

What strong Tier 1 looks like

PracticeWhat it means
Clear goalsStudents know what they’re learning and what success looks like, in words that give them a target.
Explicit instructionModeling, then guided practice, then independent practice, with worked examples and quick error correction
Frequent checksShort, low-stakes checks that show the teacher who has it and who doesn’t
Consistent routinesPredictable procedures, so students’ working memory goes to the content
Questions for everyoneRetrieval questions that every student answers, not just volunteers
Specific feedbackTelling students exactly what they got right and what to fix
Safe relationshipsA classroom where students are willing to try, be wrong, and try again

None of this requires knowing a student’s learning style. It requires knowing what the evidence says about teaching and doing it consistently.

These are whole-class practices, not supports saved for a labeled group. Clear models, purposeful diagrams, manageable steps, guided practice, and specific feedback help students across the class, including students with learning disabilities. For some students, they make the core lesson reachable. They don’t replace accommodations, IEP or 504 services, or specialized instruction. They’re the floor those supports stand on, and a strong floor makes it easier to see who needs more.

Room above, not only a net below

Tier systems are usually described from the bottom up: catch the students who fall behind. But strong Tier 1 also has room above grade level. A student who has already mastered today’s skill and gets twenty more problems of the same kind is mostly waiting.

The fix is the same idea in the other direction. A strong lesson plans for the students who finish early, with work that moves them to the next stage of the skill, not more of the same. The next concept describes those stages.

Universal Design for Learning

Universal Design for Learning (UDL), developed by the organization CAST, is a framework for planning lessons that remove unnecessary barriers for all learners from the start, instead of adding fixes later. It rests on three principles: more than one way to take in content, more than one way to show learning, and more than one way to stay engaged.

The key word is unnecessary. If a student can’t reach the content because of the format, the language, or the setup, and not because of the content itself, that’s a design problem. The barrier belongs to the lesson, not the learner.

StoryThe sidewalk was the barrier

In the early 1970s in Berkeley, California, disability activists worked to make the city’s sidewalks accessible. When official ramps were slow to come, some activists built rough asphalt ramps themselves. People who used wheelchairs helped find the barriers and plan the routes around them. A small change to a street corner opened a path that a curb had blocked.

The same design question belongs in a classroom: what is keeping this student from reaching the learning goal? Remove an unnecessary obstacle in the format, the materials, or the setting, and keep the thinking the lesson asks for. A change that helps everyone can widen access, alongside the individual supports some students still need.

Historical source: UC Berkeley, “A Place for Every Body.”. The illustration reconstructs the documented asphalt-ramp work. It is not an archival portrait.

UDL and cognitive load

UDL earns its place when it cuts extraneous load. If a student spends working memory decoding a format instead of thinking about the idea, removing that barrier is worth doing. UDL loses its value when it piles up options with no purpose. Choosing among options takes working memory too, especially for students who are new to the material. The test is always the same: does this remove a barrier that isn’t part of the learning goal, or does it just add noise?

Retrieval practiceA school puts UDL into practice by giving every student a choice of three formats for every assignment: written, visual, or oral. A colleague says this is “removing barriers.” Using cognitive load theory and the idea of extraneous load, what has the school gotten right, and where might it go wrong?►

What it gets right: UDL’s purpose is to remove barriers that aren’t part of the learning goal. If a student understands the content but can’t show it in writing because of a reading or writing disability, an oral option removes a barrier that had nothing to do with what’s being assessed. That’s a real reduction in extraneous load.

Where it might go wrong: Three choices on every assignment isn’t the same as removing barriers, and it can add new ones. Choosing takes working memory, especially for students new to the material. Students now have to decide how to show what they know before they start showing it. If the choice doesn’t remove a real barrier, that decision is just extra load.

The test is whether each option removes a barrier that isn’t part of the learning goal. If it does, it earns its place. If it doesn’t, it’s noise that only looks like inclusion.

Major concept 3 · The first difference to assess is readiness

Instructional Hierarchy

The most common difference between students is also the least dramatic. Some students haven’t yet learned what a lesson assumes. Others learned it long ago. A student who hasn’t mastered multiplication facts will struggle with algebra no matter how motivated they are. A student who mastered the unit’s skills over the summer will learn little from a unit spent practicing them.

Both are readiness gaps, differences between what a student already knows or can do and what a lesson assumes. Neither one is solved by labeling the student. Both are solved by finding out where the student is with the skill and teaching from there.

The Instructional Hierarchy, first described by Haring and Eaton (1978), describes where a student is with a particular skill and what kind of instruction fits that point. The wrong support at the wrong time does more than waste time. It can slow learning down. Drilling for speed with a student who hasn’t learned the skill frustrates without teaching. Step-by-step modeling for a student who’s ready to use the skill in new places holds them back.

StageWhat the student can doTeacher moves
Stage 1 · AcquisitionLearning the skill. Accuracy is the goal.Explicit instruction, modeling, worked examples, guided practice, immediate error correction.
Stage 2 · FluencyAccurate, but slow or effortful. Speed and automaticity are the goal.Repeated practice, immediate feedback, a gradually faster pace. Massed practice is appropriate here.
Stage 3 · Generalization and adaptationFluent, but needs to use the skill in new settings and on new kinds of problems.Vary contexts and materials, mix in related problems, ask when and why the skill applies, pose unfamiliar problems, have students explain their strategy.

Haring and Eaton split the third stage in two: generalization (using the skill in new settings) and adaptation (changing it to solve new problems). Many researchers who use the model today, including Amanda VanDerHeyden and Matthew Burns, combine them into one stage, and so does this course.

A stage belongs to a skill, not a student. The same student can be fluent with multiplication and still learning fractions.

How strong is the evidence?

The best-tested part of the hierarchy is the first step. Studies in reading and math find that students who are still making errors gain more from modeling and guided practice, while students who are accurate but slow gain more from repeated practice (Burns et al., 2010; Szadokierski et al., 2017). Building fluency also helps students keep a skill months later (VanDerHeyden & Burns, 2009). The third stage has much less research behind it, and most of the work comes from small studies of elementary reading and math. Treat the hierarchy as a guide for choosing instruction, not a law. Students also don’t need perfect fluency before they practice using a skill in new places. Mixing problem types helps them learn when a skill applies. [POINTER? The most commonly skipped stage

Schools often rush past Stage 2, fluency. Students learn a skill, show basic accuracy, and move on before the skill is automatic. The cost builds over time. A skill that never reaches fluency uses up working memory every time it’s used, which leaves less room for the harder thinking that depends on it. Reading fluency is the clearest example, but the pattern shows up in every subject. When a student who should be analyzing a story is still spending working memory on sounding out words, the bottleneck isn’t intelligence. It’s an unfinished earlier stage. [POINTER? Ch. 22, cognitive load: the current “Ch. 22 · Cognitive Load” chip.]

When a student is ahead

The hierarchy works in both directions. A student who is already fluent with a skill doesn’t need Stage 1 or Stage 2 teaching on it. They need Stage 3: new settings, harder problems, and questions about when and why the skill applies. Giving them more practice at a stage they’ve passed is the advanced student’s version of the same mismatch.

The research on moving advanced students forward is stronger than many teachers expect. A review of about a century of studies found clear academic gains from acceleration, which lets a student move through material faster than usual, such as skipping a grade or taking a subject with an older class (g = 0.42). Grouping advanced students together for instruction also helped (g = 0.37 for special grouping of gifted students), and so did grouping within a regular classroom (g = 0.19 to 0.30) (Steenbergen-Hu et al., 2016).

In a regular classroom, that can look like:

  • A pretest before a unit. Students who already show mastery skip the practice they don’t need.
  • Stage 3 work on the same skill: unfamiliar problems, real applications, or explaining a strategy to someone else
  • Acceleration in a single subject: taking math with an older class while staying with classmates for everything else

Advanced students also need to struggle sometimes. Effort that feels hard in the moment, the kind researchers call desirable difficulties, is part of how memories and skills grow strong. A student who coasts through school without ever being stretched misses that kind of growth. They may meet real difficulty for the first time much later, with no practice at handling it.

Background knowledge as a readiness gap

Readiness gaps aren’t only about skills. They’re also about knowledge a lesson assumes students already have. A word problem about a yacht means something different to a student who has seen one than to a student who hasn’t. A history lesson about “middle-class anxieties” assumes students know what the middle class is. A science unit about “household chemicals” assumes households look a certain way. None of these is a failure of intelligence. Each is a failure to account for the background knowledge the lesson was built around.

Background knowledge works the same way prior skill does in the hierarchy. New learning attaches to what students already know, and without that knowledge, new information has nothing to stick to. A student who has never met a concept isn’t behind. They need the instruction to start where they actually are.

English learners

Schools use many names for students who are learning English while being taught in English: English language learners (ELL), English learners (EL), English as a second language students (ESL), second language learners (SLL), multilingual learners, and emergent bilinguals. The names change by state, district, and decade, but they describe the same students. This book uses English learner: a student who is still developing English while being taught in English. Being an English learner is not a disability, and treating it as one is both wrong and harmful.

It’s a readiness difference in one tool: academic English. A student may understand a concept fully in their home language and still be unable to follow a lesson taught only in English, or to show what they know on an English-only test. That gap is real, and it calls for teaching, not lowered expectations.

Strong Tier 1 helps a great deal here: clear goals, visuals, consistent routines, explicit vocabulary teaching, and checking for understanding in more than one way. These lower the language barrier without lowering the thinking. Treat the home language as an asset. A student who thinks in two languages is doing something demanding that most of their teachers can’t. They aren’t behind. They’re working across a wider range of language than their classmates who speak only one.

One more point matters for the next concept. A student can be an English learner and have a disability. Mixing the two up causes harm in both directions. An English learner can be referred for special education they don’t need, and a student with a real disability can be told “it’s just the language” for years. Telling them apart takes evidence from the student’s home language, their progress over time, and specialists who know both.

A gospel lens: according to his several ability

In the parable of the talents, a man leaving on a journey trusts his servants with his goods: “unto one he gave five talents, to another two, and to another one; to every man according to his several ability” (Matthew 25:15). When he returns, the servant who had five has gained five more, and the servant who had two has gained two more. Each hears the same words: “Well done, thou good and faithful servant” (Matthew 25:21).

The parable is about stewardship, what we do with what God gives us, not about classroom instruction. But it names something this concept depends on. The servants started in different places, and each was expected to grow from where he started. A teacher who matches instruction to each student’s readiness, behind or ahead, asks every student the same question: what did you gain from what you were given? [DOCTRINE CHECK? Retrieval practiceA teacher is frustrated: “I’ve explained this three times and practiced with the whole class, but students still can’t do it on their own.” Using the Instructional Hierarchy, name two different things that might be happening. Then name the stage most often skipped and explain why skipping it matters.►

Possibility 1, a Stage 1 mismatch: Students haven’t fully learned the skill, but instruction has moved to independent practice as if they had. Explaining three times isn’t the same as Stage 1 teaching, which means modeling, worked examples, guided practice step by step, and quick error correction. The teacher may be skipping the gradual handoff that Stage 1 needs.

Possibility 2, a fluency gap: Students can do the skill accurately when prompted (Stage 1), but it isn’t automatic yet (Stage 2). Independent work puts a heavy load on working memory, and a skill that isn’t automatic falls apart under that load. Explaining again won’t fix a fluency gap. Repeated practice with quick feedback and a gradually faster pace will.

The most skipped stage is Stage 2, fluency. Skills that never become automatic keep using up working memory every time students use them. Every later skill built on that one carries the extra load, so the problem grows and gets harder to spot as the curriculum moves on.

Retrieval practiceA fourth grader finishes her math practice in five minutes, every time, with no errors. Her teacher has started giving her twenty more problems of the same kind when she’s done. She has begun rushing and making careless mistakes, and she says math is boring. Using the Instructional Hierarchy, explain what’s going on and what the teacher could do instead.►

She’s fluent with this skill, so she’s ready for Stage 3. Twenty more problems of the same kind are Stage 2 work, more fluency practice, for a student who has already reached fluency. Getting more of the same as a reward for finishing teaches her that careful work earns more busywork, which may explain the rushing.

Instead, the teacher could pretest at the start of each unit and let her skip practice she doesn’t need. When she finishes, she could get Stage 3 tasks on the same skill: unfamiliar word problems, a problem she has to explain to a classmate, or a real situation where she decides whether the skill applies. If she’s far ahead across the whole subject, the teacher could raise subject acceleration with the school.

Major concept 4 · Exceptional at either end, or both

Exceptional Learners

Some differences go beyond readiness. A disability may change how a student learns or reaches the curriculum. Giftedness may put a student so far ahead that ordinary classroom adjustments aren’t enough. Exceptional learners are students whose needs differ enough from their peers’ that they qualify for special services, including students with disabilities and students who are gifted.

The label opens a service. It doesn’t describe the need. That’s why the class in the opening story can’t be taught as one group. Every student in it carries the same label and a different plan: one for decoding, one for writing, one for spoken language, one for anxiety. Read the plan, not the label.

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act

Section 504 is a civil rights law. It prohibits discrimination against people with disabilities in any program that receives federal funding. In schools, it protects students whose disability substantially limits a major life activity, including learning. A student with a 504 plan may not need specialized instruction, but they do need accommodations, such as extended time on tests, preferential seating, a low-distraction setting, assistive technology, or printed copies of class notes. The 504 plan lists those accommodations and makes the school legally responsible for providing them.

Your role is to carry out the plan consistently and to document that you do. If something in the plan isn’t working or isn’t possible, take it to the 504 coordinator. Don’t decide on your own to skip it.

Individualized Education Programs (IEPs)

An IEP is a more intensive plan, governed by the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA). A student qualifies under one of 13 disability categories. Unlike a 504 plan, an IEP includes specialized instruction as well as accommodations. It may include a modified curriculum, related services like speech-language therapy or occupational therapy, and measurable annual goals. An IEP is written by a team that includes the student’s parents or guardians, a general education teacher, a special education teacher, and usually a school administrator.

Most students with IEPs spend most of the school day in general education classrooms. Strong Tier 1 instruction is the floor they stand on while they get more targeted support.

A note on scope

Disabilities and giftedness are real, important, and deserve serious study, and much of that study belongs in courses on special education law, gifted education, and specialized intervention. This concept gives you what a general education teacher needs: know the plan, carry it out, work with the team, and notice and refer without diagnosing. A book that tries to be everything to every reader tends to fall short for all of them.

Go deeperHow are learning disabilities identified?

For decades, schools identified a specific learning disability mainly by looking for a gap between a student’s IQ and their achievement. A student with an average IQ who read far below grade level might qualify. Critics pointed out that this often meant waiting for a student to fail badly enough to show a large gap. It also missed students whose IQ scores were pulled down by the same reading problem. In 2004, federal law changed. States can no longer require that gap, and they may use a student’s response to strong, research-based intervention instead (34 C.F.R. § 300.307). This is one reason strong Tier 1 and Tier 2 teaching matters so much. In many schools, it’s part of how disabilities are identified.

Gifted students

Gifted education isn’t covered by federal special education law, so each state sets its own rules. Idaho requires every school district to identify gifted and talented students and to provide for their special instructional needs (Idaho Code § 33-2003). Idaho’s education department describes gifted education as a required service, not a required program. It recognizes five areas of giftedness: intellectual, specific academic, creative, leadership, and visual and performing arts.

Requiring something doesn’t make it happen. In 2024, 45% of Idaho’s traditional school districts reported identifying zero gifted students (Idaho Department of Education, 2024). Across the country, many able students are never identified, especially when schools wait for referrals instead of screening everyone. For a classroom teacher, that means two things. Notice students who may be far ahead, even when they don’t look like the stereotype of a gifted kid. And know how your district identifies gifted students, so you can refer them.

Twice-exceptional students

Some students are both gifted and have a disability. These twice-exceptional students are easy to miss, because each label can hide the other. A gifted student with dyslexia may work hard enough to read at grade level, so both the disability and the giftedness go unnoticed. Or the disability gets all the attention, and nobody looks for the strengths. The exceptionally bright students in the opening story may be twice-exceptional. A twice-exceptional student may need an IEP and advanced work at the same time.

Retrieval practiceA student with a documented disability has a 504 plan with extended time and preferential seating, but no IEP. You think the student may need more. Walk through your role: what you should do, what you shouldn’t do, and what separates the two.►

What you should do: Carry out the 504 plan consistently and document that you’re doing it. Watch the student closely and write down specific, factual observations, not diagnoses. For example: “Over the past three weeks, the student had trouble starting tasks every class period, turned in below-grade-level writing on four assessments in a row, and explained ideas aloud that didn’t appear in the written work.” Bring these to your school’s student support team or counselor and ask whether a referral for evaluation makes sense.

What you shouldn’t do: Diagnose. Change the 504 plan on your own. Tell the family you think the student has ADHD, a learning disability, or any other condition. Skip accommodations because you think something else would help more. These go beyond your role and can have legal consequences.

What separates them: Your role is to observe, document, and refer. Specialists, the family, and the evaluation process decide what the student needs. Your observations are an important part of that process, but they aren’t the process. Notice and refer. Carry out the plan and document it.

Retrieval practiceAn eighth grader has an IEP for a specific learning disability in written expression. In class discussions, she makes connections nobody else sees, and her science fair project won the district competition. Her teachers mostly talk about her missing assignments. What might be happening, and what would you do?►

She may be twice-exceptional. Her disability in writing is real and documented, and it may be hiding strong ability that shows up when writing isn’t required, like in discussion and hands-on projects.

I would keep carrying out her IEP accommodations for writing, and document what I see: specific examples of her advanced reasoning, along with her work samples. Then I’d bring it to her IEP team and ask whether she has been screened for gifted services. She may need support for writing and more challenge in content at the same time. One doesn’t cancel the other.

Major concept 5 · Interests, culture, and experience

Contextual Differences

Beyond readiness and exceptionality is a large and important set of ways students differ that don’t sort neatly: their interests, the cultures they live in, the experiences they bring, what they find worth caring about, and what they’ve learned to distrust. These differences are real, they affect learning, and they don’t fit in a box.

Interest as the most actionable difference

The individual difference a teacher can most directly work with is interest: what a student cares about, what they find worth thinking about, and what they’d choose to read if given the choice. Personality types and learning styles don’t belong on that list. Interest does.

Interest matters for a clear reason. People think more deeply about information that connects to something they already care about. Their prior knowledge comes to mind more easily, and their attention lasts longer. When students care about a topic, more of their limited working memory goes to the content instead of to distraction. And because interest brings prior knowledge to mind, it gives new information something to attach to.

Interest in practice

This doesn’t mean every lesson has to be personalized to thirty different interests. That’s neither possible nor necessary. It means a teacher who knows their students can make small choices that change how much students engage:

  • A math problem set in a context students recognize works better than one set somewhere unfamiliar, with everything else held the same.
  • A writing prompt that leaves room for a personal connection tends to produce better work.
  • An example drawn from something students care about right now lands differently than one from a world they have no connection to.

These are small choices, but their effects add up. They don’t require a learning-styles theory. They require knowing what students care about.

Cultural context and background knowledge

Students don’t arrive as blank slates. They arrive as people, with histories that shape what they already know, what questions they’re ready to ask, and what they find obvious or surprising. Culture is part of this. A student whose family runs a small business brings real-world economic sense to a lesson on supply and demand. A student who moves between two languages every day has an awareness of language that is a real asset, and a student’s home language is part of this context too, something to build on, not a gap to fix. A student who carries a lot of responsibility at home may have a kind of practical intelligence that never shows up on a standardized test. A learning-styles inventory captures none of this. Attention does.

Categorical vs. relational knowing

The alternative to labels that don’t work isn’t ignoring differences. It’s a better kind of attention. Teachers are sometimes offered a false choice between elaborate sorting systems and treating everyone the same. The real choice is between categorical knowing, which sorts students into types, and relational knowing, which understands this student, in this class, at this point in their life.

Relational knowing can’t be turned into a checklist. It builds up through the everyday work of paying attention: noticing what lights students up and what shuts them down, what they bring that the curriculum hasn’t accounted for, and where they are with the skills you’re teaching. It means treating every student as a person who is more than any category you could put them in. Whatever helps you give students that kind of attention is worth holding onto. The research supports it, and so does something more basic: it’s what treating people with dignity looks like in a classroom.

President Boyd K. Packer put it this way: “The superb teacher also studies the students—studies them seriously and intently” (“Study Your Students,” 1963, p. 17, quoted in a Church teaching manual). The Church manual that reprints the line connects close observation of students with compassion in teaching. Here it names the practical alternative to learner profiles: notice the actual person, their readiness, and what they bring to class.

Retrieval practiceWhy is interest described as the most actionable individual difference for classroom teachers? Connect your answer to at least two ideas from the cognitive science in this course.►

Why it’s actionable: Teachers can discover interest, respond to it, and build on it through ordinary attention to their students. It doesn’t take a diagnostic test, a special program, or a referral. It takes knowing students and making small choices about contexts, examples, and prompts.

Idea 1, prior knowledge: When a student cares about a topic, what they already know about it comes to mind more easily, so new information has more to attach to. New learning sticks to what students already know.

Idea 2, working memory and attention: Interest means more of a student’s limited working memory goes to the content and less to distraction. A task that holds a student’s interest leaves less attention free to wander, and it pushes them to think hard about the material. And since “memory is the residue of thought” (Willingham, 2009), what students think hard about is what they remember. Interest doesn’t make hard work easy. It makes the hard work more likely to happen.

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

The question of which sorting system to use matters less than a simpler one: what do I actually know about the students in front of me, and is my teaching meeting them where they are?

  • Look past the label to the need. Two students with the same label can need completely different things. Read the plan, look at the work, and ask what’s actually in the way.
  • Retire learning styles, including the disguised versions. Match the method to the content and the goal, not to a student “type.” In an IEP, tie each accommodation to a documented barrier.
  • Teach the whole class well first. Strong Tier 1 instruction and supports help most students and make it clear who needs more.
  • Find each student’s stage, in both directions. Back up for a student who’s behind. Move a student who’s ahead to harder work on the same skill, not more of the same.
  • Treat fluency as non-negotiable, not a bonus. Skills that never become automatic keep using up working memory for as long as students use them. Build fluency practice into the sequence before moving on.
  • Plan for the students who finish early. Pretest, skip what they’ve already mastered, and have Stage 3 work ready.
  • Don’t confuse language with disability. Build English and keep high expectations for English learners, and remember that a student can be both an English learner and have a disability.
  • Know your plans and your district’s gifted process. Carry out IEPs and 504 plans faithfully. If something isn’t working, bring it to the team. Notice and refer students who may be gifted, including students who don’t fit the stereotype.
  • Study your students. Learn what they care about and use it to make small, steady choices about examples and contexts.
Chapter synthesis
Every student is unique, and most of the frameworks built to capture that uniqueness don’t work. The evidence is clear enough that continuing to use them is a choice, not a default. Learning styles has been tested and not supported, yet nearly 9 in 10 educators still believe in it. What replaces it is better attention to the differences that do matter. Some students are behind with a skill and some are ahead, and the Instructional Hierarchy tells a teacher what each one needs next. Some students are exceptional, at either end or both, and their plans describe needs a label can’t. All students bring interests, cultures, and languages a teacher can build on. Strong Tier 1 teaching is the floor all of this stands on.
Learning styles is a label, not a need. Matching instruction to styles doesn’t improve learning. Matching it to the content does, and accommodations should match documented barriers.
Strong Tier 1 serves almost everyone, with a net below and room above.
Readiness runs both directions. Find the student’s stage with the skill and teach the next one. Acceleration has strong evidence behind it.
Exceptional learners need their plans, not just their labels. That includes students with disabilities, gifted students, and students who are both.
Context is professional knowledge. Knowing what students care about and where they come from changes what they learn.
