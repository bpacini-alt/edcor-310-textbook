Statistical Significance

Signal or Noise? Innocent or Guilty?

Chapter 5~ 35 min read

Imagine you’re on a jury. The prosecutor stands up and tells you that the defendant was seen near the scene of the crime. That a witness heard arguing earlier that evening. That the defendant had motive. None of these facts, individually, prove anything — but together, she argues, they paint a picture. The defense attorney is equally persuasive. By the time closing arguments are over, you aren’t sure what to believe.

Here is the question the jury must answer: not “is this person a bad person?” Not “do I like the prosecutor or the defense attorney more?” Not even “is it possible that this person did it?” The question is precisely this: is there enough evidence to say, beyond a reasonable doubt, that the null hypothesis — innocent — should be rejected?

That framing is not a metaphor borrowed from law. It is, almost exactly, how hypothesis testing works. Every time a researcher runs a study, they are convening a kind of trial. The null hypothesis sits in the defendant’s chair: the default assumption that nothing is happening, that the intervention didn’t work, that the difference between groups is just noise. The researcher’s job — your job, when you read research — is to evaluate the evidence and decide: is there enough here to convict? Or should we extend the benefit of the doubt?

This chapter is about learning to read that trial. Not to run the statistics yourself — but to understand what the numbers mean, what questions to ask, and when a verdict is earned versus when someone has put an innocent variable behind bars.

The big picture
Chapters 3 and 4 established that evidence is not all equal — and that human intuition, left unchecked, is a poor judge of what works. Statistical significance is the formal machinery for answering a single question: is this result real, or is it noise? Chapter 5 teaches you what that question means, how researchers answer it, and how to read their answer intelligently. Chapter 6 will teach you the follow-up question that matters just as much: if it’s real — how big a deal is it?

Major Concept 1 · Preparing for Trial

Hypothesis Testing

Before a single piece of evidence is introduced in a courtroom, both sides have to state their positions. The prosecution makes a claim. The defense asserts the presumption of innocence. The rules of the trial — what counts as sufficient evidence, what standard must be met — are established in advance, not after the verdict. Statistical hypothesis testing follows exactly this logic.

Every study begins with two competing claims. The null hypothesis (written H₀) is the position of no effect — nothing is happening, the intervention didn’t work, any difference you see is just random variation. In the courtroom analogy, the null hypothesis is that the defendant is innocent: what we begin by believing. The alternative hypothesis (H₁ or Hₐ) is most often what the researcher actually believes and is trying to demonstrate: that there is a real effect, a real difference, a real relationship. In the courtroom, this is the idea that the defendant is guilty. The null hypothesis gets the benefit of the doubt. The researcher’s job is to build a case against it strong enough to get the jury to justify changing their minds.

Consider a simple example. A researcher wants to know whether a new vocabulary program improves reading comprehension scores. She sets up her hypotheses before collecting any data:

Null Hypothesis (H₀)
The vocabulary program has no effect on reading comprehension scores. Any observed difference between groups is due to random chance.
Alternative Hypothesis (H₁)
The vocabulary program does improve reading comprehension scores. The difference between groups is real, not random.
What the test decides
Whether the data is unusual enough — given the null — to justify rejecting H₀ and concluding H₁. Not which hypothesis is “true,” but which the evidence supports.

Notice something important: the test never proves the alternative hypothesis. It only evaluates whether the evidence is strong enough to reject the null. This is the same logic as a jury verdict. “Not guilty” does not mean innocent — it means the prosecution did not meet its burden of proof. “Statistically significant” does not mean definitely true — it means the evidence cleared the threshold. Keeping this distinction clear will save you from some of the most common misreadings of research you will encounter in your career.

The threshold of evidence — how clear the evidence needs to be before you change your mind — is set in advance by the researcher using a value called alpha (α). The conventional choice is α = .05. Alpha is sometimes called the level of significance, and it tells you how unusual the evidence must be before we reject the null.

As an example, a statistics professor friend of mine likes to flip a coin at the start of class. He tells students to raise their hand if they think something is wrong with the coin. First flip: heads. 50% chance, nothing unusual. Second flip: heads again. 25% chance, nothing crazy. Third flip: heads again, a 12.5% chance; a little unusual, but nothing nutsy. Fourth flip: 6.25% chance; my scalp starts to itch, the hair on my neck stands up, and I really think something is off, but I don't change anything yet. Interestingly, most students raise their hands at the fifth heads in a row: a 3.125% chance. (Students are then happy to learn that the coin is two-headed!)

Alpha tells you how unusual the evidence needs to be to get you to change your mind. Changing your mind, in statistics, is called rejecting the null. Alpha is like the standard of evidence in a courtroom: beyond a reasonable doubt for a murder case, but the preponderance of the evidence for a civil lawsuit.

Notice the tradeoff here: you can set alpha lower (say, .01) and you will get fewer convictions; more guilty people will go free. Setting alpha higher (say .10) makes it easier to find significance — but also easier to convict the innocent.

Worked exampleSetting up hypotheses for a study

A researcher wants to know whether screen time in the hour before bed reduces sleep quality in adolescents. Before collecting any data, she must state her two competing claims:

Step 1State the null hypothesis (H₀). Screen time before bed has no effect on sleep quality. Any observed difference between adolescents who use screens and those who do not is due to random variation.
Step 2State the alternative hypothesis (H₁). Screen time before bed does reduce sleep quality. The difference between the two groups is real, not a product of chance.
Step 3Notice the logic. The null is the skeptical, default position — “nothing interesting is happening here.” The alternative is what the researcher hopes to demonstrate. The study is designed to ask: is the evidence strong enough to reject the null?

Major Concept 2 · Court Is in Order

Standard Deviation, Bell Curves, and Noise

Imagine that a defendant is at the scene of a fight. There is confusion over the identity of the attack. He protests: "I was in the wrong place at the wrong time!" A magical statistics computer program runs the numbers and finds that there is a 20% likelihood that an innocent person could have been there without being guilty. Then, the prosecution provides evidence that the defendant knew the victim. The computer revises the estimate: now there's only a 10% chance of innocence. The prosecution adds more: the defendant really didn't like the victim. Now, there's only a 6% chance. Finally, the prosecution adds a final piece of the puzzle: the defendant had sent intimidating texts threatening violence. The statistician finally revises: there's a chance of innocence, but it's less than 1%.

Statistics can help us get to truth, but it doesn't tell us what is true and what is not. It uses probability to tell us where the evidence stacks up. The defendant above might still be innocent. The only thing the statistician can say is that the evidence would be very rare if the defendant were innocent. In statistical terms, that is the p-value question: assuming the null is true, how unlikely is evidence this extreme?

But what does unlikely actually mean? A jury doesn't convict because the evidence feels suspicious; it convicts because the evidence falls outside what we'd reasonably expect if the defendant were innocent. Statistics works the same way: to know whether a result is surprising, you need a model of what the world looks like when nothing interesting is happening.

That model begins with spread. In fourth grade, you likely heard a good deal about measures of center: mean, median, and mode. Those are important: averages tell you where the middle of the data is, and it's a major tool. But your fourth grade teacher likely focused a lot less onspread; spread tells you how much the data vary around a middle. As a simple example, two classes can both average 80 on a test and still look very different. In one class, nearly everyone scores between 77 and 83. In another, some students score in the 50s while others score in the high 90s. Same average, very different story.

The most important measure of spread is standard deviation. Standard deviation asks, roughly: how much does this set of data vary from the mean? A small standard deviation means scores cluster tightly near the mean. A large standard deviation means they spread widely. In plain language, standard deviation tells us what counts as a normal amount of difference in a particular setting.

You do not need to calculate standard deviations by hand in this class, but it helps to know what the calculation is trying to capture. Imagine five students score 70, 75, 80, 85, and 90. Their average is 80. Each score has a distance from 80: one student is 10 points below, one is 5 below, one is exactly average, one is 5 above, and one is 10 above. Standard deviation is built from those distances. It turns "spread of scores" into a usable ruler.

That ruler is easiest to feel with ordinary human examples. Someone one standard deviation taller than average is noticeably tall, but not shocking; someone one standard deviation shorter is noticeably short, but not shocking either. The number tells you distance from average, and the sign tells you direction: positive is above the mean, negative is below it. Two standard deviations in either direction is unusual. Five standard deviations is extreme enough that you should start checking context, measurement, or whether the case belongs to a different population. The same logic applies to test scores, reading fluency, attention ratings, or any other measurement.

The next tool to discuss is the probability distribution: a description of all the possible outcomes in a situation and how likely each one is. There are a variety of different distributions: a flip of a coin only has two possibilities, and they are equally likely. Some distributions have a bias: GPAs tend to cluster nearer to the top end, with a long spread at the bottom end. One of the most common distributions is the standard normal distribution, sometimes called the bell curve. It earns its name because when you measure many naturally occurring things (heights of adult men, IQ scores, measurement errors in a physics lab, reading scores in a large population) the values tend to cluster near the middle and taper off symmetrically toward the extremes. The shape that emerges, reliably and across wildly different phenomena, is the bell.

A histogram is the practical tool for seeing that distribution. When you plot how many students scored 60-69, 70-79, 80-89, and so on, you are building a histogram. With enough scores, many educational measures begin to form a familiar shape: high in the middle, thinner at the edges.

A histogram begins to approximate the true underlying population distribution as sample size increases. The bars do not magically become a bell curve; they become a clearer picture of whatever shape is actually underneath.

Intuition builderWhy bigger samples look smoother▸

This is the law of large numbers. An example might help: if you test a tall-potion on one unusually tall friend, the sample looks impressive for a terrible reason: your sample does not represent the population. With a larger, more representative sample, that one unusual person has less power to fool you.

With enough observations, random fluctuations average out and the distribution stabilizes. This is why a study with 8 participants is fundamentally less trustworthy than one with 800: with 8 participants, one unusual individual can tip the whole result. With 800, the noise cancels itself out and the signal has room to show up. Sample size is, therefore, a crucial part of doing good statistics.

Figure 5.1.The normal distribution — the idealized bell curve — is the smooth model that a large histogram of normally distributed data begins to approximate. Most scores cluster near the mean; extreme scores in either tail become progressively rarer. The bell shape is what makes the probability calculations of hypothesis testing possible.

The bell curve has a mathematical structure worth knowing. About 68% of scores fall within one standard deviation of the mean, about 95% within two, and about 99.7% within three. This is why the standard-deviation ruler matters: it lets us distinguish ordinary variation from evidence that sits far enough out in the tail to be genuinely surprising. (Statisticians, always being clever marketers, have named this the "68, 95, 99.7 rule.")

How does this connect with hypothesis testing? A p-value is built from this question: if the defendant were innocent, how unusual would this evidence be? In terms of testing educational interventions, our p-value question would be "if this intervention does nothing, how unlikely would this result be?" To answer that, we need a picture of what nothing happening usually produces. The bell curve gives us that picture. Results near the middle are ordinary. Results deep in the tails are rare. Statistical significance begins when the evidence is rare enough, under the null hypothesis, that we are willing to reject the null.

Worth knowing

Not everything is normally distributed, and researchers have to check. Some phenomena are skewed — household income, for example, has a long right tail because a few very wealthy people pull the average up. When data isn’t normally distributed, different statistical approaches are required. The bell curve is a useful introductory model.

Retrieval practiceA study finds that a new tutoring program improved test scores. The study used 9 students. Why should you be skeptical of this finding, even if the results look impressive?►

With only 9 students, a single outlier — one exceptionally motivated student, one student who happened to have a good week — can dramatically shift the results. The law of large numbers tells us that random variation averages out only as sample size increases. With 9 participants, you don’t have enough data for that averaging to happen reliably.

The practical consequence: small samples produce unstable estimates. The same study run with a different 9 students might produce a completely different result. A finding that won’t replicate isn’t really a finding — it’s noise that happened to look like a signal one time. This is one reason the replication crisis hit studies with small samples particularly hard.

Major Concept 3 · Reaching a Verdict

P-values & Statistical Significance

The trial is underway. The evidence has been presented. Now the jury has to decide: is this enough? In statistics, that decision is formalized through the aforementioned p-value.

The p-value answers a very specific question: if the null hypothesis were true, how likely would it be to see results this extreme just by chance? A p-value of .03 means there’s a 3% chance of getting data this unusual in a world where the null is true. A p-value of .72 means this kind of result would be completely ordinary even if nothing were happening at all.

Worked exampleReading a p-value from a real study

A study tests whether a 10-minute mindfulness exercise before a math test improves scores. Here is what the results section says:

“Students in the mindfulness condition scored an average of 4.2 points higher than the control group (p = .03, α = .05).”

How do you read this?

Step 1Identify the claim. The mindfulness exercise improved scores by 4.2 points on average.
Step 2Find alpha. α = .05. This is the threshold set in advance. To reject the null, p must be below .05.
Step 3Compare p to alpha. p = .03. Since .03 < .05, the result clears the threshold. The researchers reject the null hypothesis.
Step 4State the verdict correctly. The result is statistically significant — meaning it is unlikely to be due to chance alone. This does not mean the mindfulness exercise definitely works for all students, or that 4.2 points is a big deal. That’s Chapter 6’s question.

The verdict comes from comparing the p-value to alpha. Alpha must be decided before the evidence is gathered, because if we find evidence that is less likely than our pre-determined level of significance, we are obligated to change our minds about the issue. If p < α, the evidence is unusual enough to reject the null — the result is statistically significant. If p ≥ α, we fail to reject the null. Not “the null is true” — just “we don’t have enough evidence against it.” The jury hangs. The defendant goes free — for now.

Common misconception“p = .05 means there’s a 5% chance the result is wrong.”►

This is the single most common misinterpretation of the p-value, and it’s wrong in a specific way. The p-value is calculated assuming the null hypothesis is true. It tells you how often you’d see data this extreme in a world where nothing is happening. It does not tell you the probability that your result is a fluke.

The practical upshot: “p < .05” means “this would be unusual noise.” It does not mean “this is definitely real.” Statistical significance is a threshold, not a certificate. It needs to be combined with replication, effect size, and theoretical plausibility before it earns strong confidence.

Major Concept 4 · When the Verdict Is Wrong

Error Types

No system of justice gets every verdict right. Even with the best procedures and the most careful jury, two kinds of mistakes are possible: convicting an innocent person, and acquitting a guilty one. But you cannot drive both to zero at once — every trial, and every study, accepts that it will sometimes be wrong. The only real choice is which way. Criminal courts made theirs long ago: better that a guilty person go free than an innocent one be convicted. “Beyond a reasonable doubt” is a deliberately punishing standard, set high to keep the innocent out of prison even at the cost of letting some guilty walk. That preference has a name you already know — alpha. Alpha is the false-conviction rate you agree to live with: set it low and you demand overwhelming evidence before you’ll reject a true null, sparing the innocent but letting more real effects go unproven. It was never a technical default someone handed you; it’s a judgment about which mistake you’d least like to make. The statistical system has exactly the same two failure modes, and they have names: Type I error and Type II error.

A Type I error is a false positive — the jury convicts the innocent. In statistical terms: the researcher rejects the null hypothesis when it is actually true. The study concludes that an effect exists when it doesn’t. With α = .05, the researcher has accepted a 5% chance of this error every time they run a test. That seems small — but when thousands of studies are run every year, a 5% false positive rate adds up to a substantial number of wrong verdicts in the published literature.

A Type II error is a false negative — the guilty party walks free. In statistical terms: the researcher fails to reject the null hypothesis when there really is an effect. The study misses something real. Type II errors are often caused by insufficient sample size. When a study doesn’t have enough participants, real but modest effects don’t produce enough signal to clear the significance threshold — the evidence is there, but too quiet to hear above the noise.

Mechanically, the two errors trade off: lowering alpha (a stricter conviction threshold) reduces Type I errors but raises Type II errors, and raising alpha does the reverse. No setting eliminates both — which is exactly why, as above, choosing alpha is a judgment about which mistake is costlier, not a formula.

Type I Error (False Positive)
Concluding an effect exists when it doesn’t. Wrongful conviction. Controlled by alpha — with α = .05, the Type I error rate is 5%. The cost: adopting an intervention that doesn’t work.
Type II Error (False Negative)
Missing a real effect. The guilty party goes free. Often caused by small samples. The cost: abandoning an intervention that would have helped students.
The Tradeoff
Reducing one type of error tends to increase the other. The chosen alpha level reflects a judgment about which mistake is more costly in a given context.

For educators, both errors have real costs. A Type I error means you adopt a curriculum, an intervention, or a practice that doesn’t actually work — spending money, time, and professional energy on something that isn’t helping students. A Type II error means you dismiss something that would have helped. Neither is consequence-free, which is why smart consumers of research don’t just ask “was it significant?” but also “how well-powered was this study?” and “has it been replicated?”

Retrieval practiceA pharmaceutical company runs a trial for a new medication. The drug actually does nothing — but due to random chance, the trial produces p = .04 and the drug is approved. What type of error is this, and what are the real-world consequences?►

This is aType I error— a false positive. The null hypothesis (the drug has no effect) was true, but the evidence happened to look significant by chance. With α = .05, this is expected to happen 5% of the time even when the null is completely correct.

The consequences: patients take a medication that doesn’t work. Insurance systems pay for it. Doctors recommend it. If the drug has side effects, patients are being harmed by something that offers no benefit. The cost of a Type I error in medicine is one reason clinical trials require replication, and why drugs for serious conditions often use α = .01 rather than .05 — a stricter standard that reduces false positives at the cost of occasionally missing real effects.

For education, the parallel is direct: a curriculum approved on the basis of a single underpowered study that happened to produce a significant result could be adopted district-wide, diverting resources from approaches that actually work.

Retrieval practiceA small study of 12 students finds that a new phonics intervention produces gains, but p = .09. The researcher concludes the program doesn’t work. What type of error might this be, and why?►

This is a potentialType II error— a false negative. The intervention might genuinely work, but the study didn’t have enough participants to detect the effect clearly. With only 12 students, random variation is large relative to any real signal. The result of p = .09 doesn’t clear the α = .05 threshold — but that doesn’t mean nothing is there. It means the study lacked the statistical power to find it if it is.

The practical lesson: when a study with a small sample fails to find significance, the correct conclusion is not “the intervention doesn’t work.” The correct conclusion is “this study wasn’t able to detect whether the intervention works.” The difference matters enormously when deciding whether to pursue further research — or abandon an approach that might be genuinely valuable.

Major Concept 5 · Jury Tampering

The Replication Crisis

An appeal assumes the trial was run fairly — that the evidence presented was the evidence collected, and that the verdict reflects what actually happened in the courtroom. In 2015, the Open Science Collaboration tested that assumption by retrying 100 psychology studies. Ninety-seven of the original 100 had returned a guilty verdict the first time — the effect is real, p < .05. When those cases were retried — same procedures, same materials, larger juries — only about a third of them reached the same verdict again. Most of the original convictions did not hold up on appeal.

Some of that gap is ordinary bad luck — a 5% false-positive rate compounds over thousands of studies. But a large share is explained by something less innocent: p-hacking. A researcher who tries several statistical tests, several ways of defining the outcome, or several subgroups of the data — and reports only the analysis that crossed p < .05 — isn’t running one trial and reading the verdict. They’re running the same case in front of a dozen different juries and publishing only the one that convicted. Each individual analysis can be technically legitimate. Run enough of them and something will cross the significance threshold by chance alone, the same way rolling a die enough times eventually lands on six.

The journals made this worse before anyone tried to fix it. A study that finds an effect is more publishable than a study that finds nothing — editors want interesting results, and “we found nothing” rarely feels interesting. The result is the file-drawer problem: null results get quietly filed away while positive ones get published, so the literature you can actually read is systematically tilted toward false positives, even when no individual researcher did anything wrong. Add novelty bias — journals favor new findings over replications of old ones — and you get a system with every incentive to generate exciting new verdicts and almost no incentive to double-check the ones already on the books.

None of this means the underlying research enterprise is broken beyond use — it means the appeals process had to be built deliberately rather than assumed. Preregistration and mandatory large-scale replication are now standard practice at serious journals specifically because of findings like the 2015 study. Science’s advantage over most human institutions was never that individual scientists are unbiased. It’s that the system is built to catch bias eventually, if you insist that it does.

Evidence checkFive questions to ask before you trust a study’s verdict
1What was the null hypothesis, and did the evidence actually justify rejecting it? “Promising” and “suggestive” often mean it didn’t.
2How big was the sample? A significant result from 12 people is a different kind of evidence than one from 1,200.
3How far below alpha did the p-value land? p = .048 and p = .001 are not the same kind of confident.
4Has it been independently replicated — not just cited, actually rerun by a different team?
5Was this the only analysis run, or one of several the researcher could have reported?

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

Reading research is a professional skill. These tools let you evaluate whether a study’s verdict was earned — and whether the right questions were asked before it was rendered.

  • Before you trust a study’s verdict, run it through the Evidence Check above — the null hypothesis, the sample size, and how comfortably the p-value cleared alpha are the first three things worth checking, every time.
  • When a study fails to find an effect, ask whether it was well-powered. A Type II error is just as costly as a Type I one — abandoning an effective intervention because a small study couldn’t detect it is a real loss for students.
  • A single significant result — even a well-powered one — is a first data point, not a settled verdict. Check whether it’s held up under independent replication, and whether the analysis you’re reading was the only one run. The Evidence Check covers both.
  • Statistical significance is the first question, not the last. Chapter 6 asks the one that matters more: even if it’s real, how big is it?
Chapter synthesis
Every study is a trial. The null hypothesis is the defendant. Alpha is the standard of proof. The p-value is the evidence. Statistical significance is the verdict. And like any verdict, it can be wrong — wrongful convictions happen at rate alpha, and acquittals of the guilty happen when studies are too small to hear the signal above the noise. Learning to read the trial does not make you a statistician. It makes you a scholar-practitioner who can evaluate whether a verdict was earned — and whether it’s time to call for an appeal.
Hypothesis testing is the formal procedure for deciding whether a result is real or noise. The null hypothesis — that nothing is happening — gets the benefit of the doubt until the evidence clears the threshold.
The bell curve and the law of large numbers explain why statistics works: with enough data, random variation averages out and the signal becomes visible. Small samples are noisy; their verdicts are unreliable.
A p-value below alpha means the result is statistically significant — unlikely to be chance. It does not mean the result is large, important, or definitively true. That question belongs to Chapter 6.
Type I error convicts the innocent; Type II error acquits the guilty. Both have real costs in educational decision-making. The best protection against both is replication — verdicts that hold up across multiple independent trials.
Most appeals succeed: when 97 significant psychology findings were independently retried, only about a third held up. P-hacking and the file-drawer problem are the main reasons why. Preregistration and mandatory replication are the profession’s answer.
