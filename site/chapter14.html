Unit 3 · The Developing Learner

Identity and Belonging

He Who Loseth His Life

Chapter 14~ 45 min read

The question arrives sometime in middle school and doesn’t leave. It takes different forms —What do I believe? Where do I belong? What am I becoming?— but it is always the same question underneath: Who am I? And it is almost always asked most urgently in precisely the years your students spend inside your building.

Erik Erikson spent his career documenting that this is not a distraction from the work of education. It is one of its deepest purposes. Stage 5 of his eight-stage framework — Identity vs. Role Confusion — sits at the hinge of a person’s development: everything before it is foundation, everything after it is application. What happens during the adolescent years shapes who a person will be capable of becoming.

Teachers are inside that process. Not as bystanders — as participants. James Marcia mapped the positions a student can occupy in the search: genuinely exploring, prematurely settled, simply adrift. Charles Cooley showed that other people are mirrors in which students see themselves forming. That mirror includes you. Claude Steele showed that the stereotypes a student carries about their group follow them through your door every day — and tax the resources they need to learn.

This chapter gives you the frameworks to understand what your students are navigating. What you do with those frameworks — in this building, with these students, on this particular Monday morning — is the work it points toward.

The big picture
Who am I? It is the oldest question in human experience — and one that students are most urgently asking during precisely the years they spend in your classroom. Identity formation is not a distraction from education; it is one of its deepest purposes. This chapter explores how identity develops through Erikson’s stages and Marcia’s statuses, how the people around us become mirrors for our self-concept, how social dimensions like race and gender are real but not the substance of who anyone is, and what it means to find a self by giving that self to something larger. Understanding these processes is not optional for teachers — it is the foundation of building classrooms where real people can really grow.

Major concept 1 · Who am I becoming?

Erikson’s Psychosocial Stages

Erik Erikson proposed that identity is built across a lifetime through eight sequential psychosocial crises — each one a tension between the individual’s inner needs and the demands of the surrounding social world. Successfully navigating each stage produces a psychological virtue that becomes a resource for the next challenge. Unresolved tensions don’t disappear; they resurface.

The full map is worth knowing, but teachers are responsible for a specific stretch of it. The stages below are color-coded: gray stages are background knowledge; the two green stages are where your students are living right now.

Background — before your students arrive
Trust vs. MistrustBirth – 18 months
Can I trust the world? Responsive care builds safety; neglect builds mistrust. Virtue: Hope.
Autonomy vs. Shame18 months – 3 years
Can I do things on my own? Supported independence builds will; excessive control builds shame. Virtue: Will.
Initiative vs. Guilt3 – 5 years
Is it okay to want things and pursue them? Encouragement builds purpose; punishment of initiative builds guilt. Virtue: Purpose.
Your classroom — these are your stages
Industry vs. Inferiority5 – 12 years
Am I capable? Children begin measuring themselves against peers. Success builds competence; persistent failure or negative comparison produces a foundational sense of inferiority that echoes into every subsequent stage. Virtue: Competence.
Identity vs. Role Confusion12 – 18 years
Who am I? The central stage for secondary educators. Adolescents explore values, beliefs, roles, and relationships to forge a coherent sense of self. Successful resolution produces stable identity and fidelity. Failure produces role confusion. Virtue: Fidelity.
After your students leave
Intimacy vs. Isolation18 – 40 years
Can I love and be loved? A stable Stage 5 identity makes genuine intimacy possible. Virtue: Love.
Generativity vs. Stagnation40 – 65 years
Am I contributing something that will outlast me? Virtue: Care.
Ego Integrity vs. Despair65+ years
Did my life have meaning? Virtue: Wisdom.
The stakes of stages 4 and 5: A child who leaves elementary school feeling fundamentally incompetent — “I’m just not smart” — carries that wound directly into adolescent identity formation. A teenager who never resolves the question of who they are carries that confusion into adult relationships and careers. What you do in your classroom is not separate from these developmental tasks. It is part of them.
Retrieval practiceA 9-year-old consistently tells you “I’m just not good at school.” According to Erikson, what stage is this child navigating — and what is the developmental risk if this belief is not addressed?►

Stage 4 — Industry vs. Inferiority. This is the period when children first begin measuring themselves against peers and developing a sense of their own competence. A persistent belief in incompetence at this stage produces what Erikson called a foundational sense of inferiority — not just a bad feeling, but a settled conclusion about what kind of person one is and what one is capable of.

The developmental risk: that conclusion doesn’t stay in Stage 4. It travels directly into Stage 5. A teenager who enters adolescent identity formation already believing themselves incompetent has a fundamentally different platform from which to ask “who am I?” than one who enters feeling capable. The Stage 4 wound shapes what Stage 5 can build. What you say and do in a classroom with a 9-year-old is participating in the foundation of that person’s adult identity.

Major concept 2 · The Looking Glass Self

Identity Formation

Adolescence is the first period when young people have the cognitive capacity to ask genuinely big questions: Who am I? What do I believe? What do I stand for? These are not rhetorical — they are urgent developmental tasks, and the social environment of school, peers, and family is exerting enormous pressure about who this person should become.

Psychologist James Marcia elaborated Erikson’s framework by identifying four positions a person can occupy in the identity formation process. The most important for teachers to understand is foreclosure — committing to an identity without ever genuinely exploring alternatives. The foreclosed student looks certain, even settled, but their identity has never been tested. Challenge feels like attack, because there is no resilience built from having actually wrestled with alternatives.

The opposite failure is diffusion — no exploration and no commitment, just drift. But the status teachers most often misread is moratorium: active exploration without a landing point yet. The moratorium student looks scattered and inconsistent. They are actually doing exactly what healthy development requires. Most adolescents who arrive at a genuine, chosen sense of self — what Marcia called achievement — pass through a period that looked, from the outside, like a mess.

Context — The World Your Students Are Inheriting

Something measurable happened to American culture around 2012. Survey researchers noticed attitudes about race, identity, and group membership shifting not gradually but fast. The vocabulary of identity —systemic, privilege, cisgender, intersectional— migrated from academic journals into newsrooms, HR departments, and high school hallways. Whatever drove this shift, your students grew up inside it.

Adolescents are already in the most identity-intensive developmental period of their lives. They are also growing up in a cultural moment that has turned the volume on group identity up to eleven. These two facts interact. A student who might have explored an uncertain, expansive sense of self in a quieter era now faces enormous social pressure — from peers, from media, from institutions — to identify with a group, adopt that group’s frameworks, and organize their self-concept accordingly. That pressure has a name in Marcia’s framework: it pushes toward foreclosure. And foreclosure, however confident it looks from the outside, is not identity. It is identity stopped.

None of this means you take a political position in your classroom. It means you understand the developmental terrain your students are actually navigating — not the idealized terrain of a textbook written in calmer times.

Kierkegaard’s warning: “Once you label me, you negate me.” A teacher who responds to difficult behavior by reaching for a category — “troublemaker,” “she’s not a math person,” “he’s just ADHD” — forecloses the possibility of becoming. The label ends the story. The better question is not what is this student? but who is this student trying to become?

This is why the people around a student matter so much. Sociologist Charles Cooley observed that self-concept is built by imagining how we appear to others and internalizing that perception — what he called the looking glass self. Other people function as a mirror. A student who reads in your responses that you see a capable, interesting person developing in front of you will tend to internalize that. A student who reads that you see a problem or a foregone conclusion will internalize that too. You are not a neutral observer of your students’ identity development. You are a participant in it.

Retrieval practiceA student confidently declares they know exactly who they are — same values as their parents, same career plan, no uncertainty at all. Using Marcia’s framework, what might be cause for concern despite the apparent confidence?►

This looks like foreclosure. The student has commitment without exploration — they have adopted an identity wholesale, likely from family, without ever genuinely testing it against alternatives. Marcia’s insight is that this kind of certainty is structurally brittle: because it was never tested, it has no resilience. When the foreclosed student encounters a genuine challenge to their values or worldview, they tend to experience it as an attack rather than an opportunity to deepen their thinking.

The contrast with achievement is important: an achieved identity also involves commitment and confidence, but it has been earned through moratorium — through a period of genuine exploration, uncertainty, and wrestling with alternatives. That process builds the kind of identity that can be questioned without collapsing. The student who has never questioned what they believe is not developmentally ahead of the student in the middle of moratorium’s mess. They may be developmentally stuck in a form that looks stable until something important tests it.

Major concept 3 · Real but Not Sufficient

Social Identity

Race, gender, class, religion, and other social dimensions are real — they shape how the world receives a student, what risks they carry, what assumptions follow them through the door. Dismissing these dimensions as irrelevant is not neutrality. It is inaccuracy, and it makes you a less effective teacher for the students who carry them.

And yet: real is not the same as sufficient. A dimension of experience is not the same as a self.

Dimension of experience
Race, gender, sexuality, class, religion, neurodiversity — real features of a person’s life that shape how they move through the world. They affect what a student encounters and how they are perceived. They cannot be ignored because they are already present in the room.
Substance of identity
Character, values, commitments, relationships — what a person chooses and builds and gives themselves to. These are not inherited; they are constructed. They cannot be read off any demographic marker, however real that marker is.

The danger Kierkegaard named is when a dimension of experience becomes the totality of self-definition. Identity foreclosed around a group membership is still foreclosed identity. The student whose entire sense of self is organized around a racial identity, a gender, a religion, or any other inherited category has collapsed the distinction between the context in which a self is built and the self itself. That collapse — wherever it comes from — is a developmental problem, not a solution.

Stereotype threat (Steele): When a student is aware of a negative stereotype about a group they belong to, that awareness itself impairs performance — not because they believe the stereotype, but because managing it consumes cognitive resources that could otherwise go toward the task. The effect has been documented across race, gender, class, age, and other group identities. This means identity dimensions affect learning in your classroom whether you acknowledge them or not. The stereotypes exist in the broader culture and arrive with your students every day.

The teacher’s posture is not complicated to describe, even if it takes practice: take the dimension seriously — do not reduce the person to it. A teacher who ignores the dimension fails the student whose experience it shapes. A teacher who reduces the student to the dimension fails the person who is trying to grow beyond any single label into a full and particular self.

Retrieval practiceBefore a math test, a teacher says: “Today’s test covers material that girls sometimes find challenging, so I want you all to try your best.” What does Steele’s research predict will happen — and why?►

This is close to a textbook example of stereotype-threat activation. By making gender identity salient immediately before a high-stakes math task — and associating it with a negative expectation — the teacher has loaded cognitive overhead onto every female student in the room. Steele’s research shows that the awareness of a negative stereotype about one’s group depresses performance not because the student believes the stereotype, but because managing the awareness of it consumes working memory and attentional resources that should be going toward the math.

The performance gap the teacher may observe afterward was partly produced by the introduction. This is the practical implication of the research: stereotype threat is activated by making group identity salient right before performance, especially with language that links the group to a negative expectation. The intervention runs in reverse: avoid making group identity salient before high-stakes tasks; briefly affirm belonging and capability specifically rather than generally; frame challenges as high expectations paired with genuine confidence in the student’s ability to meet them.

Major concept 4 · The Person Behind the Label

Sex, Gender, and Sexuality

Among the most hotly contested areas of culture war battles involve sex, sexuality, and gender. There are two objectives for an evidence-based teacher: first, use accurate, professional terminology. Second, show dignity to all students. 

Why focus so much on vocabulary? Concept 3 warned against the danger of reducing a student to a category. There is an opposite failure, and it is just as real: imprecision. A teacher who does not understand the words their students, parents, and colleagues are already using cannot listen accurately, cannot speak clearly, and cannot serve well. So begin with the vocabulary — plainly, the way you would learn any technical terminology in your field.

Note that knowing what a word means commits you to nothing except competence. Precision is not an endorsement on either side of the culture wars. Rather, it is a commitment to professional language and being an effective, evidence-based teacher. The terms below name distinctions that are in live use in every school in the country; a professional educator should be able to use them accurately whatever they conclude about the deeper questions underneath.

Sex
A person’s biological classification as male, female, or — in rare cases — intersex, based on physical characteristics such as chromosomes, hormones, and reproductive anatomy.
Gender
The social and cultural dimension: the roles, behaviors, and expectations a society associates with being male or female. In everyday speech “sex” and “gender” are used interchangeably; in professional usage they name different things.
Gender identity
A person’s internal, subjective sense of their own gender.
Gender expression
How a person outwardly presents — dress, grooming, mannerisms, speech — which may or may not match cultural expectations for their sex.
Sexual orientation
The pattern of a person’s romantic and sexual attraction. Distinct from gender identity: orientation is about whom a person is drawn to; identity is about their own sense of self. Collapsing the two is the single most common vocabulary error.
Cisgender / Transgender
Cisgender describes a person whose gender identity corresponds to their birth sex; transgender describes a person whose gender identity differs from it.

Fluency in these terms is a professional skill, not a philosophical position. The vocabulary describes distinctions people draw. It does not, by itself, answer the deeper questions underneath — which of these features is most fundamental to who a person is, and what any of them means about human nature and destiny. Those are questions that religious and philosophical traditions answer, and answer differently. This textbook is written inside one of them, and I prefer to be direct on the matter. 

Where this book stands. The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints sponsors BYU - Idaho, my employer. The teachings of the church include that “gender is an essential characteristic of individual premortal, mortal, and eternal identity and purpose” — that each student in your future classroom is, most fundamentally, a son or daughter of Heavenly Parents with a divine nature and destiny. That is a doctrinal claim, and it is one that I support in earnest. The teachings of the church equally include the necessity to live up to Jesus' calls of Christian love and dignity. There is no contradiction between protecting the dignity of sexual minorities and holding to these doctrinal beliefs. To the contrary, a Latter-day Saint is obligated to do both. 

It can feel as though conviction and compassion pull against each other: as though honoring a student would mean betraying what you believe, or holding what you believe would mean withholding warmth. That felt conflict is a mistake. The Latter-day Saint tradition, and the doctrines of The Church of Jesus Christ dissolves the apparent conflict: there is an expectation to hold to the beliefs of the church on matters of family and gender, and an equally binding claim about how you are to treat every person without exception.

The duty of dignity. Latter-day Saints have been taught: “Anyone who does not treat individuals who face gender identity challenges with love and dignity is not aligned with the teachings of the first and second great commandments.” The worth of every soul is great in the sight of God. So the dignity you owe a student is not conditional on whether you share their understanding of themselves, and not something you extend only where it is comfortable. It is owed to every child in the room, in full, as a matter of covenant — not sentiment, and not reward for agreement. 

Here the doctrine and the developmental science of this chapter say the same thing in two languages. Cooley showed you that you are a mirror in which students watch themselves forming. Steele showed you that a student who carries the sense of being unwelcome or diminished spends cognitive resources managing that sense instead of learning. So a teacher who lets personal conviction curdle into coldness, exclusion, or contempt toward a particular student is not only failing a Christian duty — they are measurably harming that student’s development, in precisely the ways the research documents. Dignity is not the soft counterpart to rigor. It is a condition of the learning you are there to produce.

In practice the duty is more concrete than it sounds. It means learning who each student actually is rather than who you assumed. It means a classroom where no student is humiliated, isolated, or made a spectacle — and where every one of them can learn. It means being the trustworthy adult a struggling student can approach. 

Many of the specific questions teachers now face — about names, about pronouns, about what to tell parents and when — are governed by law, district policy, and professional judgment. It is the duty of a teacher to research the policies of a school district before getting hired, and deciding whether they can confirm to those policies. These questions are the subject of genuine, good-faith disagreement. That does not negate your duty of loyalty to your district, and that decision is best made in advance. 

This chapter does not resolve them for you; your school and your conscience will. But the principle beneath them is not in dispute: every student in your care is a person of infinite worth, entitled to your respect, your protection, and your best teaching, whatever you believe about the questions they are living through.

Retrieval practiceA teacher holds sincere religious convictions about the nature of gender. A student in the class identifies as transgender. Some part of the teacher assumes these two facts must collide — that honoring the student would betray the conviction, or holding the conviction would require withholding warmth. Using this chapter, why is that assumed collision a mistake?►

The conflict is imagined, not real, and the chapter dissolves it from two directions at once. Doctrinally, the same tradition that teaches a conviction about gender teaches — in equally binding terms — that failing to treat the student with love and dignity puts a person out of alignment with the two great commandments. The duty to honor the student is not a concession to be weighed against the conviction; it issues from the same source.

Developmentally, Cooley and Steele show that a student who reads coldness or contempt in a teacher’s regard is harmed in measurable ways — the mirror distorts, and cognitive resources drain away from learning. So the teacher who believes they must choose between conviction and care has misread both their faith and the science. Full dignity to the student is what each of them independently requires. The teacher can hold the conviction and give the student their whole-hearted respect, protection, and teaching. That is not a tension to be managed. It is the assignment.

Major concept 5 · Losing Yourself to Find Yourself

Altruistic Belonging

The subtitle of this chapter — He Who Loseth His Life — points toward a paradox that appears consistently across the psychology of identity: the deepest sense of self is often found not through self-assertion, but through commitment to something beyond the self. People whose identity is organized around contribution and shared purpose tend to be more psychologically grounded than people whose identity depends primarily on recognition from others.

Egoistic Belonging — “I am seen”
Identity grounded in individual recognition — being valued for who you are. Necessary and healthy; without it, a person feels invisible. But when identity depends entirely on others’ recognition, it is fragile — easily threatened by any challenge to status or approval.
Altruistic Belonging — “I contribute”
Identity grounded in service, contribution, and connection to something larger. Produces purpose and resilience that doesn’t depend on any single relationship or moment of recognition. The self that contributes is more stable than the self that merely performs.
Narrative identity (McAdams): Psychologist Dan McAdams argues that identity is the story we construct about our own life — and that the shape of that story matters enormously. People who narrate their hardships as turning points — where something hard led to growth — show greater resilience and wellbeing than those whose stories follow a contamination arc, where something good was ruined and never recovered. Teachers participate in which kind of story gets written. A response to student failure that communicates “this is hard, and you’re going to figure it out” contributes to a different chapter than one that communicates judgment or low expectation.

For educators, the practical implication is direct: one of the most powerful things a classroom can do is give students something genuinely worth contributing to. Not token leadership roles — real investment in a shared project or problem that depends on their specific contribution. When students experience themselves as genuinely needed, something shifts in how they carry themselves through the world.

Retrieval practiceTwo students both feel a sense of belonging in their school. One feels belonging because classmates know and like them. The other feels belonging because they are genuinely contributing to a team project that depends on their specific work. According to the egoistic/altruistic distinction, how do these differ — and which is more likely to be resilient?►

The first student has egoistic belonging — recognition-based, dependent on others’ ongoing approval. It’s genuine and valuable: without it, a person feels invisible. But it is structurally fragile. Any social setback, any period of invisibility or rejection, and the ground gives way. Identity built primarily on how others see you is hostage to how others happen to feel on any given day.

The second student has altruistic belonging — contribution-based, grounded in a sense of mattering to something larger than themselves. This form is more resilient because it doesn’t depend on any single relationship or moment of recognition. The project still needs them whether or not any individual peer is particularly warm that day. Research on purpose and meaning consistently shows that people whose sense of self is organized around contribution weather adversity better than those whose identity is organized primarily around status or recognition.

The paradox the chapter title names: losing the self — into a cause, a community, a genuine commitment — is often how the self is actually found.

In the classroomSeven practices with mechanisms

Each practice below connects directly to a concept in this chapter. The mechanism is the reason it works — knowing it makes the practice more portable.

  • Give students genuine responsibility, not busywork roles. Altruistic belonging requires that students experience themselves as genuinely needed. Assign tasks that actually matter: student-led instruction, real feedback roles, ownership of shared resources. When a student’s contribution is consequential, their self-concept shifts accordingly.
  • Name character, not just performance. “You stayed with that problem when it got hard” lands differently from “good job.” The first describes who the student is; the second describes what they produced. The looking glass self tells us students internalize what they perceive you seeing in them. Name what careful looking actually finds.
  • Refuse academic identity labels — in your language and your thinking.“ Not a math person.” “My difficult student.” These are foreclosure moves — they end the story before it’s written. Your language participates in whether students foreclose or keep exploring.
  • Mitigate stereotype threat before high-stakes tasks. Avoid making group identity salient right before a test. Briefly affirm belonging and capability specifically, not generically. Frame challenges as high expectations paired with genuine confidence in the student’s ability to meet them. These small design choices have documented effects.
  • Make space for moratorium — don’t demand premature certainty. The student who is scattered and inconsistent may be doing exactly what healthy development requires. A classroom that demands students perform a fixed identity collapses the space moratorium needs. Ask open questions. Tolerate productive uncertainty. Let students change their minds without penalty.
  • Connect academic content to students’ actual questions. The student asking “who am I?” is not separate from the student in your classroom. When students experience academic content as relevant to the real work of becoming who they are, engagement stops being a problem you have to solve separately.
  • Extend dignity that isn’t conditional. Every student is owed your respect, protection, and best teaching regardless of whether you share their understanding of themselves. This is both a Christian duty and a developmental necessity: the looking glass self and stereotype threat both predict that a student who reads coldness or exclusion is harmed in ways that surface in their learning. Learn who each student actually is; make sure no one is humiliated or made a spectacle; be the trustworthy adult they can approach.
Chapter synthesis
Identity formation is not a distraction from education. It is one of its deepest purposes — and one that teachers are inside, whether they intend to be or not. Erikson’s stages give you a developmental map that locates your work precisely: stages 4 and 5 are where you operate, and what happens in them echoes into everything that follows. Marcia shows you the positions your students can occupy in the search, and the moratorium mess that looks like failure is often health in progress. Cooley shows you that you are in the mirror. Steele shows you that the stereotypes your students carry tax them before they sit down to learn. And the distinction between egoistic and altruistic belonging points toward the classroom’s deepest leverage: not recognition alone, but genuine contribution to something that needs them. Beneath all of it sits a duty the tradition sponsoring this university states plainly: every student is owed accurate understanding and full dignity — a duty that, as it happens, the developmental science demands in its own language too.
Erikson’s Stage 4 (Industry vs. Inferiority) is not just about elementary school. A child who leaves feeling fundamentally incompetent carries that wound into Stage 5 identity formation — and the wound shapes what Stage 5 can build.
Foreclosure looks like stability but is brittle — it was never tested. Moratorium looks like a mess but is developmentally healthy. Teachers who demand premature certainty are pushing students toward the wrong kind of settled.
The looking glass self is not a metaphor. Students are actively constructing their self-concept from what they read in your responses. You are not a neutral observer — you are a participant who is shaping what they see.
Altruistic belonging is more resilient than egoistic belonging. The classroom that gives students something genuinely worth contributing to is doing something deeper than engagement — it is giving students a more durable place to stand.
The vocabulary of sex, gender, and sexuality is a professional lexicon, not a creed — use it accurately. And the dignity owed every student is not conditional on sharing their view of themselves. Conviction and care are not a trade-off: the tradition sponsoring this work and the developmental science agree that withholding dignity fails the student and the teacher’s duty at once.
