Anthropology & Culture

Tradition Is Just Peer Pressure from Dead People

Chapter 17~ 50 min read

The title of this chapter is not original. It appeared on a bumper sticker, then a meme, then everywhere. Its appeal is obvious: it reframes tradition as something being done to you rather than something you choose, and that reframing feels liberating — at least until you notice that the sentence itself is functioning as peer pressure, nudging you toward skepticism of tradition in the same way traditions nudge people toward conformity.

Culture is like that. It doesn’t announce itself. It works by making certain things seem obvious, inevitable, and natural — and by making the alternatives seem strange, wrong, or simply unimaginable. The meals you find comforting, the amount of eye contact you consider respectful, the way you think about time, the emotions you feel free to display in public: these are not universal human behaviors. They are cultural ones. And you didn’t choose most of them. They arrived before you had the language to question them.

This chapter is about the water you swim in — where it comes from, how it shapes you, what happens when people push back against it, and what happens when cultures shaped by different histories end up in the same classroom. One warning before you start: this chapter is not about other people’s cultures. The goal is not to help you understand your students’ backgrounds as fixed categories. It is to develop the kind of cultural self-awareness that makes you a more useful adult in the room. That is harder than it sounds — because the thing you are trying to see is the lens you are looking through. That is the difficulty. That is also the point.

The big picture
Culture is so pervasive it is nearly invisible — the water we swim in, the air we breathe. It shapes what we value, how we see the world, what we conform to, and what we dare to question. This chapter explores where culture comes from, how it exerts pressure on individuals sometimes across generations, what it looks like when people push back, and how teachers navigate classrooms where cultures — and culture wars — collide. Understanding culture is not optional for educators: every classroom is a collision of worldviews, and knowing how to navigate that — without reducing any student to a category — is one of teaching’s most essential skills.

Major concept 1 · Culture

The Water We Swim In

In 1956, anthropologist Horace Miner published a study of a tribe called the Nacirema — a group occupying, as Miner described it, “the territory between the Canadian Cree, the Yaqui and Tarahumare of Mexico, and the Carib and Arawak of the Antilles.” Miner noted that this was a culture that had received surprisingly little scholarly attention. What follows is a brief excerpt from his field report.

The Nacirema have an almost pathological horror of and fascination with the mouth, the condition of which is believed to have a supernatural influence on all social relationships. The daily body ritual performed by everyone includes a mouth-rite. Despite the fact that these people are so punctilious about care of the mouth, this rite involves a practice which strikes the uninitiated stranger as revolting. It was reported to me that the ritual consists of inserting a small hog’s hair bundle into the mouth, along with magical powders, and then moving the bundle in a highly formalized series of gestures.

Miner goes on to describe the Nacirema’s household shrines — small boxes mounted on the wall of a special room in nearly every home, filled with magical potions and charms — and the powerful medicine men, called “holy-mouth-men,” whom members of the tribe visit twice yearly to have their teeth scraped with metal instruments. He describes a great temple called the “latipso,” where the very sick go and are subjected to elaborate rituals by robed practitioners. He notes that the Nacirema seem to believe that the human body is inherently ugly and prone to disease, and that only constant ritual can stave off decay and social rejection.

You may have noticed by now that Nacirema is American spelled backwards.

Miner was describing people brushing their teeth. Medicine cabinets. Dentists. Hospitals. Things so ordinary to American readers that they had never once thought to examine them — until he described them the way an anthropologist might describe a ritual from a culture they had never encountered. Suddenly the familiar became strange. The invisible became visible. And that, precisely, is the point.

What “Body Ritual Among the Nacirema” teaches us

Miner’s essay is a trick, but it is a trick with serious underlying points. Here are a few:

Culture is invisible
from the inside.

Your culture may not feel strange, but it would to an outsider. The Nacirema didn’t know they had rituals. They had a morning routine. The practices that feel most natural to us are often the most culturally constructed. Like your parents’ accent, or the faint smell of your own house — you stopped noticing it long ago.

All cultures
have magic.

Before you judge the Nacirema’s “magical powders” and “holy-mouth-men,” consider what it looks like to an outsider when you take a small white pill because a credentialed stranger told you it would help. Every culture has practices that require trust, symbol, and belief. Ours included.

Strange is a matter
of position, not fact.

Nothing Miner described was actually strange — it was familiar, described from an unfamiliar angle. This is useful to remember when a student’s cultural practices seem odd to you. They are not odd. You are standing in an unfamiliar position.

You are not exempt
from what you study.

This is perhaps the deepest lesson. Teachers who think about culture, worldview, and conformity are not above those forces — they are inside them. The goal of this chapter is not to understand other people’s cultures. It is to understand culture, including your own.

Anthropology — the study of human societies, behaviors, and cultures across time and place — begins with a deceptively simple observation: people are not born with a way of life. They are born into one. Culture is the accumulated set of shared meanings, practices, values, symbols, and social norms that a group passes from one generation to the next. It is both the product of human activity and the invisible force that shapes it. Culture tells us what is beautiful and what is shameful, what counts as success, what we owe each other, and what stories explain why the world is the way it is.

What makes culture so powerful — and so easy to miss — is its hiddenness. Most of what culture does, it does without announcement. The meals you find comforting, the amount of eye contact you consider respectful, the way you think about time, the emotions you feel free to display in public — these are not universal human behaviors. They are cultural ones. People raised in different cultures do not simply have different opinions; they often have genuinely different perceptions of what is happening in the same situation. Culture is not static. It evolves — sometimes slowly, through gradual drift, and sometimes rapidly, through contact with other cultures, technological change, or deliberate challenge from within. The title of this chapter — tradition is just pressure from dead people — is not a dismissal of tradition. It is an invitation to examine it. Every tradition was once a new idea. Every norm was once a choice.

Every person inside a culture also carries something more personal: a worldview — the fundamental lens through which they interpret reality, encompassing their beliefs about what is true, what is good, and what life means. Worldviews are shaped by culture, but they are also shaped by religion, family, individual temperament, and experience. No two are identical, even within the same household. This means that knowing a student’s cultural background gives you useful context but never the full picture — the student in front of you is always more than any category you can name.

For educators

You cannot be culturally neutral in a classroom — your own worldview, communication style, assumptions about learning, and ideas about what “good” behavior looks like are all cultural products. The goal is not neutrality but awareness. A teacher who assumes that sustained eye contact signals respect, that speaking up in discussion signals engagement, or that a quiet student is a disengaged one is not seeing clearly — they are seeing through a cultural lens they have not yet examined. The practice is to pause before interpreting and ask: is what I am seeing a problem, or is it a difference?

When students disengage, resist, or push back against content, it is often not laziness or defiance — it is a worldview in friction with what is being presented. Skilled teachers distinguish between “this student does not understand” and “this student understands but finds it conflicts with something they hold deeply.” The second situation requires a different response — not abandoning the content, but acknowledging the friction honestly: I can see this pushes against something important to you. That tension is worth sitting with. Can you tell me more about what’s bothering you?

Retrieval practiceMiner’s Nacirema essay describes tooth-brushing as a “mouth-rite” performed with a “hog’s hair bundle and magical powders.” What is the actual point of this exercise — and what does it teach about culture that a straightforward description wouldn’t?►

The essay makes visible what is normally invisible. By describing a familiar practice in anthropological language — as if from outside the culture — Miner forces the reader to see their own routine as strange. The lesson: culture is not invisible because it is hidden, but because it is too close. We see through our cultural assumptions rather than seeing them.

A straightforward description (“Americans brush their teeth twice a day”) does not produce this effect. The defamiliarization is the mechanism. For teachers, the implication runs in both directions: your students’ practices are not strange — you are standing in an unfamiliar position. And your own practices are not natural — they only feel that way because you stopped noticing them.

Major concept 2 · Conformity

The Group’s Quiet Authority

One of the most consistent findings in social psychology is that human beings are far more influenced by the people around them than they believe themselves to be. Conformity — the adjustment of one’s beliefs or behaviors to align with group norms — is not a character flaw or a sign of weakness. It is a deeply wired social behavior that evolved because cooperation within groups was essential for survival. The same neural systems that make us sensitive to belonging and exclusion also make us responsive to group pressure, often without our conscious awareness.

Solomon Asch’s classic conformity experiments demonstrated just how powerful this effect is. When participants were asked to judge which of three lines matched a target line — an objectively simple perceptual task — they gave the wrong answer up to 75% of the time when surrounded by confederates who unanimously chose the wrong answer. Most participants reported genuine doubt about their own perception, not simply a desire to go along. The group did not just pressure them — it changed what they saw. This has profound implications for any classroom discussion, peer review process, or group activity: students are not just performing for the group, they are being genuinely influenced by it.

Positive Conformity & Peer Pressure
Friends who study together and hold each other accountable. Team culture that normalizes effort, hustle, and healthy habits. Classroom norms where asking questions is admired rather than embarrassing. Peer modeling of intellectual curiosity, kindness, or civic engagement.
Negative Conformity & Peer Pressure
Suppressing academic effort to avoid appearing “too eager.” Going along with exclusion, bullying, or risky behavior to maintain social standing. Abandoning one’s own values or interests to match the group’s identity. Silence in the face of injustice because speaking up risks belonging.

Peer pressure is not always loud or explicit. Much of it operates through implication — through the looks exchanged across a room, the subtle withdrawal of warmth when someone breaks an unwritten rule, the intangible sense of what the group finds worthy of respect. Students learn to read these signals rapidly and adjust accordingly. This is why classroom culture is so powerful: the peer norms operating in any given room either support or undermine everything the teacher is trying to do. A teacher whose stated values are curiosity and effort but whose classroom culture rewards compliance and silence will produce compliance and silence — regardless of the lesson plan.

Group synchrony — the tendency for people in groups to unconsciously coordinate their movements, rhythms, and behaviors — is a related phenomenon worth understanding. In a series of experiments by Wiltermuth and Heath, people who walked in step, sang together, or kept time with music subsequently cooperated more, trusted each other more, and made greater personal sacrifices for the group — even when they felt no particular positive emotion during the synchronous activity. Rituals across cultures have always known this, even without the neuroscience to explain it — from religious chant to military drill to school fight songs. In the classroom, shared rituals and routines do more than create order; they build the felt sense of “we” that makes a group of individuals into a community.

The practical applications are simpler than the neuroscience might suggest. A consistent opening routine — the same question on the board every Monday, a brief stretch before a long work period, a moment of silence before a test — costs almost nothing and quietly builds the kind of rhythmic shared experience that synchrony research points to. Call-and-response is one of the oldest classroom tools in existence, and now we know why it works beyond simple attention management. Group reading aloud, choral repetition of key terms, even clapping out a rhythm to help students memorize a sequence — these are not elementary school novelties, they are synchrony, and the trust they build transfers into the academic work that follows.

Design implication

Teachers who want to build positive peer culture cannot simply prohibit negative conformity — they must actively engineer the norms they want. This means publicly recognizing the behaviors that reflect the values you want to spread, designing activities where the prosocial move is also the socially rewarding one, and being the kind of adult whose modeling makes intellectual curiosity and kindness look like strength rather than vulnerability. Shared routines, call-and-response, starting every class the same way — these are not soft touches. They are synchrony, and synchrony builds trust.

Retrieval practiceAsch’s experiments showed people gave wrong answers 75% of the time when surrounded by confederates choosing incorrectly. What does this finding specifically mean for teachers who run group discussions or peer review activities?►

The finding means that in a classroom group discussion, students are not merely reporting their views under social pressure — the group is likely changing what they actually believe they perceive. A student who agrees with the majority may have genuinely updated their view, not just performed agreement. This creates at least two implications:

First: The order in which students share opinions matters. Calling on a vocal student first can suppress the authentic views of everyone who speaks afterward — not because those students are dishonest, but because the Asch effect is real. Anonymous response systems, written responses before discussion, or cold-calling in random order all reduce this effect.

Second: The presence of even one dissenter dramatically reduces conformity in Asch’s experiments. A teacher who models intellectual independence — who disagrees publicly with a source, questions a consensus, or says “I used to think that too, but here’s what changed my mind” — is not just modeling thinking. They are weakening the conformity pressure in the room.

Major concept 3 · Expressive Individualism

The Culture of the Authentic Self

Expressive individualism is one of the defining cultural currents of contemporary Western life. At its core, it holds that each person has a unique inner self — a true identity — and that the highest human good is to discover, articulate, and live in accordance with that self, even when doing so requires rejecting the expectations of family, tradition, religion, or community. The philosopher Charles Taylor traced this idea through Romanticism and into the present, noting that it has become so widespread in Western culture that it functions less like a philosophy and more like common sense — the water that particularly modern people swim in.

Expressive individualism has produced genuine goods. It has empowered people to live more honestly, to resist oppressive conformity, to honor identities that previous generations were forced to hide, and to hold institutions accountable when their demands for conformity cause genuine harm. These are not small things. At the same time, it has also generated real tensions. When personal authenticity becomes the supreme value, it can clash with the demands of community, commitment, and shared responsibility. The question “but is this really me?” can become a reason to exit any relationship, tradition, or obligation that feels constraining — even those that, on reflection, might be genuinely worth keeping.

For educators, expressive individualism is not a problem to be solved — it is a cultural reality to be understood. Many students today operate from within it as a default worldview. Understanding this helps explain why students sometimes resist group assignments, chafe against uniform expectations, or experience genuine distress when asked to conform to norms that feel like they conflict with who they are. It also helps explain why appeals to tradition or authority are less persuasive than they once were — and why approaches that connect learning to personal meaning tend to be more effective than those that simply demand compliance.

It is also worth noticing what expressive individualism can obscure. When every difference feels like identity, and every preference feels like a deep truth about who someone is, students and their families can come to treat ordinary variation — in interest, in energy, in mood — as something more fixed and categorical than it actually is. The teacher who understands expressive individualism can hold this with some perspective: honoring the genuine self each student brings while not treating every discomfort as a signal that something is fundamentally wrong with the environment.

Retrieval practiceA student says: “I just can’t do group projects — it’s not who I am as a learner.” Using expressive individualism as a lens, what might this student be expressing — and what would an effective teacher response look like?►

Through the lens of expressive individualism, this student is doing something culturally coherent: invoking their authentic self as a grounds for exemption from a collective expectation. The framing “it’s not who I am” treats a preference as an identity — and within an expressive individualist worldview, identity claims carry strong moral weight. The student is not being dishonest; they are using the vocabulary their culture has given them.

An effective teacher response neither dismisses the claim nor simply capitulates to it. It takes the student seriously while gently separating preference from identity and reframing discomfort as a growth opportunity. Something like: “I hear that this feels uncomfortable — a lot of people feel that way about group work. The goal isn’t to ignore that, it’s to help you develop skills that make you more capable in situations you can’t always control. Let’s figure out what specifically feels hardest.” This treats the student as capable of growth rather than as a fixed self with fixed constraints — which is, incidentally, closer to what the Stoics would suggest.

Major concept 4 · Culture Wars

When Culture Becomes a Battlefield

A culture war is what happens when competing worldviews stop coexisting and start fighting over who gets to shape shared institutions — think of political issues in the classroom. The conflict is not usually about facts in the ordinary sense — it is about which values, histories, and ways of understanding the world should be taught, celebrated, and passed on. Schools are almost always at the center of these conflicts, because schools are where societies do the work of transmitting culture to the next generation. What gets taught, how it gets taught, whose stories get told, and what counts as truth are not neutral questions. They are the questions culture wars are made of.

This is not a new phenomenon. American schools have been contested ground over evolution, sex education, history curricula, prayer, and the content of library shelves for as long as there have been American schools. What has changed is the intensity, speed, and political organization of those conflicts. A curriculum decision that once took years to become controversial can now generate a national news cycle in 48 hours. Teachers find themselves navigating a landscape in which parents, administrators, legislators, and advocacy groups on multiple sides may all have strong and incompatible views about what should happen in the classroom — and where the teacher’s professional judgment is often the last thing anyone asks about.

For teachers, the first and most useful distinction is between three types of questions that often get lumped together as “controversial”:

Genuinely contested
Questions where reasonable, informed people genuinely disagree based on different values — not just different information. Abortion, capital punishment, and immigration policy generate different answers from different people based on different values: these are normative, not empirical questions. Teachers should present multiple perspectives without advocating for one.
Edgy but answerable
Questions that feel controversial but actually have defensible answers most people would accept on reflection. Was slavery wrong? Was the Holocaust evil? These questions may be uncomfortable, but discomfort is not the same as genuine controversy. Treating them as “both sides” issues is not neutrality — it is a failure of clarity. Teachers have an obligation to stand for what is right.
Empirically contested
Questions where scientific evidence is genuinely uncertain or actively debated among experts. These require intellectual humility and precision — distinguishing what we know, what we think we know, and what remains open. Teachers succeed when they frame the debate honestly and point out the gaps in what we know.

Age and developmental stage matter enormously in how — and whether — any of this gets raised at all. A kindergartner has no business hearing about the latest school funding battle. A high school political science teacher who never engages live issues is not doing their job. Between those poles lies a lot of territory that requires judgment rather than rules.

Even when engagement is appropriate, watch for a subtler risk: the slow drift from professional judgment into personal advocacy. A science teacher who presents the evidence on climate change is doing their job. That same teacher who also nudges students toward environmentalism as a political identity, shares a clip mocking one political party, and occasionally editorializes about the current administration has crossed a line — not in any single dramatic moment, but in the accumulated pattern of small choices that, taken together, signal whose side they are on. Students notice. The goal is to model how to think about hard questions, not to recruit students to your conclusions.

Culture wars also create a specific pressure on teachers worth naming directly: the pressure to signal tribal loyalty rather than think clearly. When a topic becomes politically charged, the social cost of engaging it honestly — of saying something that does not fit either side’s preferred narrative — can feel high. This is conformity operating at the professional level, and it is just as powerful as the peer pressure Asch documented in his line experiments. The antidote is not fearlessness — it is a clear sense of what you are actually there to do. You are there to help students think well, not to tell them what to think. That clarity is a more stable guide than any political position.

A final note: teachers have an obligation to know enough about cultural landmines to avoid stepping on them. There is a difference between engaging hard issues thoughtfully and simply behaving in ways that are culturally offensive or professionally inappropriate. That is not a free speech or academic freedom issue — it is a professional conduct issue. Teachers lose jobs not usually because they taught something controversial but because they posted something on social media, made a comment in class that became a headline, or otherwise became a spectacle that the institution could not absorb. You are a full human being with a cultural life, political opinions, and strong views — and none of that has to disappear. But you are also a public-facing professional whose conduct reflects on your school, your students, and your community. Know the difference, and conduct yourself accordingly.

For educators

When a topic becomes politically charged in your school or community, ask yourself two questions before deciding how to handle it. First: is this a genuinely contested value question, an edgy-but-answerable question, or an empirically contested one? Second: what would it look like to teach students how to think about this, rather than what to think? Those two questions will take you further than any scripted answer to any specific controversy.

Retrieval practiceA colleague tells you: “I never take sides on anything — I just present all perspectives equally.” Using the three-category framework, evaluate this stance. What does it get right, and where does it fail?►

What it gets right: On genuinely contested questions — where reasonable people with different values reach different conclusions — presenting multiple perspectives without advocating for one is exactly correct. A teacher who pushes their own position on abortion policy or immigration is not modeling epistemic virtue; they are using their authority to recruit students. For this category, the colleague is right.

Where it fails: Applied to edgy-but-answerable questions, “presenting all perspectives equally” is not neutrality — it is false balance. Treating “was slavery wrong?” as a genuine both-sides question does not protect students from bias. It models that moral clarity is unavailable or suspect. These questions are uncomfortable, but discomfort is not the same as genuine controversy. A teacher has an obligation to stand for what is clearly right.

Applied to empirically contested questions, equal-sides presentation also misleads — when experts with genuine evidence disagree, that is different from manufactured controversy, and students deserve to know the difference. The colleague’s stance, universally applied, flattens all three categories into one — and that flattening is itself a kind of failure of professional judgment.

Major concept 5 · Student Cultures

Context, Not Conclusions

Understanding that students come from different cultural backgrounds is useful. Treating a student as a representative of their background is not. This is the line that cultural competence training most often fails to hold — and failing to hold it produces a version of awareness that is arguably worse than ignorance, because it comes with false confidence. A teacher who has learned a few facts about a student’s culture of origin and now believes they understand that student has not achieved cultural competence. They have achieved a more sophisticated form of assumption.

Cultural knowledge gives you context. A student who rarely makes eye contact with adults may be demonstrating deep respect, not evasiveness. A student who defers consistently to the group rather than offering their own opinion may be operating from a worldview that locates wisdom in collective judgment rather than individual assertion. A student whose family communicates concerns through a community elder rather than a direct parent-teacher conference may not be aiming for avoidant — they could be following a protocol that makes sense within their cultural framework. Knowing these things helps you read situations more accurately and respond more thoughtfully.

But cultural knowledge does not provide conclusions. Knowing that a student comes from a culture with strong family hierarchy tells you something worth knowing. It does not tell you how that particular student experiences that hierarchy — whether they embrace it, quietly resist it, or hold it in some complicated mixture of both. It does not tell you what they need from you today, in this moment, in this classroom. The category describes a group. The student in front of you is not a group. They are a person, shaped by their culture and also by their individual temperament, their birth order, their particular experiences, and their own developing sense of who they are.

The practical discipline is to use cultural knowledge to generate better questions, not premature answers. Not “students from this background tend to value X, so I will assume this student does” — but “I noticed this student seems uncomfortable when I single them out for praise publicly. I wonder if that connects to something cultural, something personal, or something I am not seeing yet. I should find out.” That is the difference between cultural assumption and cultural curiosity. The second is more useful, and it keeps the student — rather than the category — in focus.

The richest resource in the room

The most valuable things your students’ cultures have to offer your classroom are not the foods and the holidays — they are the different ways of thinking, valuing, and making meaning that walk through the door every day. The student whose family tradition emphasizes collective responsibility over individual achievement has something to contribute to a discussion about what we owe each other. The student whose religious worldview frames suffering as meaningful has something to contribute to a discussion about resilience. Cultural difference is not a variable to manage. It is an intellectual resource — but only if you approach each student with enough curiosity to find out what they actually bring, rather than assuming you already know.

Retrieval practiceYou’ve learned that a student comes from a culture with strong family hierarchy. A colleague says: “Now I understand why she never speaks up in class — it’s cultural.” What is right and wrong about this conclusion?►

What’s right: The colleague is correctly using cultural knowledge as context rather than ignoring it. Knowing that deference to authority figures is meaningful in some cultural frameworks is genuinely useful — it prevents misreading silence as indifference, apathy, or disengagement. As a starting hypothesis, it is more sophisticated than the default assumption.

What’s wrong: The colleague has converted context into conclusion. “Now I understand why” implies that the explanation is complete — that the category has done the explanatory work. But this student may embrace her family’s cultural norms, resist them, or hold them in a complicated mixture. She may also be quiet for reasons that have nothing to do with family hierarchy: anxiety, a bad week, a classroom dynamic she’s still reading, or simply the fact that she hasn’t felt asked a question she finds interesting yet.

The discipline is to hold the cultural hypothesis as one possibility among several — and to find out, by asking and observing, which one fits this particular student. “I wonder if” is always more accurate than “now I understand why.” The category gives you a better question. It doesn’t give you the answer.

Major concept 6 · Stoicism

What Another Culture Can Teach Us

Expressive individualism says that the highest human good is to discover your true inner self and live in accordance with it — that your feelings are a guide to who you are, that authenticity is the measure of a life well lived, and that any constraint on self-expression is at least a little suspect. This is the cultural water most of your students swim in. It is also, to a significant degree, the water you swim in. Which is precisely why it is worth spending time with a tradition built on almost exactly the opposite set of assumptions — not to replace one worldview with another, but to see your own more clearly by standing somewhere else for a while.

Stoicism is a philosophical tradition founded around 301 BC by Zeno of Citium, a merchant who — after losing his entire fortune in a shipwreck — walked into an Athenian bookshop, encountered the works of Socrates, and began what would become one of the most enduring schools of philosophy in history. The origin story is itself a Stoic lesson: the external world can take everything from you, but it cannot take your response to what happens. That distinction — between what is in our control and what is not — is the heart of Stoic thought, and it cuts directly against the expressive individualist assumption that your emotional life is the most important thing about you.

Stoicism is frequently misunderstood as the suppression of emotion or the cultivation of indifference. It is neither. Stoics do not argue that suffering is not real or that emotions do not matter. They argue that our suffering is often amplified, extended, and multiplied by the way we think about events — by our interpretations, our demands that the world be other than it is, and our attachment to outcomes we cannot guarantee. Where expressive individualism says feel it and express it, Stoicism says examine it, and choose your response. That is not the same as suppression. It is something closer to freedom.

Courage
Both physical and moral — the willingness to face difficulty, discomfort, or opposition without abandoning one’s values. In a classroom: speaking an unpopular truth, trying a hard task, admitting you do not know, or giving honest feedback when it would be easier to stay quiet.
Temperance
Moderation and self-discipline — neither excess nor deprivation. Avoiding impulsive reactions, finding balance between competing demands, not letting any single desire or emotion dominate judgment. The pause between stimulus and response.
Justice
Treating others with fairness, respect, and genuine care for their wellbeing — not just following rules, but actively contributing to the good of the community one belongs to. Noticing who is being left out, and doing something about it.
Wisdom
The knowledge to distinguish what is within your control from what is not, and the judgment to respond to each appropriately. Wisdom makes the other three virtues possible — without it, courage becomes recklessness and justice becomes self-righteousness.

The Stoic framework has unusual relevance for teachers — not only as a personal philosophy for navigating a demanding profession, but as a lens through which to understand student behavior. A student who melts down when a plan changes may be, in Stoic terms, suffering from attachment to an outcome they cannot control. A student paralyzed by fear of failure is treating external evaluation as the measure of their worth, rather than the quality of their effort and character. A student who responds to frustration with aggression has not yet developed the temperance to pause between stimulus and response. From the perspective of expressive individualism, these students are just feeling their feelings. From the Stoic perspective, they are practicing a skill — and it is a skill that can be explicitly taught, modeled, and developed.

There is one more reason Stoicism belongs in this chapter. The Stoics were cosmopolitans — they believed in the fundamental equality and dignity of all human beings, regardless of tribe, nation, culture, or status. At a time when most philosophical traditions assumed that your group determined your worth, the Stoics insisted that what mattered was your character and your reason, which were available to everyone equally. This pushes back against both expressive individualism and tribalism simultaneously — neither unconstrained self-expression nor uncritical loyalty to the group, but a principled, examined life guided by reason and virtue. And it comes from a merchant in ancient Athens who lost everything in a shipwreck. That should make us curious about what else we might be missing — in other cultures, other centuries, and other ways of being human that we have not yet stood close enough to learn from.

Stoicism in the classroom

The Stoic question — “Is this within my control?” — is one of the most useful metacognitive tools a student can learn. Teaching students to sort their concerns into things they can act on and things they cannot change reduces anxiety, builds agency, and produces more effective problem-solving. It is also, quietly, one of the most culturally countercultural ideas a teacher can introduce: the suggestion that your inner life is not determined by what happens to you — and that virtue, not feeling, is the measure of how well you are doing.

Retrieval practiceA student melts down whenever a project earns a lower grade than expected — distressed for days, refusing to engage with feedback, saying things like “I’m just bad at this.” Using the Stoic framework, analyze what is happening — and describe what a teacher might do.►

The Stoic analysis: The student is treating an external outcome (the grade) as the measure of their worth, rather than the quality of their effort and character — which is precisely what the Stoics argued we should not do. The grade is not within the student’s control; the effort, the revision, and the response to feedback are. By locating their sense of self in the outcome, the student has made themselves vulnerable to exactly the distress they’re experiencing. The withdrawal from feedback is also Stoically relevant: it is avoidance of a situation that feels threatening, rather than the courageous engagement that would actually produce growth.

What a teacher might do: Rather than reassuring the student that the grade “doesn’t matter” (which it does, somewhat) or that they are “actually great” (which the student won’t believe), a Stoic-informed teacher might help the student separate the grade from the learning: “The grade is done. What’s in your control now is what you do with the feedback. Let’s look at it together.” Over time, the explicit question — “What here is actually within your control?” — becomes a teachable metacognitive habit. It is more durable than reassurance, and it does not require the student to believe something false.

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

Every classroom is a collision of worldviews — including yours. These are the habits of mind and practice that follow from taking that seriously.

  • Before interpreting student behavior, pause and ask: is what I am seeing a problem, or is it a difference? A student who avoids eye contact, defers to the group, or stays quiet may be demonstrating respect, operating from a communal worldview, or reading a social situation — not disengaging from yours.
  • Design for positive peer norms deliberately, not just by prohibiting negative ones. Call out the behaviors that reflect the values you want to spread. Use shared routines, call-and-response, and consistent openings to build the group synchrony that makes trust possible. Peer culture is a design problem, not a discipline problem.
  • When a controversial topic comes up, classify it before responding: genuinely contested (present multiple perspectives), edgy-but-answerable (stand for what is clearly right), or empirically contested (model intellectual humility). Do not treat all three the same way.
  • Use cultural knowledge to generate better questions, not conclusions. When you notice something culturally unfamiliar, your job is to get curious — not to assume you already understand. “I wonder if” is always more accurate than “now I know why.”
  • Introduce the Stoic distinction — “what is within my control?” — as a teachable metacognitive tool. It reduces anxiety, builds agency, and is among the most countercultural gifts you can give students who have been taught that their feelings are the most important thing about them.
  • Examine your own cultural lens as regularly as you examine theirs. The Nacirema exercise was not about Americans being strange. It was about what happens when you look at your own practices from the outside. That is a discipline, not a one-time insight.
Chapter synthesis
Every classroom is a collision of worldviews — including yours. Culture is not something that happens to other people; it is the water every person in the room is swimming in, including the teacher. Knowing this changes the work: not to achieve neutrality (which is impossible) but to achieve awareness — of what you are assuming, what you are missing, and what your students might be bringing that your lens does not yet allow you to see. The traditions your students carry are not obstacles to education. They are invitations to think more broadly than any one culture can manage alone.
Culture is invisible from the inside — which is why the Nacirema exercise works. The practices that feel most natural are often the most culturally constructed. Teachers cannot be culturally neutral; the goal is awareness, not neutrality.
Conformity is not a character flaw — it is a wired social behavior. Groups change what people see, not just what they say. Positive peer culture must be actively engineered, not passively hoped for.
Expressive individualism is the default worldview of most contemporary students — and understanding it helps explain resistance to group norms, authority, and standardization. It is a cultural reality to be understood, not a problem to be solved.
Not all controversial questions are the same. Genuinely contested, edgy-but-answerable, and empirically contested questions each require a different teacher response. Treating them identically is not neutrality — it is a failure of judgment.
Cultural knowledge generates better questions, not conclusions. The student in front of you is always more than any category you can name. Cultural curiosity keeps the person in focus; cultural assumption replaces them with a stereotype.
Stoicism offers what expressive individualism cannot: a framework where virtue, not feeling, is the measure of how well you are doing — and where the question “is this within my control?” is the most useful metacognitive tool a student can develop.
