Intelligence & IQ

Is Smart a Number?

Chapter 23~ 55 min read

In 1917, the United States Army faced a logistical problem: it needed to process 1.75 million recruits and assign them to appropriate roles, quickly. The solution was the Army Alpha test — the first large-scale deployment of psychological testing in history. The results determined who received officer training and who received manual assignments. Within a few years, the same data had been cited in Congressional testimony to help justify immigration quotas that reduced arrivals from Southern and Eastern Europe by 80%.

The psychologists who designed those tests believed they were doing science. Some of them were. Some of the science was genuinely useful — and some of it was shaped by conclusions the researchers wanted to reach. The tests measured something real. What people did with the results, and what they claimed those results proved, was a different matter entirely.

This tension — between the genuine scientific content of intelligence research and the uses to which it has been put — runs through everything in this chapter. The goal is not to resolve it by pretending one side doesn’t exist. It is to understand the science accurately enough to hold both things at once.

The big picture
Intelligence is real, measurable, and consequential — and it is also more complicated, more contested, and more malleable than most popular accounts suggest. This chapter covers what psychologists actually know about intelligence: what the evidence supports, where genuine debates remain, and what the research means for teachers. The goal is not to settle every argument. It is to make sure you are arguing about the right things, with an accurate understanding of what the science actually says.

Major concept 1 · Defining Intelligence

What Is Intelligence — and Why Is It Hard to Define?

“Smart” is one of the most loaded words in education — and one of the least precise. Ask ten psychologists what intelligence is and you will get ten different answers. Ask ten teachers and the variation will be even greater. This is not a sign that the concept is meaningless. It is a sign that intelligence is genuinely complex, and that its study sits at the intersection of science, culture, and contested values.

What psychologists can say with confidence is this: performance on cognitive tasks is not random. People who do well on one kind of mental task tend to do well on others. That consistency is real, it is measurable, and it has been replicated across countries, cultures, and testing instruments for over a century. Something is producing that consistency. What to call it, how to measure it, and what causes it — those are the questions where the debates begin.

For teachers, the stakes in this debate are not merely academic. How you think about intelligence shapes what you expect from students, how you design instruction, and who you believe can learn what. A teacher who believes intelligence is fixed and easily measured will behave differently in front of a classroom than one who understands it as complex, multi-determined, and responsive to instruction. Both teachers think they are being realistic. Only one of them is being accurate.

A note on history

Intelligence research has a genuinely troubled past. IQ tests have been used to justify forced sterilization, immigration restriction, ethnic discrimination, and the systematic denial of educational opportunity — particularly to students of color and students from low-income backgrounds. These are not ancient abuses confined to a different era. Their effects persist in contemporary tracking systems, gifted identification practices, and the disproportionate placement of students from marginalized groups in special education programs.

The science is real and useful. It has also never been neutral. Engaging with this chapter requires holding both of those things at once: intellectual seriousness about what the evidence shows, and critical awareness of how that evidence has been used and misused. Those two things are not in conflict. They are both part of being a well-prepared educator.

Retrieval practiceTwo teachers are told a student has an IQ score of 85. Teacher B is more aligned. Teacher A is committing the error of treating a predictive statistic as a prescription — and worse, using it as a ceiling. An IQ of 85 (one standard deviation below average, roughly the 16th percentile) tells you something meaningful about average performance on cognitive tasks at the population level. It tells you almost nothing actionable about what this individual student knows, what they’re capable of with high-quality instruction, what their strengths are within the profile, or how they respond to different types of teaching.

More fundamentally, Teacher A is treating measured cognitive performance as a fixed property of the student rather than as a developmental outcome shaped by experiences, exposures, and opportunities. The science does not support that reading. Adjusting expectations downward based on a single number — before determining what the student actually knows — is not realism. It is the mechanism by which the Pygmalion effect produces the outcomes it anticipates. Teacher B is asking the right questions. The IQ score is one data point in a larger picture, not the picture itself.

Major concept 2 · Spearman’s g

The Case for General Intelligence

Charles Spearman, working in the early 20th century, made an observation that has anchored intelligence research ever since: scores on different cognitive tests tend to correlate positively with each other. People who score well on vocabulary tests also tend to score well on spatial reasoning, math problems, and pattern recognition — even when those tasks seem to have little in common on the surface. Spearman called this pattern the positive manifold, and proposed that it reflected a single underlying cognitive factor he labeled g.

Alongside g, Spearman identified specific abilities (s) — domain-particular skills like verbal fluency, spatial reasoning, or numerical facility that vary independently from one person to the next. In Spearman’s model, g and s work together: general capacity sets the ceiling, specific abilities determine where within that ceiling a person tends to excel. The concept of g remains one of the most replicated findings in all of psychology and the theoretical foundation of modern IQ testing.

Fluid and Crystallized Intelligence

Raymond Cattell and John Horn proposed an influential refinement: what Spearman called g is actually two distinct capacities working in tandem. Fluid intelligence (Gf) is the capacity to reason, solve novel problems, and recognize patterns — relatively independent of prior knowledge, sensitive to biological and environmental conditions, peaking in early adulthood. Crystallized intelligence (Gc) is accumulated knowledge and skill built through experience and education — it reflects everything learning has deposited, and tends to grow across the lifespan. In most real-world tasks, both operate together.

Crystallized Intelligence (Gc)

A student whose parent is a history teacher arrives in class already rich in historical knowledge — that advantage is Gc, and it is deeply tied to circumstance.
Classroom implication — don’t mistake knowledge gaps for capacity limits

A student who appears “gifted” may simply have a richer crystallized knowledge base — built by a stimulating home environment, more books, more travel, or more culturally aligned prior schooling. A student who appears to “struggle” may have strong fluid intelligence but thin crystallized knowledge on the specific topic at hand. Confusing these two things leads to misidentification, tracking errors, and missed potential — especially for students from under-resourced backgrounds. A knowledge gap calls for instruction. A capacity limit — if such a thing even exists at the classroom level — calls for something much harder to verify and much more dangerous to assume.

The Heritability Question

Twin studies and adoption studies consistently find that a substantial portion of IQ variation within populations is attributable to genetic differences. In children, heritability estimates run around 40–50%; in adults, those estimates rise to 60–80%. These are real numbers from well-designed studies. But heritability is one of the most systematically misunderstood concepts in science, and the misunderstanding matters enormously in educational settings.

Heritability does not mean that a trait is fixed. Height is highly heritable — and average heights across populations have risen dramatically in the 20th century in response to improved nutrition. The heritability of a trait tells you about the sources of variation within a specific population under specific conditions. It tells you nothing about whether that trait can change when conditions change. It does not mean that environmental interventions won’t work. And it says nothing about differences between groups — only about variation within a population.

The reaction range — a useful corrective

One of the most useful concepts for navigating the nature-nurture debate in intelligence is the reaction range: genes set a range of possible outcomes for a trait, and environment determines where within that range an individual actually lands. Two people with the same genetic potential can develop very different measured IQs depending on the quality of their nutrition, the stability of their home environment, the richness of their early language exposure, and the quality of their schooling. This is not a soft compromise between genetics and environment. It is an accurate description of how complex traits actually develop. The range matters. The environment matters. And for teachers, the environment is the part you can actually do something about.

Group Differences: An Honest Account

Average score differences between demographic groups on IQ tests are documented in the research literature. The honest account is this: the differences are real in the sense that they are consistently measured. They are not fixed. Score gaps between Black and white Americans, for example, narrowed substantially in the latter decades of the 20th century as educational and economic opportunities became more equitable. The Flynn effect — scores rising across entire populations in a single generation in response to environmental improvement — demonstrates that measured IQ is far more sensitive to environmental conditions than genetic explanations would predict.

The scientific consensus among researchers in intelligence, genetics, and developmental psychology is that the available evidence does not support a genetic explanation for group differences in average IQ scores. The evidence does support environmental explanations: historical and ongoing differences in educational quality, economic resources, exposure to environmental toxins, chronic stress, and accumulated disadvantage. These are not soft sociological observations. They are documented, measurable factors with known effects on cognitive development.

What this means in your classroom

The group difference data matters for teachers not because it tells you anything about individual students — it does not — but because it tells you something about the systems those students have moved through. A student who has grown up in a high-poverty area, attended under-resourced schools, and experienced chronic environmental stress has not had the same opportunity to develop measured cognitive skills as a student who has not. That gap is real. It reflects circumstance, not capacity. The teacher’s job is not to adjust expectations downward based on group membership. It is to understand that measured gaps in knowledge and skill reflect instructional history — and to teach accordingly.

Retrieval practiceA student from a low-income family scores below average on a cognitive assessment. A colleague concludes this reflects the student’s cognitive ceiling. Identify the two conceptual errors this colleague is likely making — one about fluid vs crystallized intelligence, and one about heritability.►

Error 1 — Confusing Gc with Gf: The test result reflects performance on a mix of cognitive tasks — many of which draw heavily on crystallized intelligence: vocabulary, background knowledge, and culturally specific schema. A student from a low-income background who has had fewer books, less academically rich conversation, and less culturally aligned prior schooling will perform lower on Gc-heavy tasks regardless of their fluid reasoning capacity. The colleague is treating a low Gc score as evidence of limited Gf — conflating knowledge gaps with reasoning limits. These are not the same thing and they call for completely different responses.

Error 2 — Misunderstanding heritability: The colleague appears to be reasoning from “intelligence is heritable” to “this student’s score reflects their fixed genetic ceiling.” This is the core heritability error. Heritability estimates describe the proportion of variance within a population attributable to genetics under current conditions. They say nothing about whether any individual’s measured cognitive ability is fixed, or whether intervention can change it. The Flynn effect alone — 30 points of population-level IQ gain in a single generation — demonstrates that measured IQ is enormously responsive to environmental change. The colleague’s conclusion is not what the evidence supports.

Major concept 3 · Multiple Intelligences

Gardner’s Theory: A Useful Instinct with Weak Evidence

Howard Gardner’s theory of multiple intelligences, introduced in 1983, proposed that what we call intelligence is not a single faculty but a family of distinct abilities — each with its own developmental trajectory, neural basis, and cultural expression. Gardner identified nine intelligences: linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, bodily-kinesthetic, musical, interpersonal, intrapersonal, naturalistic, and existential.

The theory became enormously influential in education — and its educational instinct is sound. Different students do have different strengths. Traditional schooling does reward a narrow slice of human ability. Looking for capability in places that standardized tests don’t measure is genuinely good practice.

Ultimately, the multiple intelligences framework does not hold up well under empirical scrutiny. Cognitive scientists have consistently found that the abilities Gardner proposes are not independent of each other — they correlate in ways that are far more consistent with the existence of a general factor (g) than with nine distinct, separable systems. The positive manifold is the fundamental problem: Gardner’s theory predicts independence; what the data show is correlation. Several of his categories look more like talents, personality traits, or cultural values than intelligences as psychologists define the term. And the theory was built more on philosophical argument and biographical case studies than on controlled experimental evidence.

Why it spread — and what to take from it

Gardner’s theory spread so widely in part because it offered teachers something the psychometric literature did not: a framework for valuing students who did not thrive in traditionally academic settings, and a vocabulary for talking about human ability that felt more generous and more humane than an IQ score. Those are real needs. The theory met them even though the specific nine-intelligence architecture isn’t warranted by the evidence.

The honest takeaway: Gardner’s broadest message — look for ability in many places; design varied learning experiences; don’t mistake narrow academic performance for the full range of human cognitive potential — is both valid and important. The specific framework should be held lightly. What the research actually supports is varied instruction — using multiple modalities, contexts, and types of tasks — because it benefits all learners, not because it matches individual intelligence profiles. In short: teach as if Gardner’s instinct is right. Don’t teach as if his nine-category taxonomy is empirically established. It isn’t.

Retrieval practiceA principal announces that all lesson plans must identify which of Gardner’s intelligences is being addressed. As a teacher who has read this chapter, what would you say — respectfully but clearly — in response to this directive?►

A respectful response might acknowledge the valid educational instinct while being honest about the evidence: “I understand the spirit of this — making sure we design varied learning experiences and reach students with different strengths is genuinely important. But the specific Gardner taxonomy has real problems as an organizing framework for lesson design. The core empirical issue is that Gardner’s intelligences are not actually independent: students who do well on linguistic tasks tend to do better than average on logical-mathematical, spatial, and other tasks too. This is the positive manifold, and it’s one of the most replicated findings in psychology. What the research actually supports is varied instruction — using multiple modalities, entry points, and types of tasks — because it benefits all learners regardless of their specific profile. Could we frame the lesson planning requirement around varied modalities rather than the nine-intelligence taxonomy? That would accomplish the real goal without building on a framework the evidence doesn’t support.”

Major concept 4 · IQ

What It Measures, What It Predicts, and When It’s Useful

An IQ score is a standardized measure of performance on a battery of cognitive tasks, normed so that the average score is 100 and approximately two-thirds of the population falls between 85 and 115. It is not a direct measure of intelligence — no single number could be. It is a useful approximation of general cognitive ability, with real predictive validity for a range of important life outcomes. What IQ tests actually measure is performance on tasks requiring reasoning, working memory, verbal ability, processing speed, and spatial cognition — the cognitive skills that tend to cluster together under g. They do not measure creativity, motivation, character, interpersonal skill, or domain-specific knowledge.

In the context of psychological research, where effect sizes of .20 are considered medium and .30 are considered large, correlations of .50 and above are exceptional. IQ is among the most robust predictors of life outcomes in all of psychology. That said, correlations are not destinies. An IQ score predicts outcomes at the population level. It does not determine outcomes for any individual. The range of outcomes for people at any given IQ level is wide. Motivation, opportunity, instruction quality, social support, and circumstance all play significant roles that IQ scores do not capture.

Holding the data honestly

Two mistakes are equally available here. The first is to dismiss IQ data as meaningless or socially constructed — which is not what the evidence supports. The second is to treat IQ as destiny — which is also not what the evidence supports. The honest position is that IQ scores predict real outcomes with real consistency, and that those scores themselves are shaped by real environmental conditions that teachers and institutions can influence. A student’s measured cognitive ability at age eight is informative. It is not a ceiling. And the conditions that produced it are exactly what schools are positioned to address.

Legitimate Uses: Special Education Identification

One of the most important legitimate uses of IQ testing is in the identification of students who qualify for special education services. Intellectual disability is formally defined in part by significantly below-average intellectual functioning — typically two or more standard deviations below the mean, corresponding to an IQ score below approximately 70 — alongside deficits in adaptive behavior. IQ testing is a required component of this determination in most jurisdictions. At the other end of the distribution, IQ testing is used to identify students for gifted and talented programs — though this use is more contested, in part because gifted identification has historically been shaped by race and socioeconomic status in ways that reflect access to test preparation and cultural alignment rather than underlying ability alone.

What teachers should know about IQ scores

If you teach students who have been formally assessed, you may encounter IQ scores in cumulative records, eligibility reports, or IEP documentation. A few things worth knowing: a score of 100 is exactly average. A score of 85 is one standard deviation below average (roughly the 16th percentile). A score of 115 is one standard deviation above (roughly the 84th percentile). Scores below 70 are associated with intellectual disability classifications. Scores above 130 are associated with gifted classification.

What the score does not tell you: how hard the student works, how much they know about the specific content you are teaching, what their strengths are within the profile, how they respond to different types of instruction, or what they are capable of becoming with high-quality teaching. Use the number as one piece of a larger picture — never as the picture itself. Every child is deserving of dignity, high expectations, and high-quality education, regardless of their IQ score.

Retrieval practiceA student’s IQ score is 115. What does this predict — with what degree of certainty, and for which outcomes? And what does it tell you almost nothing about that still matters enormously for this student’s success in your class?►

What it predicts: A score of 115 (one standard deviation above average, roughly the 84th percentile) is associated with above-average educational attainment, above-average job performance in cognitively complex roles, and stronger health outcomes on average. At the population level, a student at this score is more likely than average to complete college and succeed in demanding academic environments. These are probabilistic statements based on group-level correlations — not guarantees.

Degree of certainty: Meaningful but not high. The correlations between IQ and academic outcomes run .50–.70, which is strong by psychological research standards, but still leaves substantial unexplained variance. Many people with IQs of 115 underperform relative to expectations; many people with IQs of 85 outperform. The correlation is useful for understanding populations. It is much weaker as a guide to individual outcomes.

What it tells you almost nothing about: How hard this student works, how much they know about your specific content, whether they have been taught the prerequisites, what their specific skill profile looks like within the IQ composite, how they respond to different types of instruction, their motivation, their resilience, their relationship to school, or what they might become with excellent teaching. All of those things are at least as important as the number — and none of them are captured by it.

Major concept 5 · The Flynn Effect

Intelligence Responds to Environment

In the 1980s, researcher James Flynn documented one of the most striking findings in the history of intelligence research: IQ scores had been rising steadily across the developed world for decades — approximately 3 points per decade, with gains of 5–6 points per decade on fluid reasoning tests like Raven’s Progressive Matrices. Across the 20th century, average scores rose by roughly 30 points. That is two full standard deviations — a shift so large that a person who scored average in 1900 would be classified as intellectually disabled by 2000’s standards.

This finding — now called the Flynn effect — carries an implication that cannot be overstated: measured intelligence is not fixed. It rises and falls in response to environmental conditions. The gains are too large and too rapid to be explained by genetic change. Something about the environment — better nutrition, expanded education, reduced exposure to toxins, more cognitively demanding daily life, smaller family sizes — was raising measured cognitive ability across entire populations within a single generation.

The Flynn effect’s recent reversal

Since the 1990s, the Flynn effect has plateaued or reversed in several developed nations — Norway, Denmark, Finland, Britain, France, and others have all documented declining scores. A 2023 study in Intelligence found similar declines in abstract reasoning and verbal comprehension among younger Americans.

The most important study is Bratsberg and Rogeberg (2018), which showed the reversal occurring even within families — siblings born later scoring lower than siblings born earlier. That within-family finding rules out genetics. The cause of both the rise and the fall is environmental.

What changed is not yet fully settled, but the candidates are familiar: less reading, more passive screen consumption, shifts away from cognitively demanding instruction. The screen ecosystem is a plausible contributor — short-form video does not exercise the same cognitive muscles as sustained reading or complex problem-solving.

The practical implication is the one that matters most for teachers: if the gains were environmental, the losses are environmental too. That makes them, at least in principle, reversible. The question of what school environments should look like to support cognitive development is not separate from this data. It is exactly what this data is about.

Retrieval practiceThe Bratsberg and Rogeberg (2018) study showed the Flynn reversal occurring within families — siblings born later scored lower than siblings born earlier. Why is a within-family finding particularly significant — and what does it rule out as an explanation for the reversal?►

A within-family analysis holds genetic factors and many stable environmental factors constant. Siblings share roughly 50% of their genetic material and typically share the same household, neighborhood, socioeconomic status, parental education level, and broad cultural context. When siblings from the same family, raised in the same home, show a consistent IQ decline across birth years, the explanation cannot be genetic — the genes haven’t changed between siblings. It also cannot be stable environmental factors like family income or parental education, since those don’t systematically differ between siblings born a few years apart.

What the within-family design rules out: genetic explanations, and most stable socioeconomic confounds. What remains are environmental changes that affect younger siblings differently from older ones — specifically, changes that occurred in the broader cultural and educational environment between the older sibling’s childhood and the younger sibling’s. This points strongly to changes in how children spend their time: what they read, how they learn, what kinds of cognitive demands their daily activities make on them. The IQ decline, like the Flynn rise before it, is an environmental story. The environment built the gains and — if the reversal research holds — is now eroding them.

Major concept 6 · What Raises IQ

Environmental Levers — and What Teachers Can Do

If the Flynn effect demonstrates that measured intelligence responds to environmental conditions, the next question is: which conditions? The research on this is clearer and more actionable than many teachers realize. Cognitive ability is not a fixed endowment. It is a developmental outcome, shaped by the accumulation of experiences, exposures, and opportunities across childhood and adolescence.

The teacher’s leverage

None of the factors above are beyond the reach of schools. Some are systemic — lead remediation, nutrition programs, early childhood investment — and require advocacy beyond the classroom. But several are directly in the teacher’s hands: the richness of language in instruction, the quality of vocabulary teaching, the predictability and warmth of the classroom environment, the cognitive demand of the work, and the consistency of high expectations. The research on what raises IQ is also, not coincidentally, a description of what good teaching looks like. Cognitively rich instruction, high expectations, a safe and predictable environment, and sustained engagement with complex material are not just best practices for achievement. They are, in a meaningful sense, cognitive development.

The effect of schooling on measured intelligence deserves its own emphasis. The evidence that formal schooling raises IQ is robust — not just in correlational studies, but in natural experiments exploiting variation in school attendance due to compulsory schooling laws, starting age cutoffs, and calendar length. Children who get more school — by accident of birthdate or geography — score higher on cognitive assessments.

The mechanism appears to be genuine cognitive development, not just test familiarity. School provides sustained practice with exactly the kinds of tasks that IQ tests measure: abstract reasoning, sustained attention, working with novel problems, holding information in mind while manipulating it. It also provides the vocabulary, background knowledge, and structured thinking practice that crystallized intelligence reflects. In this sense, schooling does not merely prepare students to perform on tests. It builds the cognitive architecture that IQ tests are designed to measure.

This means that every child receiving mediocre instruction for a day is, in a small but real way, a cognitive development opportunity lost. And it means that the quality of what happens during school — the cognitive demand, the language richness, the instructional effectiveness — matters not just for academic achievement but for the broader development of the minds in the room.

Retrieval practiceA new teacher says: “I can’t control my students’ intelligence — I can only help them maximize what they’ve got.” Using the research from this concept, identify two things that are right about this framing, and two things that a more accurate understanding would correct.►

What’s right: The framing correctly implies that teachers shouldn’t expect to produce instant dramatic IQ changes in individual students — cognitive development is cumulative, not instantaneous. It also correctly implies that teachers should focus on the quality of instruction rather than waiting for “smarter” students. Both of these are reasonable starting points.

What a more accurate understanding would correct: First, the framing treats intelligence as a fixed input rather than a developmental outcome. The research is clear: each additional year of schooling raises measured IQ by 1–5 points; vocabulary instruction, cognitively rich discussion, reduced stress, and consistent high expectations all measurably support cognitive development. The teacher is not just working with what students “have got” — they are actively shaping it, cumulatively, over time. Second, the framing frames intelligence as something entirely outside the teacher’s influence. What’s actually in teachers’ hands — language environment, cognitive demand, emotional safety, high expectations — are not small things. They are some of the most powerful levers the research has identified.

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

The research on intelligence is not primarily a source of constraints on what teachers can do. It is a description of how consequential what teachers do actually is.

  • Never treat an IQ score as a ceiling. Use it as one data point in a larger picture. The score reflects a developmental history — not a fixed limit. Your job is to find out what the student knows and teach from there.
  • Distinguish knowledge gaps from capacity limits. A student who struggles with a topic may have thin crystallized knowledge, not low fluid intelligence. These are not the same thing and they call for different responses. A knowledge gap calls for instruction, not adjusted expectations.
  • Teach as if Gardner’s instinct is right; don’t build lesson plans around his nine-category taxonomy. Varied instruction — multiple modalities, entry points, and types of tasks — benefits all learners. The framework doesn’t hold up; the underlying value of variety does.
  • Invest in the language environment of your classroom. Vocabulary instruction, rich discussion, and reading across content areas are among the highest-leverage cognitive development activities available to a teacher. This is not supplemental to “real” instruction — it is instruction at its most consequential.
  • Make your classroom predictably safe. Chronic stress impairs prefrontal cortex function and working memory measurably. A relationally warm, stable, low-threat classroom is not a soft priority — it is a prerequisite for the cognitive work you are trying to do.
  • Understand that measured gaps between groups reflect circumstance, not capacity. The same environmental factors that produced the Flynn effect — richer language, better nutrition, higher-quality schooling — are the factors that close gaps. You are one of those factors.
Chapter synthesis
Intelligence is real, measurable, contested, and malleable — and holding all four of those things at once is exactly what the evidence requires. g exists and is predictive. Heritability is real and widely misunderstood. The Flynn effect demonstrates that 30 points of IQ can be built and lost in a single generation through environmental change. Gardner’s instinct is educationally valuable; his nine-category architecture is not empirically established. And the factors that measurably raise IQ — schooling, language, safety, nutrition, reduced stress — are a description of what good teachers do every day.
The positive manifold — that scores on all cognitive tests correlate positively — is one of the most replicated findings in psychology. It supports the existence of g. It does not mean intelligence is fixed.
Fluid intelligence (reasoning capacity) and crystallized intelligence (accumulated knowledge) are distinct. Confusing a Gc gap with a Gf ceiling is one of the most consequential errors in classroom practice — and one of the most common.
Heritability is not fixity. Height is highly heritable and rose dramatically in response to improved nutrition. High heritability estimates for IQ are fully compatible with — and documented alongside — strong environmental effects on measured intelligence.
The Flynn effect’s reversal in developed nations since the 1990s, confirmed by within-family studies that rule out genetics, points to environmental changes in how children spend their time. If the gains were environmental, the losses are too — which means they are reversible.
What raises IQ — schooling, language richness, safety, nutrition, high expectations — is a description of what good teaching looks like. The distinction between “raising cognitive ability” and “teaching well” is smaller than most people assume.
