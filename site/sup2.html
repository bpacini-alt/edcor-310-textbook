Quick Reference · The Scientific Method
Step What it means Example
1. Observe Notice something in the world that raises a question. Good science starts with careful, honest attention to what is actually happening. Students in one class seem to retain vocabulary better than students in another class taught by a different teacher.
2. Ask a Question Narrow the observation into a specific, answerable question. The question should be testable — not a matter of opinion or values. "Does spaced practice produce better vocabulary retention than massed practice?"
3. Form a Hypothesis Propose a specific, falsifiable prediction. A good hypothesis can be proven wrong — if it can't be, it isn't scientific. "Students who review vocabulary across three sessions will retain more words after two weeks than students who study the same amount in one session."
4. Design a Study Plan how to test the hypothesis while controlling for other variables. Decide who the participants are, what will be measured, and how. Randomly assign 60 students to spaced or massed practice groups, hold total study time constant, and test retention at two weeks.
5. Collect Data Carry out the study and record results systematically and honestly — including results that don't support the hypothesis. Administer the same vocabulary test to both groups under the same conditions and record each student's score.
6. Analyze Results Look for patterns in the data. Statistical analysis helps determine whether differences between groups are real or likely due to chance. The spaced group averaged 82%; the massed group averaged 67%. Statistical tests show this difference is unlikely to be random.
7. Draw Conclusions Interpret what the results mean — and what they don't mean. One study rarely settles a question. Conclusions should be proportionate to the evidence. "This study supports spaced practice for vocabulary retention, but replication across different age groups and subjects is needed before drawing broad conclusions."
8. Share & Replicate Publish findings so others can scrutinize, challenge, and attempt to reproduce them. Scientific knowledge is built through repeated independent confirmation — not a single study. Other researchers run similar studies in different schools and subjects. If results hold across contexts, confidence in the finding grows.
