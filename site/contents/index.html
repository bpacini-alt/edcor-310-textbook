I

Unit 1 · Chapters 1–8

Intro to Psychology

Every course has to start somewhere, and this one starts with you. Before you can evaluate a teaching strategy, a research claim, or a neuromyth, you have to reckon with the instrument doing the evaluating: your own mind.

Unit outcomes (4)
  1. Evaluate a teaching strategy or educational claim using the evidence hierarchy, distinguishing statistical significance from practical significance. CLO 1 CLO 4
  2. Identify the cognitive biases most likely to distort a teacher's judgment, and explain how the scientific method is structured to compensate for them. CLO 1
  3. Evaluate a popular claim or neuromyth in educational psychology by tracing it to its original evidence. CLO 4
  4. Propose evidence-based classroom practices that support student and teacher mental health and flourishing. CLO 5 CLO 1

II

Unit 2 · Chapters 9–14

Developmental Psychology

Unit 1 gave you a way to weigh evidence and catch your own blind spots. Now point those tools at a moving target: the developing child.

Unit outcomes (4)
  1. Apply principles of child and adolescent cognitive development to the design of an age-appropriate learning experience. CLO 3
  2. Apply research on moral development to explain how adolescents reason about right and wrong, and what that implies for classroom practice. CLO 3
  3. Evaluate competing claims about the relative influence of parenting versus genetics and peers, using the standards of evidence from Unit 1. CLO 1 CLO 3
  4. Apply principles of identity development to explain how a student's sense of self shapes their classroom engagement and behavior. CLO 3

III

Unit 3 · Chapters 15–20

Motivational Psychology

Unit 2 explained how students grow. This unit explains why they try — or don't.

Unit outcomes (4)
  1. Identify strategies to build students' sense of agency and self-efficacy, and explain why belief in one's own agency predicts outcomes as strongly as it does. CLO 2 CLO 3
  2. Distinguish extrinsic and intrinsic motivation, and identify classroom practices that build intrinsic motivation without undermining it. CLO 2
  3. Propose solutions for fostering belonging in a classroom, informed by principles from sociology and anthropology. CLO 5 CLO 2
  4. Evaluate a motivational strategy against the research evidence on when it helps versus backfires. CLO 1 CLO 2

IV

Unit 4 · Chapters 21–26

Cognitive Psychology

Units 1 through 3 covered why students believe what they believe, how they develop, and what drives them to engage. This final unit turns to the machinery underneath all of it: attention, memory, and cognitive load as tools you design around, not just concepts to memorize.

Unit outcomes (4)
  1. Apply principles of attention, memory, and cognitive load to design instruction that reduces extraneous load and supports retention. CLO 3
  2. Evaluate common claims about intelligence and IQ against the research evidence. CLO 4
  3. Propose accommodations or instructional adjustments that address learner differences, including executive function and attention-related needs. CLO 5 CLO 3
  4. Evaluate why well-supported research findings often fail to reach classroom practice, and propose a strategy for closing that gap in your own teaching. CLO 1 CLO 5
