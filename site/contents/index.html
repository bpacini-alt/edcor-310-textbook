I

Unit 1 · Chapters 1–8

Intro to Psychology

Every course has to start somewhere, and this one starts with you. Before you can evaluate a teaching strategy, a research claim, or a neuromyth, you have to reckon with the instrument doing the evaluating: your own mind. This unit hands you the tools of evidence and exposes the biases that quietly work against using them well.

That foundation is not optional groundwork to get through before the "real" content starts. It is the lens the rest of this course asks you to look through — starting with how children and adolescents actually develop, which is where Unit 2 picks up.

Unit outcomes (4)
  1. Evaluate a teaching strategy or educational claim using the evidence hierarchy, distinguishing statistical significance from practical significance. CLO 1 CLO 4
  2. Identify the cognitive biases most likely to distort a teacher's judgment, and explain how the scientific method is structured to compensate for them. CLO 1
  3. Evaluate a popular claim or neuromyth in educational psychology by tracing it to its original evidence. CLO 4
  4. Propose evidence-based classroom practices that support student and teacher mental health and flourishing. CLO 5 CLO 1

II

Unit 2 · Chapters 9–14

Developmental Psychology

Unit 1 gave you a way to weigh evidence and catch your own blind spots. Now point those tools at a moving target: the developing child. This unit traces the arc of a student's thinking from constructivism through social learning, follows adolescent moral reasoning as it takes a shape adults often misread, and shows how a student's sense of identity steers their behavior before you ever say a word.

Understanding how students develop is what makes the next question answerable: once you know where a student is developmentally, what actually moves them to engage? That's where Unit 3 goes next.

Unit outcomes (4)
  1. Apply principles of child and adolescent cognitive development to the design of an age-appropriate learning experience. CLO 3
  2. Apply research on moral development to explain how adolescents reason about right and wrong, and what that implies for classroom practice. CLO 3
  3. Evaluate competing claims about the relative influence of parenting versus genetics and peers, using the standards of evidence from Unit 1. CLO 1 CLO 3
  4. Apply principles of identity development to explain how a student's sense of self shapes their classroom engagement and behavior. CLO 3

III

Unit 3 · Chapters 15–20

Motivational Psychology

Unit 2 explained how students grow. This unit explains why they try — or don't. You'll dig into agency and self-efficacy, the fragile difference between motivation that lasts and motivation that a reward quietly kills, and the pull of belonging that shapes whether a student shows up ready to learn or shows up ready to disappear.

Motivation research answers why a student engages, but it doesn't fully answer how their mind processes what happens next once they do. That mechanical question — attention, memory, load — is where the course closes, in Unit 4.

Unit outcomes (4)
  1. Identify strategies to build students' sense of agency and self-efficacy, and explain why belief in one's own agency predicts outcomes as strongly as it does. CLO 2 CLO 3
  2. Distinguish extrinsic and intrinsic motivation, and identify classroom practices that build intrinsic motivation without undermining it. CLO 2
  3. Propose solutions for fostering belonging in a classroom, informed by principles from sociology and anthropology. CLO 5 CLO 2
  4. Evaluate a motivational strategy against the research evidence on when it helps versus backfires. CLO 1 CLO 2

IV

Unit 4 · Chapters 21–26

Cognitive Psychology

Units 1 through 3 covered why students believe what they believe, how they develop, and what drives them to engage. This final unit turns to the machinery underneath all of it: attention, memory, and cognitive load as tools you design around, not just concepts to memorize. You'll pressure-test popular claims about intelligence and learning styles, and confront the gap between what research has settled and what actually reaches classrooms.

That gap is the note this course ends on deliberately. Everything in Units 1 through 3 is only useful to the extent it changes what you actually do in a classroom — which is the same test this unit, and this course, ultimately asks of you.

Unit outcomes (4)
  1. Apply principles of attention, memory, and cognitive load to design instruction that reduces extraneous load and supports retention. CLO 3
  2. Evaluate common claims about intelligence and IQ against the research evidence. CLO 4
  3. Propose accommodations or instructional adjustments that address learner differences, including executive function and attention-related needs. CLO 5 CLO 3
  4. Evaluate why well-supported research findings often fail to reach classroom practice, and propose a strategy for closing that gap in your own teaching. CLO 1 CLO 5
