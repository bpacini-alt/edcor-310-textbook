# Chapter Quality Manual: Readiness, Revision, and Triage

**Status:** Approved by Benjamin, October 30, 2026. First used on EDCOR 310's Chapter 16 rewrite.  
**Applies to:** Econ 150 and EDCOR 310  
**Purpose:** Improve chapters without creating an endless revision cycle.

This manual supports two common requests:

1. **Publication pass:** decide whether a chapter is ready to publish.
2. **Comparative evaluation:** evaluate a selected group of chapters and identify which are ready, which need targeted work, and which cannot be published yet.

It is a decision tool, not a demand for perfection.

## Governing principle

A chapter is ready when it is **trustworthy, teachable, understandable, and usable**. It does not need to be flawless.

The protocol should be comprehensive in what it can notice, but selective in what it requires us to fix. A small improvement completed now is often more valuable than a perfect revision that prevents the book from moving forward.

Ask:

> What small number of changes would most improve this chapter for students right now?

Do not ask:

> What could an ideal editor possibly improve?

## The bounded-review rules

### 1. Separate findings from obligations

Every observation belongs in one of three buckets:

- **Fix before publish:** a serious problem with accuracy, student understanding, trust, accessibility, or usability.
- **Worth fixing next:** a meaningful improvement that should enter the next revision queue.
- **Record for later:** a plausible improvement that does not justify interrupting current work.

Only the first bucket blocks publication.

### 2. Keep the immediate list short

A normal publication pass should end with no more than **three to five immediate actions**. If there are more, combine related items, identify the true underlying problem, or move lower-value items to the backlog.

A chapter may contain more notes than this. It should not contain more than this number of required next steps.

### 3. Use a stopping rule

Stop the pass when:

- the central explanation is accurate enough to teach;
- students can identify what the chapter is trying to explain;
- no major unsupported, misleading, or badly outdated claim remains;
- the chapter's core examples and practice are usable;
- serious accessibility or rendering problems are absent; and
- the remaining issues are polish, preference, or future enrichment.

At that point, publish or move on.

### 4. Match the depth of work to the problem

Some chapters need a light polish. Some need a targeted repair. Some need a full rewrite. The manual should help us tell the difference rather than impose the same amount of work on every chapter.

Investigate further when a problem is central, consequential, controversial, unusually surprising, or likely to mislead students. A full rewrite is justified when the chapter's structure, teaching purpose, evidence, or student usability is substantially broken—not merely because many sentences could be improved.

Do not verify every minor citation at publication-level depth. Do not turn one interesting question into a new research project unless it changes the chapter's central teaching claim. Conversely, do not stop investigating a serious problem merely because a nominal time limit has been reached.

The controlling question is:

> Is this work likely to produce a meaningfully better book for students, and can we define what “better” will require?

### 5. Preserve momentum

Do not reopen a settled chapter because a later pass discovers a merely better sentence, example, title, or activity. Add it to the backlog unless it materially affects student understanding.

## Mode A: Publication pass

A publication pass produces a short report with:

- **Decision:** Ready / Ready after small fixes / Not ready
- **Immediate actions:** no more than three to five
- **Next revision:** worthwhile items that should wait
- **Parking lot:** ideas for later
- **Confidence note:** any important uncertainty or evidence limitation

### A. Core teaching job

- What is the chapter trying to help students understand or do?
- Is that purpose visible early enough?
- Does each major section serve the chapter's central job?
- Are the key terms and distinctions explicit?
- Is the ending consistent with the argument rather than merely stopping?

### B. Accuracy and evidence

Check only claims that matter to the chapter's teaching job, plus any claims that are especially strong or surprising.

Ask:

- Is the claim accurate?
- Is it stated with the right level of certainty?
- Is the evidence current enough for the claim?
- Is there a primary or otherwise appropriate source?
- Does the chapter distinguish one study from a broader evidence base?
- Has the central finding been replicated, independently supported, contested, or superseded?
- Does the prose accurately represent what the cited source actually shows?

Do not use “not perfectly replicated” as an automatic reason to remove a useful study. The goal is calibrated confidence, not a simplistic pass/fail label.

### C. Student clarity

Read as a student encountering the idea for the first time.

Look for:

- unexplained terms;
- long or overloaded sentences;
- abstract claims without an example;
- jumps in reasoning;
- pronouns with unclear referents;
- unnecessary qualification before the basic idea is clear;
- paragraphs that make several claims at once;
- places where a student could reasonably misunderstand the point.

Prefer concrete wording, short explanations, and examples that do real conceptual work.

### D. Structure and pacing

Ask:

- Does the chapter earn its length?
- Is the order of ideas doing useful work?
- Are the most important ideas receiving the most attention?
- Are hooks, stories, studies, figures, and side notes serving the central explanation?
- Could a student tell what to remember after each major section?
- Is anything redundant enough to cut?

Do not shorten merely to make a chapter shorter. Cut or move material when it obscures the main idea or weakens the pace.

### E. Writing and voice

The chapter should sound like a thoughtful human teacher: clear, specific, lively, and willing to make an argument.

Flag:

- generic openings;
- repetitive “not X, but Y” constructions;
- reveal-pair or drumroll sentences;
- strings of fragments;
- excessive three-part lists;
- abstract nouns where a concrete verb would be clearer;
- transitions that announce rather than explain;
- paragraphs that sound polished but say little;
- repeated AI-like cadence.

Do not flatten the author's personality or remove an effective rhetorical move merely because it is stylized. The issue is accumulated pattern, not one sentence.

### F. Practice and learning design — tentative, not currently scored

Practice and learning design remains an important future dimension, but it is excluded from the current numerical rubric. The recurring practice system is still being designed, so chapters should not be penalized for failing a standard we have not yet settled.

When the practice framework is established, add this dimension back to the rubric or evaluate it separately. Until then, record obvious practice needs as future work without allowing them to determine publication status.

### G. Accessibility and student use

Check the things students will actually encounter:

- heading hierarchy;
- readable links and references;
- alt text or equivalent descriptions where needed;
- figures that remain legible on phones;
- usable tables and equations;
- contrast and spacing;
- keyboard and screen-reader basics for interactive elements;
- no sideways scrolling or overlapping controls;
- print/text-only behavior where relevant.

Fix serious barriers now. Record minor design preferences for a later book-wide pass.

## Mode B: Comparative chapter evaluation

Use the same manual when evaluating several chapters together. The evaluator may choose four chapters, a unit, a whole book, or any other useful comparison set. There is no fixed number.

The goal is not to produce a repair list for every chapter. The goal is to sort the selected chapters into useful readiness categories and identify where the next revision effort will have the greatest student payoff.

For each chapter, record a rough rating:

| Dimension | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy and evidence | Serious concern | Some concerns | Generally sound | Strong and well-calibrated |
| Student clarity | Frequently confusing | Uneven | Usually clear | Consistently clear |
| Structure and pacing | Needs reconstruction | Significant drag or gaps | Works with some roughness | Strong |
| Writing and voice | Generic or difficult | Uneven | Mostly engaging | Distinctive and compelling |
| Accessibility and presentation | Serious barriers | Several problems | Mostly usable | Clean and reliable |

The current rubric is worth **90 points**:

| Dimension | Points |
|---|---:|
| Conceptual accuracy and nuance | 20 |
| Evidence quality and calibration | 20 |
| Student clarity and reading level | 15 |
| Structure and pacing | 15 |
| Compelling human writing | 10 |
| Chapter purpose and coherence | 5 |
| Accessibility and presentation | 5 |
| **Total** | **90** |

Convert each 1–4 rating into points with: `rating ÷ 4 × category points`.

As a rough guide, a chapter scoring **77–90** is strong enough to be ready for now, **63–76** is generally publishable with targeted fixes, and a score below **63** signals substantial revision. These ranges never override a serious publication blocker.

When the practice system is settled, practice may be restored as a separate 10-point dimension, bringing the rubric back to 100 points.

Then assign:

- **Publication status:** Cannot publish yet / Publishable with targeted fixes / Ready for now
- **Revision scale:** Light polish / Targeted revision / Substantial rewrite / Full rewrite
- **Likely payoff:** High / Medium / Low

The evaluation should identify:

- chapters that cannot be published in their current form;
- chapters that are publishable but need targeted fixes;
- chapters that are ready for now; and
- the chapter or chapters where revision would produce the greatest student benefit.

A low score does not automatically mean “work on this first.” Prioritize chapters where:

- the problem affects many students;
- the central idea is important;
- a bounded revision could substantially help;
- the chapter is not blocked by a larger unresolved decision; and
- the likely payoff justifies the scale of the work.

The rubric is a comparative aid, not a mechanical verdict. A chapter with a lower average score may still be the wrong next project if it requires a large rewrite with little immediate payoff. A chapter with one severe weakness may deserve priority even if its average score is otherwise strong.

## What counts as a publication blocker?

A blocker is a problem that would make publishing the current chapter materially irresponsible or unhelpful. Examples include:

- a central factual error;
- a claim presented as settled when the evidence is seriously disputed;
- a missing qualification that changes the meaning;
- a structure that makes the central idea inaccessible;
- a broken or misleading figure, calculation, or exercise;
- an important citation that does not support the claim;
- a serious accessibility or mobile-rendering failure;
- a practice activity that teaches or reinforces the wrong answer.

These are not blockers:

- a sentence that could be more elegant;
- a potentially better example;
- a missing enrichment section;
- a study that could be added;
- a preference about headings or box styles;
- a minor inconsistency better handled in a book-wide sweep.

## What the final report should look like

Keep the report short:

> **Decision:** Ready after small fixes  
>
> **Fix before publish**
> 1. Qualify the opening claim about ___.
> 2. Add the missing explanation after Figure ___.
> 3. Repair the mobile layout of ___.
>
> **Worth fixing next:** The transition between ___ and ___ is abrupt.
>
> **Record for later:** Consider adding a short comparison with ___ .
>
> **Confidence note:** The evidence for ___ is mixed; the current wording is appropriately cautious.

## Econ and EDCOR: shared protocol, different emphasis

The books should use the same **decision structure**, severity labels, stopping rules, triage scale, and report format. That consistency reduces cognitive load and makes book-wide prioritization possible.

They should not use identical substantive checklists.

### Econ 150 deserves extra attention to:

- assumptions and boundary conditions;
- graph-to-prose and prose-to-graph consistency;
- calculations, units, labels, and numerical examples;
- positive versus normative claims;
- policy tradeoffs and unintended consequences;
- whether examples accidentally imply a universal economic law;
- current laws, programs, prices, and institutional facts.

### EDCOR 310 deserves extra attention to:

- the difference between a theory, an interpretive lens, and a guaranteed prescription;
- research design, measurement, and limits of inference;
- whether a famous finding is being overstated;
- translating research into classroom or professional action;
- ethical, cultural, and contextual complexity;
- whether practical advice is evidence-aligned but appropriately modest;
- the distinction between scientific evidence and personal, religious, or professional judgment.

The important answer is therefore:

> The two books should share one quality-control architecture, but they should not be forced through identical content checks.

## How to invoke the manual

### Single-chapter request

> Review this chapter using the Chapter Quality Manual. Tell me whether it is ready to publish, identify the small number of changes that matter most, separate blockers from later improvements, and recommend whether the work is a light polish, targeted revision, substantial revision, or full rewrite.

### Comparative request

> Evaluate these chapters using the Chapter Quality Manual. Give each one a readiness status and rubric scores, explain the most important weakness or strength, identify which cannot be published yet, and recommend where I should spend revision time first.

The response should remain concise enough to act on. A comprehensive review does not require reporting every possible improvement.

## Proposed first use

Test the protocol on:

1. one chapter we believe is already strong;
2. one chapter we suspect needs substantial work; and
3. one chapter from the other book.

Then test a comparative request on any useful group of chapters. If the process produces a long list but no clear next action, the protocol is too elaborate. If it misses an obvious substantive problem, strengthen the relevant check. The test is whether it helps us make and finish good revisions.
