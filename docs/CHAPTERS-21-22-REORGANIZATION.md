# Chapters 21–22 reorganization

**Status:** Planned November 24, 2026. Benjamin approved the new split. Nothing has been moved yet. This is a working document, so delete it once the reorganization merges.

**For:** Mayleigh, Benjamin, and anyone else working on these two chapters.

## Why we're doing this

Chapter 21 (Executive Function & ADHD) and Chapter 22 (Attention, Memory & Cognitive Load) were blending together. Here's where:

- **Working memory is taught twice.** Chapter 21 presents it as one of the three executive function skills, the one that keeps your plan or the steps in mind. Chapter 22 presents it as a small workspace that holds about four chunks. Neither chapter explains that these are the same system.
- **Chapter 21 teaches cognitive load theory before the book names it.** The "Good evidence" supports (posted schedules, written directions, small steps) are cuts to extraneous load. Then Chapter 22's three cognitive load questions give the same advice again.
- **"Chunks" means opposite things.** In Chapter 21, "smaller chunks of work" means breaking a task apart. In Chapter 22, chunking means grouping pieces into one unit. Quiz items show students mixing these up.
- **The ADHD discussion is split.** Chapter 22's perceptual load section explains why a student focuses on a hard task and drifts on a dull one. It also cites an ADHD trial, but it never connects either point to Chapter 21.
- **Chapter 22 is in the wrong order.** The cognitive load section opens with "Everything so far…" and goes back to working memory, skipping over the long Learning Strategies section that sits in between.

## The new split

The organizing question is: **where did the learning break down?**

| Chapter | Breakdown | Practical payoff |
|---|---|---|
| **21: Attention & Executive Function** | It didn't get in. It didn't get managed. | Focus and follow-through: supports and systems that fade as the student's skill grows |
| **22: Working Memory & Long-Term Memory** | It didn't fit. It didn't stick. | Cutting extraneous load, then retrieval and spacing |

Managing comes first because most of attention is executive function at work. Choosing where to aim the spotlight and ignoring the hallway noise are both inhibitory control (Diamond, 2013, which Chapter 21 already cites).

The CEO metaphor already in Chapter 21 can carry the whole split. Working memory is the desk. Cognitive load is how much paper the lesson piles on it. Executive function is the assistant who keeps the right papers on top. So Chapter 22's cognitive load material is about **the lesson**, which is the same for every student. Chapter 21's material is about **the student**, whose skill varies and is still developing.

**A rule for where distractions go:** things that compete with the lesson from outside (phones, decorated walls, hallway noise) go in Chapter 21. Things built into the lesson (seductive details, cluttered slides, directions split between the board and a handout) go in Chapter 22.

## Is this content or just reorganizing?

It's mostly reorganizing. Almost every paragraph moves as written. There are a few small writing edits, plus one bigger decision (the trim).

### Small writing edits

1. **Merge the attention concepts.** Write a short opening for the merged concept and one eyebrow. Cut any repetition at the seam between the old selective attention and perceptual load sections.
2. **Working memory bridge.** In Chapter 21, add one sentence noting that Chapter 22 covers how much working memory holds, while here the focus is using it to keep the plan in mind. In Chapter 22, adjust the hook to match.
3. **"Chunks" → "steps" in Chapter 21.** This happens in three places: the executive dysfunction paragraph, the evidence table, and the strategy box. The chapter already says "small, labeled steps" elsewhere. Once this is done, the "two meanings of chunking" note from PR #172 can shrink to one line or be dropped.
4. **Reconcile desirable difficulties with cognitive load theory.** Right now, one section says to make learning harder and the next says to make it lighter. One paragraph can resolve this: keep the effort on remembering the content, and cut every other source of difficulty. The existing line "A confusing worksheet is hard too…" is the seed for it.
5. **Cross-reference cleanup.**
   - Chapter 21 refers to its own concepts by number ("The evidence in Concept 4," "Concept 5 covers why," and others). All of these shift by one.
   - Chapter 24 says "perceptual load theory, Chapter 22." This becomes Chapter 21.
   - Chapter 20's pointer to Chapter 21 is still correct.
