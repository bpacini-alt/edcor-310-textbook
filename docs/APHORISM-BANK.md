# Aphorism Bank

Aphorisms are load-bearing sentences: they compress a chapter’s central insight into a few memorable words. They should not become decorative slogans or recurring section furniture.

## Working rule

Keep no more than two or three aphorisms in a chapter. Add one only when it:

- expresses an important idea in unusually compact language;
- remains accurate when separated from the surrounding explanation;
- gives students or teachers something useful to remember;
- does not merely repeat a heading, definition, or application paragraph.

When a good aphorism does not fit the current chapter, keep it here with a note about where it belongs rather than forcing it into the wrong unit.

## Current Chapter 11 aphorisms

1. **We build our understanding; teachers choose the materials.**

   Constructivism’s central idea, paired with the teacher’s responsibility for designing the materials, examples, explanations, and tasks students will use to build that understanding.

2. **Your brain thinks in examples, not definitions.**

   A compact way to express the role of prototypes and exemplars in category learning.

3. **To change a schema, roll out the red carpet. Then tug on it.**

   Learners need to articulate and temporarily stand on an existing idea before instruction can help that idea give way to a better one.

The chapter’s final recap now repeats these three ideas rather than introducing a longer list of aphorisms.

## Deferred candidates

### Cognition / Willingham

> Your brain avoids thinking, so make the right decision once.
>
> For teachers: build it right the first time.

This was removed from Chapter 11. It fits better in the final cognition unit with Daniel Willingham, where it can be developed alongside cognitive effort, habits, fluency, and the value of well-designed defaults. The wording may need refinement before publication because “the brain avoids thinking” is memorable but broader than the evidence supports.

### Background knowledge

> What you already know decides what you can learn.

This was removed from Chapter 11. It belongs with the later treatment of background knowledge and prior knowledge, where the wording can be qualified: accurate and relevant knowledge supports learning, while incomplete or misleading knowledge can interfere. The aphorism is powerful, but “decides” may overstate the relationship.

## Review prompt

When adding or revising aphorisms, ask:

> If this sentence disappeared, would the chapter lose a useful mental handle—or only a nice-looking line?
