# Research Videos and Book Side Quests

**Status:** Working standard for EDCOR 310 research videos and in-chapter Research Side-Quests.  
**Purpose:** Make short research videos and their textbook companions clear, accurate, reusable, and easy to build without reinventing the workflow.

This guide covers two connected deliverables:

1. **YouTube show notes:** the public-facing explanation and source list for the video.
2. **Research Side-Quest box:** the in-chapter companion that adds context, evidence calibration, limitations, and application without simply repeating the video.

The video is the invitation. The box is the deeper, quieter research companion.

## The governing idea

Keep the claim short, useful, and calibrated.

A research video should usually:

- begin with a memorable question or tension;
- summarize a corpus of evidence rather than pretending one study settles the issue;
- distinguish what the evidence directly shows from what we are applying or inferring;
- tell viewers how strong the evidence is;
- end with a practical implication for teachers, parents, or students.

The textbook box should then add what the short video cannot:

- study designs and methods;
- limitations and alternative explanations;
- the evidence hierarchy;
- a more careful application;
- links to the primary studies or research syntheses.

Do not make the box a transcript of the video. The video should be the hook; the box should provide context.

## Workflow

### 1. Start with the transcript and the actual claim

Before writing, identify:

- the question the video asks;
- the one-sentence answer;
- the evidence the video actually mentions;
- any claim in the video that needs qualification;
- the chapter concept the video is meant to interrupt.

If the video compresses several studies into one message, say so explicitly. For example:

> This clip compresses several related findings into one classroom message.

Do not silently expand a video into a stronger claim than the recording supports.

### 2. Verify the evidence

For every study or synthesis, record:

- full citation;
- link to the primary article, PubMed record, DOI, or authoritative full text;
- sample and design;
- main finding;
- the most important limitation;
- whether the finding is correlational, longitudinal, experimental, or a research synthesis.

Use the evidence hierarchy as a thinking tool:

1. One-time observations
2. Longitudinal follow-up
3. Randomized experiments
4. Research syntheses

The hierarchy is not an automatic causality score. A meta-analysis can inherit weak or heterogeneous studies, and a well-designed experiment may answer only a narrow question. State what a study can establish and what it cannot.

### 3. Assign an evidence-strength label

Use plain-language labels sparingly:

- **Bulletproof consensus:** reserved for unusually convergent, well-established findings.
- **Strong evidence:** repeated, reasonably consistent evidence with meaningful support from stronger designs.
- **Moderate evidence:** a useful and plausible pattern, but with important limitations, mixed findings, or limited causal evidence.
- **Preliminary evidence:** promising findings that need replication or broader testing.

Always explain the reason for the label. For example:

> Overall strength: moderate. Several studies point in the same direction, but most are correlational and the mechanism remains uncertain.

Do not label a claim “strong” merely because it appears in a meta-analysis. Do not label a claim “settled” if the synthesis contains few studies, large heterogeneity, weak designs, or competing explanations.

## YouTube show notes

YouTube descriptions should be readable on their own. They should not require viewers to have watched the video or opened the textbook.

### Recommended structure

#### Opening

Start with the video’s question and a one- or two-sentence explanation of why it matters.

#### What the research says

Use two to four short paragraphs:

1. State the central finding.
2. Explain the most relevant supporting studies or synthesis.
3. Add the important qualification.
4. Translate the finding into a practical implication.

Use language such as:

- “This video compresses several related findings.”
- “The evidence does not show that…”
- “The strongest conclusion is more modest…”
- “This is consistent with…, but it does not directly establish…”

#### Evidence strength

Include a short section such as:

> **Evidence strength: moderate.** The pattern appears across several studies, but most are correlational and the evidence does not establish one universal intervention.

#### Key studies

List the most relevant sources. Each entry should include:

- author and year;
- title when useful;
- study design or synthesis type;
- what it found;
- its most important limitation;
- a direct link.

Five strong sources are usually better than fifteen undigested citations.

#### Application

Tell viewers what the evidence means for a teacher, parent, or student. Keep the advice proportional to the evidence. If the evidence is correlational, do not write as though a specific behavior has been proven to cause the outcome.

#### Textbook invitation

End with:

- the relevant chapter link;
- the main textbook link;
- a short note that the project is ongoing.

Recommended language:

> Learn more in The Science of Learning, a free and open textbook. The video is the short version; the book provides more context, methods, limitations, and applications. This is an ongoing project, and we are improving the chapters and adding research day by day.

### YouTube show-notes template

    [Question or title]

    [One-sentence hook.]

    In this short Research Side-Quest, I look at [claim or question]. The video
    compresses several related findings into a short classroom message.

    ## What the research says

    [Two to four short paragraphs explaining the finding and its limits.]

    ## Evidence strength

    [Bulletproof consensus / strong / moderate / preliminary, with one sentence
    explaining why.]

    ## Key studies

    - [Author (year), title] — [design; finding; limitation]. [Link]
    - [Author (year), title] — [design; finding; limitation]. [Link]

    ## What this means for practice

    [A proportional application for teachers, parents, or students.]

    Learn more in [relevant chapter link], part of [The Science of Learning],
    a free and open textbook.

    This is an ongoing project. We are improving the chapters, adding research,
    and refining explanations day by day.

## Building the textbook Research Side-Quest box

### Placement comes first

A Research Side-Quest should interrupt the relevant chapter argument. It should feel like a worthwhile detour, not one more isolated block at the bottom of a chapter.

Place it:

- after the prose has introduced the question or tension;
- before the chapter moves too far away from the relevant idea;
- between ordinary prose blocks in the argument;
- outside retrieval-practice details, misconception boxes, notes, application boxes, and other interactive boxes.

The side quest may sit inside the relevant concept container if that is the natural location, but it must be a sibling of the prose—not a child of another box.

On desktop, Research Side-Quests should be full-flow, centered blocks. Do not float them beside the text. A float can allow the next retrieval or note box to wrap around the video and make it look nested or visually broken. The standard layout is:

- display block;
- clear both;
- full available width up to a reasonable maximum;
- centered with vertical spacing.

### Standard visible structure

Use the existing Research Side-Quest component and preserve its accessible structure:

- an outer aside with a unique ID;
- the visible label **Research Side-Quest:**;
- a question-style title;
- a one-sentence teaser;
- a collapsible panel;
- the embedded video;
- a direct YouTube link;
- expandable research sections.

The usual sections are:

1. **Findings**
2. **Nuance**
3. **Application**
4. **Takeaway** when the video needs a memorable final principle

The box should remain useful even if a student does not watch the video.

### Box content rules

#### Findings

Give the context the video had to compress:

- identify the relevant corpus or synthesis;
- name the major designs;
- state what the studies actually found;
- avoid turning an association into a cause.

The findings section should not simply restate the video line by line.

#### Nuance

Include:

- the evidence-strength label;
- the evidence hierarchy;
- the most important limitations;
- alternative explanations;
- distinctions between direct evidence and application.

A good nuance section answers:

> What would a careful reader need to know before turning this finding into advice?

#### Application

Give students or practitioners a next move. Use concrete language, but keep it proportional to the evidence.

Prefer:

> Match support to demonstrated readiness.

Avoid:

> This one strategy will solve the problem.

#### Takeaway

Use a short statement students can remember. If the takeaway adds a broader human or professional principle, mark it as an application rather than presenting it as though the cited studies directly tested it.

Do not put a Research Side-Quest inside:

- <details class="etb-retrieval">;
- another <details> box;
- a note, misconception, or application box;
- a paragraph or list;
- an iframe-only wrapper that prevents the side quest from standing on its own.

Use a unique ID such as teen-stereotypes-video, teen-parenting-video, or math-anxiety-video. Use the actual YouTube video ID in both the iframe and the YouTube link.

## Source-of-truth and build rules

Chapter content belongs in etb-live/. The published site is generated in site/.

After changing a chapter:

1. Edit the etb-live/ source.
2. Run:

       python3 build/build_site.py

3. Confirm that the generated site/ page includes the same side quest.
4. Commit both the source and generated output in the same pull request.
5. Never edit only site/ by hand.

Page styles must live in the etb-live/ file. If a new component style is needed, put it in the source page and rebuild the generated page.

## Review checklist

Before opening or requesting review:

### Research

- [ ] The video’s claim is stated in one sentence.
- [ ] The notes distinguish a corpus from one study.
- [ ] Every important source exists and actually supports the wording.
- [ ] The design, sample, finding, and main limitation are recorded.
- [ ] Correlation, longitudinal evidence, and randomized evidence are not conflated.
- [ ] The evidence-strength label matches the evidence.
- [ ] The application does not outrun the evidence.
- [ ] The textbook links to the relevant chapter.

### YouTube

- [ ] The title and opening are clear without context.
- [ ] The description is readable on its own.
- [ ] Key studies have direct links.
- [ ] The ongoing-project note is included.
- [ ] The textbook link works.

### Book box

- [ ] The label is exactly **Research Side-Quest:**.
- [ ] The title is a question or clear claim.
- [ ] The teaser earns the click.
- [ ] The box interrupts the chapter argument.
- [ ] The box is not nested inside another interactive box.
- [ ] The side quest is not floated beside the prose.
- [ ] The iframe has a useful title and lazy loading.
- [ ] A direct YouTube link is available.
- [ ] The box works on a phone.
- [ ] The source and generated pages match.

### Publishing

- [ ] Start from current main.
- [ ] Run the site build.
- [ ] Inspect the pull request’s Files changed tab.
- [ ] A video/content change should not unexpectedly change unrelated files.
- [ ] Open the Cloudflare preview.
- [ ] Check the relevant chapter on desktop and mobile.
- [ ] After merge, check the live chapter.
