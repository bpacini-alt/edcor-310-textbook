Introduction to Empiricism

The Bomb Worked!

Chapter 3~ 40 min read

Dr. Dibble was setting up a demonstration. Patiently, a little distracted, moving through the steps with the unhurried confidence of someone who has done this several hundred times. He paused to note an assumption: "Of course, we'll want to assume no friction here."

Dr. Dibble was my wonderful college philosophy of science professor and was, in every possible positive way, the picture of a mad scientist.

Someone in the back — someone braver, or perhaps less aware of the room's unspoken rules than the rest of us — decided that was his moment.

"With all these assumptions," he said, "how do we even know that science works?"

The room went still. You could feel it. My breath caught. It was that particular silence that occurs when a student has said something that might be either profound or catastrophic, and no one yet knows which. I held my breath and looked at Dr. Dibble.

He looked up from his demonstration. He looked at the student. And then, with an expression of complete and amiable bewilderment — as if the question had answered itself — he said: "The bomb worked."

Then he returned to his experiment happily muttering to himself.

The nuclear explosion that resulted from a fission chain reaction happened in spite of all science's flaws. The scientists who built it almost certainly started in classrooms that made assumptions in order to make learning simpler. The bombstillworked. Not because the assumptions were perfect. Not because every theoretical wrinkle had been ironed out. Because the thing they built, using the methods they had developed, produced the outcome the theory predicted — at catastrophic, irreversible scale. Empiricism does not require perfect premises. It requires that its conclusions survive contact with the real world. This chapter is about how that works, and why it matters for educators.

The big picture
Chapter 2 named the problem: human cognition is systematically unreliable. We see patterns that aren't there, remember things that didn't happen, and change our minds less readily than the evidence warrants. Chapter 3 introduces the solution — or at least the best one humans have invented. The scientific method was not designed for people who think clearly. It was designed precisely because we don't. Understanding how it works, what it can establish, and where it reaches its limits is the foundational knowledge of the scholar-practitioner identity this course is building.

Major Concept 1 · Following the weight of the evidence

Empirical Epistemology

Epistemology is the branch of philosophy concerned with knowledge itself — what we can know, how we can know it, and what justifies a belief. Every time a teacher decides to use a particular instructional strategy, they are implicitly making an epistemological claim: I believe this works, and here is how I know it. Every time a school adopts a curriculum, the decision is grounded — consciously or not — in some theory of what counts as good evidence.

The central epistemological question is old: how do we distinguish genuine knowledge from confident belief that happens to be wrong? How do we tell what is true from what is confidently stated and wrapped in confirmation bias? History is full of people who were certain of things that turned out to be false — not foolish people, but educated, thoughtful people operating with the best tools available to them. Physicians bled patients for centuries because blood imbalance was the accepted theory of disease. Teachers taught reading using three-cueing strategies for decades because meaning-first reading was the accepted theory of literacy. How do we find the dividing line between confidence and correctness?

Philosophers have proposed many answers, but the one we will hold to in this class is empiricism. Empiricism holds that claims about the world should stand up to scrutiny when compared to the evidence. The core of empiricism is the insistence that claims about the world must be testable against the world. A theory that could not be shown false by any possible observation is not science. The falsifiability criterion, proposed by Karl Popper, captures this precisely: the willingness to be wrong is what distinguishes scientific from non-scientific claims. In short, empiricism holds that we cannot hold to propositions if they are not resonant with the way the world works. It is a philosophy of "show me the evidence."

The scientific method is one of the most significant intellectual achievements in human history — not because scientists are smarter than other people, but because the method is specifically designed for people who are not. It is a system built around the frank acknowledgment that human observers are biased, selective, and unreliable, and that the only way to arrive at knowledge we can trust is to build in systematic compensations for those failures. The scientific method, at its core, follows a recognizable sequence: observe a phenomenon, form a hypothesis that could explain it, design a test that could show the hypothesis false, collect data, and revise the hypothesis in light of what the data shows.

The willingness to revise — to change the theory when the evidence demands it, rather than changing the interpretation of evidence to protect the theory — is what makes it work. It is also, as Chapter 2 predicted, the step that human cognition resists most fiercely.

Experimental psychology applies this logic to questions about human behavior and mental processes. Before experimental methods were applied to psychology, most of what practitioners believed about human behavior was derived from clinical observation, philosophical reasoning, and the kind of confident pattern-recognition that Chapter 2 showed us is systematically unreliable.

If empiricism is the commitment to grounding knowledge in evidence, then one of the most important questions empiricism generates is: which evidence? A new teacher's gut-feeling about how to teach is one form of evidence. A classroom teacher's twenty years of observation is evidence. A case study published in a practitioner journal is evidence. A randomized controlled trial is evidence. A meta-analysis of forty randomized controlled trials is evidence. These are not equally good evidence for the same claim, and treating them as if they were is one of the most consequential errors a scholar-practitioner can make.

The evidence hierarchy ranks how much confidence a form of evidence can justify in causal claims about what produces what effects in what populations. Case studies are useful for generating hypotheses and understanding mechanisms. They are not useful for establishing that a given intervention reliably outperforms alternatives.

The hierarchy is ultimately imperfect too. Using it is no guarantee of perfect decisions. Rather, use of the evidence hierarchy is a way to improve our thinking and maximize the likelihood that we make decisions on the basis of good evidence.

One practical use of the hierarchy is as a question generator. When someone presents a claim about what works in education, the first question is: what kind of evidence supports this? A single study sits lower in the hierarchy than a consistent pattern across multiple replications. A pattern that holds across different populations, contexts, and independent researchers is more reliable than one found only in one lab with one population by researchers who had a stake in the outcome. The hierarchy helps you interpret the overall picture of studies, not just react to whichever study happens to be in front of you. You will use this hierarchy every time you encounter a research claim.

Retrieval practiceA teacher says: "I know this approach works because I've been using it for fifteen years and I can feel it in my bones." What is the epistemological problem with this claim — and what would a stronger epistemological basis look like?▸

The claim is grounded in personal experience and felt conviction — which Chapter 2 showed us are among the least reliable sources of knowledge. The teacher has nocontrol group, no way to distinguish the effect of the approach from the effect of their enthusiasm, their relationships, or dozens of other variables operating simultaneously. Fifteen years of experience can deepen skill in implementing a flawed approach — but cannot establish that the approach is actually the cause of whatever good outcomes occur.

A stronger epistemological basis would involve: research evidence from controlled studies testing this approach against alternatives; systematic data showing measurable progress attributable to the approach; and genuine willingness to update if the evidence changes. The difference is not confidence — the unreflective teacher and the scholar-practitioner may feel equally certain. The difference is whether that certainty is grounded in something that can survive scrutiny.

Common misconception"This is peer-reviewed research, so it must be reliable."▸

Peer reviewis a quality-control filter, not a quality guarantee. The peer review process asks: is this study methodologically sound enough to publish? It does not ask: is this finding true? Studies that pass peer review can still be underpowered, can involve outcome measures that don't capture what matters, can reflect publication bias (journals favor positive over null results), and can fail to replicate when independent researchers try to reproduce them.

Retrieval practiceRank these four sources from most to least reliable for the claim "morning exercise improves student academic performance": (a) a meta-analysis of 34 RCTs on exercise and cognition, (b) a teacher's observation that students seem more focused after morning PE, (c) a single RCT showing improved math scores in students who exercised before school, (d) a systematic review of observational studies on physical activity and school outcomes.▸

Most reliable: (a) the meta-analysis.Synthesizes 34 RCTs — among the strongest evidence for causal claims when the review is well-conducted, with the precision that comes from combining many samples.

Second: (c) the single RCT.Random assignment enables causal inference, but a single study is vulnerable to chance findings and specific population effects. The meta-analysis is more reliable specifically because it aggregates across many such studies.

Third: (d) the systematic review of observational studies.A rigorous synthesis, but of studies that cannot establish causation — confounding cannot be ruled out in observational data.

Least reliable: (b) the teacher's observation.No control condition, no systematic data collection, subject to confirmation bias and attention effects from being observed or studied. Valuable as hypothesis-generation — "I noticed something worth investigating" — but cannot support a causal claim.

Major Concept 2 · Measurement and meaning

Approaches to Psychology

The choice to use empiricism is neither obvious nor without costs. The history of psychology shows us why: psychology is no monolith; it is a series of disagreements, arguments, and counter-arguments. Many of these arguments are oscillations between the side of measurement, rigor, and objective evaluation and the side of meaning, experience, and subjective purpose. One way to look at the history of psychology is as a long argument over measurement and meaning.

The meaning-centered side of the discipline, spanning from Sigmund Freud to Carl Rogers, recognized that the rigid scientific tools of their respective eras were not always up to the task of capturing the full, chaotic experience of humanity. Their arguments deserve their due: the deep, abiding argument they made is that humans are not reducible to neat equations.

The discipline’s early history was dominated by Sigmund Freud’s psychodynamic theory, which claimed that our behavior is driven by unconscious currents and forgotten childhood traumas. Despite being substantially revised by modern research, the language of Freud is still deeply embedded in our culture: Id, Ego, repressed memories, and defense mechanisms are household terms. It was a captivating, literary view of the mind, but it suffered from a serious scientific vulnerability: you couldn't actually see, isolate, or measure the unconscious. Because the conclusions of early psychoanalytic theories were based on interpretation rather than replication, they were difficult to align with rigorous science.

In the early 20th century, behaviorism arrived as a direct, aggressive revolt against this guesswork. Led by thinkers like John B. Watson and B.F. Skinner, behaviorists declared that if you cannot observe something from the outside, you have no business claiming to study it scientifically. They moved psychology out of the therapist's armchair and into the laboratory, reducing behavior to a series of predictable responses to environmental rewards and punishments. Skinner and Watson spent decades charting how rats and pigeons navigated levers and mazes, but their core premise was fundamentally epistemological:we will only measure what is publicly measurable.

But in stripping away the mystery, behaviorism also risked stripping away the humanity. By mid-century,humanistic psychologypushed back hard against both behaviorism and psychodynamic theory. Humanists argued that behaviorism’s "rats-and-levers" view flattened human beings into stimulus-response machines, while psychodynamic theory too often viewed people through the lens of pathology and the past. Led by figures like Abraham Maslow and Carl Rogers, this approach focused instead on unique human potential, free will, and the conscious drive toward self-actualization.

Yet, while the humanists made serious progress at reclaiming meaning, dignity, and inner life, they faced the same scientific roadblocks as the early psychoanalysts: their branch of psychology lacked the rigorous, objective tools required to map the intellect. That changed in the 1950s and 60s with thecognitive revolution. Armed with the brand-new metaphor of the digital computer, cognitive psychologists reopened the "black box" of the mind that behaviorism had locked away. They showed that hidden mental processes—like memory encoding, selective attention, and language acquisition—could indeed be studied scientifically through elegant, indirect experimentation.

As technology caught up with theory, theneuroscientific approachemerged as a natural inheritor of the cognitive school. It derived much of its scientific power from entirely new measurement abilities. Brain-imaging technology gave researchers new ways to observe the physical biological structures involved in thought and behavior.

The final major movement widened the lens entirely, shifting focus from the individual to the global structures that contain us.Sociocultural approacheschallenged the traditional, Western, laboratory-centric view of psychology by showing that culture, language, socio-economic status, and community shape our thinking just as much as anything happening inside a single head.

These six approaches represent a useful case study in how a science evolves. Each one was a reaction to, and a partial correction of, the school that came before it. Why take the time to share all six, rather than just teaching the single approach that is currently deemed most empirically valid? We do so for three powerful reasons.

  • It makes you a better practitioner.The teacher or leader who can use the tools of all the various disciplines, at the right time and in the right context, has a better toolkit than the one whose tools are limited to a simplistic view of psychology as a field.

  • It helps you see the nuance.The teacher who knows that psychology isn't a monolith has a serious edge over one who believes all therapists, researchers, and counselors think exactly alike, because they understand the contours of arguments and the disagreements in the field that are live and unsettled.

  • It serves as an inoculant against intellectual blind spots.Understanding a little of each school of thought prevents you from falling victim to the dogmas of any single sub-discipline. Finding the utility in Skinner's behavioral experimentsandthe value in Freud's focus on early childhood will make you a far more sophisticated student of human nature.

Major Concept 3 · What evidence cannot tell us

Positive and Normative Questions

Empiricismis an incredibly powerful tool, but it is not a flawless mirror of reality. It has blind spots. No matter how careful the researcher, pre-empirical assumptions inevitably work their way into supposedly rigorous, objective research. As the philosopher Daniel Dennett famously noted, "there is no such thing as philosophy-free science; there is only science whose philosophical baggage is taken on board without examination." Scientists are just as susceptible to fallacy, cultural bias, and personal dogma as the rest of us, and those flaws can be reflected in their data.

Empiricism is also not a fully formed life philosophy. It can answer scientific questions, but it cannot offer answers to questions of beauty, of love, or of things of the soul. That limitation matters because some of the most important questions in education are not only questions about what works. They are questions about what is worth working toward.

Hume's guillotinenames the divide between claims about what is true and claims about what ought to be done. In this chapter's terms, it is the divide between positive and normative questions.

Positive claimsdescribe reality: what happens, what causes what, what effects an intervention produces. These are the kinds of claims empirical research is built to test.Normative claimsevaluate reality: what should matter, what we ought to do, what kind of people and communities we are trying to form. These claims require values, purposes, and moral judgment.

This distinction matters constantly in education. Research can tell you whether a strict behavior system reduces disruptions. It cannot, by itself, tell you whether fear-based compliance is the kind of classroom culture you ought to build. Research can tell you whether a reading intervention raises scores. It cannot tell you whether test scores are the ultimate purpose of literacy. Evidence can discipline our methods, but it cannot settle our ultimate aims.

Retrieval practiceA school finds strong evidence that a strict rewards-and-consequences system reduces classroom disruptions. What is the positive claim, what is the normative question, and why can't the evidence settle both?▸

The positive claim is that the system reduces disruptions. That claim can be tested empirically: compare classrooms, measure behavior, look for confounding variables, and ask whether the effect replicates.

The normative question is whether that system builds the kind of classroom culture teachers ought to want. Does it cultivate trust, agency, charity, courage, and love of learning — or only compliance? Evidence matters deeply, but it cannot by itself decide what education is ultimately for. That requires values and moral judgment.

Major Concept 4 · A machine for finding truth

Experimental Psychology

It is reasonable to conclude that if I administer medication and the patient gets better, that the medication worked. It is also reasonable to conclude that if I introduce a new reading program and student scores rise, the program helped. But rigorous empiricism is not so easy.

Perhaps the most important distinction in research methodology is the one betweencorrelationandcausation— and it is one that human intuition consistently fails to make. When two things vary together, the temptation to conclude that one causes the other is powerful and almost always premature. Correlation describes a statistical relationship: when X goes up, Y tends to go up or down. To use our previous example, when I administer medication, my patient gets better. Causation describes a mechanism: X produces Y; the medication caused my patient to get better. They look identical from the outside. The difference only becomes visible when you ask: if I change X while holding everything else constant, does Y change? That is what experiments are designed to answer.

The reason correlation cannot establish causation isconfounding variables— third factors that influence both X and Y, creating the appearance of a relationship without either causing the other. Students who read more at home may also come from homes with more books, more educated parents, more consistent routines. Any of these might explain the reading performance independently. My patient may have gotten better due to the medication, or may have gotten better because time passed and the body healed itself. The reading scores may have risen because of the new program, or because teachers received new training, administrators paid closer attention, or students were tested differently. To make a causal claim, we need a design that accounts for those rival explanations. That is the logic of experimental design.

Treatment Group
Receives the intervention being tested. Outcome differences between treatment and control are attributed to the intervention — but only when assignment was random and conditions were otherwise equal.
Control Group
Receives no intervention — or a placebo. Provides the baseline for comparison. Without a control group, there is no way to know whether outcomes would have changed anyway.
Random Assignment
Assigns participants to groups by chance. The strongest general method for reducing pre-existing group differences — and the feature that allows a study to claim causation rather than mere correlation.

These three elements — treatment group,control group, andrandom assignment— form the foundation of arandomized controlled trial (RCT). But even a well-randomized trial is not fully protected from human bias. Two additional design layers close two more specific loopholes: thesingle-blindand thedouble-blind. Various blinding protocols mitigate participant and investigator bias, as we will discuss in a moment.

Theplacebo effectis one of the most important findings in experimental psychology. Aplacebois an inert treatment — a sugar pill, a sham procedure — that produces real, measurable improvement simply because the recipient believes they are being treated. The effect shows up in brain scans, hormone levels, and objective clinical measures. It reveals that expectation is itself an active ingredient in outcomes. For educators, this means any new program may produce some improvement because introducing something new signals that change is expected and resources are being invested. Even observation can affect behavior: students and teachers often perform differently when they know they are part of something being watched and measured. A single-blind design addresses this: participants don't know whether they are receiving the actual intervention or a control condition, so their expectations cannot differ systematically between groups.

But blinding participants alone is not enough. Researchers themselves introduce bias even when they are trying not to. A teacher who knows which students are using the experimental curriculum may unconsciously call on them more, grade their work more generously, or spend more time supporting them. A researcher who knows which group received the treatment may interpret ambiguous data in ways that favor the expected outcome. This isexperimenter bias— and it is subtle enough that even highly trained, well-intentioned researchers are not immune to it. A double-blind design closes this loophole: neither participants nor researchers know who is in which group until the data collection is complete.

Why this matters especially for education research

Full double-blinding is usually impossible in education. Teachers cannot be kept unaware of which instructional method they are using. This is not a failure of education research — it is a structural constraint of studying human beings in institutional settings. What it means practically is that education RCTs are often subject to some degree of experimenter bias, and thatblind outcome scoring— where the people marking assessments don't know which condition produced the work — is a critical quality indicator to look for. When you read an education study, ask: who scored the outcomes, and did they know which group was which?

Common misconception"These two things are strongly correlated — so one must cause the other."▸

Correlation is compatible with three distinct causal structures.X could cause Y(the intuitive reading).Y could cause X(reverse causation): students who perform better academically may be assigned to smaller classes, rather than smaller classes causing better performance.A third variable Z could cause both: schools in wealthier districts may have both smaller class sizes and higher test scores because wealth buys both — the class size and the scores are both effects of the same cause.

Strong correlations make good hypotheses. They are reasonable starting points for asking "is there a causal relationship worth investigating?" They do not establish causation. This distinction has produced real harm when violated — the correlation between whole-language instruction and some students learning to read was taken as evidence that the approach caused reading, when in fact those students might have read even better under structured literacy.

Retrieval practiceA district adopts a new math curriculum. After one year, scores improve by eight points. The developer claims the program caused the improvement. What would you need to know to evaluate that claim — and what design features would make the study more convincing?▸

The eight-point gain is a positive finding — something happened. But the developer's causal claim requires considerably more. The first question is whether there was acontrol group: did comparable students who did not use the program also improve? If scores rose district-wide due to a policy change, a testing format shift, or simple regression to the mean, the curriculum deserves no credit.

The second question iswhat else changed. A new curriculum rarely arrives alone — it typically comes with training, administrative attention, and teacher enthusiasm. Any of those could drive improvement independently. This is the confounding problem.

The third question ishow outcomes were scored. If the developer's own team scored the assessments knowing which classrooms used their product, experimenter bias is a live threat. A more convincing study would use blind outcome scoring — assessors who did not know which condition produced which work.

The fourth question iswho is making the claim. A developer reporting on their own product has a financial interest in a positive result. That does not make the claim false, but it is a reason to look for independent replication before updating strongly.

Eight points is a fact. That the curriculum caused it is an interpretation — and a self-serving one. The design features that would make the claim more convincing: random assignment, a genuine control group, blind outcome scoring, and replication by researchers with no stake in the result.

Experimental Psychology: Three Examples

Stanley Milgram looked at the horrors of the Holocaust and had a hypothesis: perhaps German people were unusually obedient to authority figures. Maybe the terrors of Auschwitz and Birkenau had something to do with a national predisposition toward order, hierarchy, and loyalty.

Ignore, for a moment, the implied racism. This story is not one of Milgram's personal malice, but about the experiment that changed his mind; and his willingness to have his mind changed through rigorous scientific investigation. The reality is that Milgram's hypothesis was common at the time: many people assumed that Nazi atrocities were caused byotherpeople being unusually predisposed to obey and harm.

Experimental psychology has produced many important findings, but I have selected three studies that stand apart — not only for the scale of their impact on psychological theory but for what they revealed about ordinary human behavior that no one expected. I also select them, here, as examples of the kind of work that experimental psychologists do: so that you can understand how good research is built, and on what building blocks. Each arrived at a finding that most people, before the study, would have confidently said was impossible. Each has direct implications for anyone who works with other human beings in positions of authority. And each raised profound ethical questions that permanently changed how research involving human subjects is conducted.

What ties the three together is a single tendency in how we read other people. When someone behaves badly — cruelly, weakly, foolishly — our first instinct is to explain it by who they are: their character, their disposition, the kind of person they must be. Milgram's original assumption treated German character as the explanation for obedience to Nazi evil. Psychologists call this thefundamental attribution error: the systematic habit of overweighting character and underweighting circumstance when we explain what other people do. Tellingly, we reverse it for ourselves — our own missteps always seem to have perfectly good situational reasons. What Milgram found, instead, was that Americans are also very likely to obey authority figures, dashing his neat hypothesis and leaving a far more unsettling question: what if evil is not located in an ethnicity or country? What if it, instead, runs through the heart of every man or woman?

The three experiments below are each, in their own way, a demonstration of how powerfully situations shape behavior — and therefore of how badly that instinct misleads us.

SetupParticipants were told they were assisting in a study on learning and memory. Assigned the role of "Teacher," they administered increasingly severe electric shocks to a "Learner" (a trained actor) for wrong answers. Shocks ran from 15V ("Slight Shock") to 450V ("XXX"). When participants hesitated, an authority figure in a lab coat gave standardized prompts: "Please continue." "The experiment requires that you continue."
FindingApproximately65% of participants administered the maximum 450-volt shock— labeled "Danger: Severe Shock." Before the study, psychiatrists predicted fewer than 1% would reach the maximum. The actual result was nearly 65 times higher. Participants were not indifferent — they expressed genuine distress and moral discomfort throughout. They continued anyway, because an authority figure told them to.
For teachersStudents in your classroom are not immune to obedience pressure — and neither are you. The Milgram findings suggest that institutional authority structures produce compliance even when individuals have moral reservations. Professional identity — the scholar-practitioner identity this course is building — provides an internal source of judgment that can resist external authority when that authority is wrong.
EthicsParticipants were deceived and subjected to significant psychological distress. Milgram conducted debriefings, but lasting effects on some participants remain debated. The study helped intensify debate about deception, informed consent, and oversight in human-subjects research.
SetupParticipants were shown cards displaying one reference line and three comparison lines of clearly different lengths. They were asked to identify which comparison line matched the reference. Unknown to the real participant, everyone else in the room was a confederate instructed to give the same obviously wrong answer on predetermined trials.
FindingOn critical trials, approximately75% of real participants conformed at least once— giving an answer they could see with their own eyes was wrong. The overall conformity rate was approximately 37%. When participants completed the task alone, the error rate was less than 1%. Social pressure distorted clear perceptual judgments — not just opinions or ambiguous situations, but simple visual facts.
For teachersClassrooms are social conformity environments. When most students raise their hands for one answer, when the peer norm is disengagement, when the dominant culture has a particular shape — students and teachers are under continuous Asch-style pressure to conform. Understanding this pressure helps teachers design environments where intellectual dissent is easier, not harder.
EthicsDeception was central to the design. Asch conducted immediate debriefings, and participants generally reported understanding the purpose without lasting distress. The deception is considered more defensible than Milgram's because the potential for harm was significantly lower — though it still prompted debate about informed consent in research.
SetupLed by Dr. Philip Zimbardo, twenty-four male college students, screened for psychological health, were randomly assigned to play either "guards" or "prisoners" in a simulated prison in Stanford's psychology building. Guards were given uniforms, reflective sunglasses, and batons; prisoners were assigned numbers and wore smocks. The study was planned for two weeks.
FindingThe study was halted after six days. Guards had begun engaging in psychological abuse — sleep deprivation, humiliation, arbitrary punishment — and several prisoners showed signs of acute emotional breakdown.Zimbardo himself became so absorbed in his "superintendent" rolethat a visiting colleague had to point out that what was happening was wrong. The situation, not the individuals' personalities, appeared to be driving the behavior.
For teachersThe Stanford experiment illustratessituationism— behavior can be shaped powerfully by situational forces. When a student acts badly, the situationist question is: what features of this situation are producing this behavior? Not "what is wrong with this student?" but "what is wrong with this environment?" Both questions matter; situationism argues we weight the second too lightly.
EthicsNow considered one of psychology's most famous ethical cautionary tales — no independent oversight, no clear stopping criteria, and the lead researcher was himself a participant. Subsequent investigation also revealed that some guard behavior may have been influenced by Zimbardo's own instructions, raising questions about how spontaneous the findings really were. The experiment is taught as much for what it reveals about research ethics as for its situationist findings.
Research studyWhat all three experiments share — and why it matters

Milgram, Asch, and Zimbardo all found evidence of the same thing: situationism — the idea that behavior can be shaped powerfully by situational forces. Ordinary people, in certain situational arrangements, do things they would never predict of themselves.

Milgram's study is the clearest illustration of why this matters. He began his work in 1961, against the backdrop of the Holocaust and the trial of Adolf Eichmann — the Nazi official who had organized the deportation of Jews to the death camps — in Jerusalem. The prevailing explanation for how an entire society could participate in such atrocities located the cause in the German people themselves: a defect of national character, sometimes called the "Germans are different" hypothesis, that supposedly made them uniquely willing to obey monstrous orders. (If that sounds like ethnic prejudice, it was.) Milgram planned to measure obedience in the United States first, fully expecting individualistic Americans to score low, and then to run the study in Germany to document the gap. He never made the trip. The American baseline was so high — roughly two in three ordinary people willing to deliver what they believed were dangerous, even lethal, shocks — that the premise collapsed. Whatever produced the Holocaust's obedience, it was not uniquely German. It was human.

This is thefundamental attribution erroroperating at the scale of an entire nation — the same instinct that says "he's lazy" or "she's difficult," applied to a whole people and challenged by the data. Understanding the tendency does not excuse harmful behavior. But it should change how we interpret it — and what we do about it.

Retrieval practiceA student in your class is consistently disruptive, unmotivated, and unresponsive to your efforts. A colleague says: "Some kids are just like that — you can't reach everyone." Using what you know from the three experiments, offer a different interpretation of this student's behavior.▸

The colleague's explanation is dispositional — it locates the problem inside the student. The three experiments suggest a situationist alternative: behavior that looks like personality is often situationally produced. The question to ask is not "what kind of student is this?" but "what situational forces are operating on this student?" — which might include: peer dynamics in the classroom (Asch), the student's relationship to authority figures based on prior experience (Milgram), the role the student has settled into within the classroom social structure (Zimbardo), and environmental factors the teacher can potentially change.

This does not mean character is irrelevant or that every student is reachable by every teacher. It means that "what can I change about this situation?" should come before "what is wrong with this student?" — because the situational question is the one the teacher actually has leverage over.

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

Epistemology, experimental design, and the evidence hierarchy are not background knowledge — they are active tools for every professional decision you will make.

  • When someone makes a claim about what works, ask where it sits in the evidence hierarchy. Is this a meta-analysis, a single study, expert opinion, or a colleague's impression? The hierarchy calibrates how much confidence the claim has earned.
  • When you read an education study, ask: was there a control group? Was there random assignment? Were outcomes scored blind? These three questions cut through most of the noise in education research reporting.
  • When you observe improvement in your students after trying something new, ask: what else changed? What is my control group? Am I observing a real effect, or regression to the mean, attention effects from being observed, or my own confirmation bias? These are not cynical questions — they are the questions that distinguish professional judgment from wishful thinking.
  • When a curriculum vendor presents outcome data, ask who scored the assessments and whether they knew which students used the product. Blind outcome scoring is one of the clearest signals that a study took experimenter bias seriously.
  • The three experiments remind you that situational forces can shape behavior more than most people realize. Before concluding that a student "is" a certain way, ask what features of the situation might be producing the behavior. That question has more leverage.
  • Correlation is everywhere in education data. Get into the habit of asking automatically: could there be a third variable? Could causation run the other direction? The answer will often be yes — and knowing that will protect you from expensive interventions based on spurious relationships.
Chapter synthesis
The bomb worked. That is the whole epistemological argument in three words. Not because the assumptions were perfect. Not because every theoretical wrinkle had been ironed out. Because the methods produced outcomes that could be tested against reality — and survived the test, at scale, repeatedly. This is what empiricism offers: not certainty, but a system for being wrong in increasingly reliable ways, a system that corrects itself. Chapter 3 has given you the tools for asking what the evidence can show, what it cannot show, and how those tools matter for educators.
Epistemology is not abstract. Every instructional decision is an implicit claim about what counts as good evidence for what works. Making that claim consciously is the beginning of professional expertise.
Correlation is not causation — and the temptation to treat it as such is a systematic feature of human cognition that experimental design was invented to compensate for.
Single-blind designs and blind outcome scoring close specific loopholes that even well-designed RCTs leave open — and in education, where full double-blinding is usually impossible, blind outcome scoring is the key quality signal to look for.
Milgram, Asch, and Zimbardo all found evidence for situationism: behavior can be shaped powerfully by situational forces. "What is wrong with this situation?" should come before "what is wrong with this student?"
The evidence hierarchy calibrates confidence — it tells you how much a particular form of evidence can justify, and when to hold conclusions more loosely.
