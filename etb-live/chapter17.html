Anthropology & Culture

Tradition Is Just Peer Pressure from Dead People

Chapter 17~ 45 min read

“There are these two young fish swimming along and they happen to meet an older fish swimming the other way, who nods at them and says ‘Morning, boys. How’s the water?’ And the two young fish swim on for a bit, and then eventually one of them looks over at the other and goes ‘What the hell is water?’”

The writer David Foster Wallace opened his 2005 graduation speech at Kenyon College with this joke. His point was that the most obvious, important realities around us are often the hardest to see.

You have been swimming in your own water since before you could talk. It taught you what to eat for breakfast, how close to stand to a stranger, and what it means to be a good person. You can probably name a few things about other people’s cultures. What would it take to notice your own?

The big picture

Every person grows up inside a culture they didn’t choose and mostly can’t see. That includes you, and it includes every student you will teach. Teachers can’t step outside their own culture, but they can learn to notice it, and they can help students examine what they’ve inherited instead of just obeying it or throwing it out.

We start with how culture hides in plain sight, then look at how groups keep people in line. Next we compare two very different ideas about how to live: expressive individualism, which shapes many of your future students, and Stoicism, an ancient tradition that pushes against it. Then we turn to what happens when cultures clash over schools, and we end with the student in front of you, who is always more than any culture you can name.

Major concept 1 · The Water We Swim In

Culture

In 1956, the anthropologist Horace Miner published a report on a North American tribe called the Nacirema. He described a people deeply worried about their bodies, who believed that only constant ritual could keep them from disease and social rejection.

Every Nacirema home, Miner wrote, has a small shrine box built into the wall, filled with charms and potions. Each day, people perform a mouth ritual: they put “a small bundle of hog hairs” into their mouths along with special powders and move it in a set pattern. Once or twice a year they visit “holy-mouth-men,” who scrape at their teeth with metal tools. When the Nacirema are very sick, they go to a great temple called the latipso, where robed practitioners perform elaborate rites.

You may have noticed by now that Nacirema is American spelled backward. Miner was describing toothbrushes, medicine cabinets, dentists, and hospitals.

What Miner was showing us

Miner’s essay is a trick with a serious point. Three lessons matter most for teachers.

Culture is invisible from the inside. Americans didn’t think of brushing their teeth as a ritual. It was just a morning routine. The things that feel most natural to us are often the most cultural, the same way you stop noticing the smell of your own house.

Strange is a matter of position, not fact. Nothing Miner described was actually strange. It was familiar, seen from an unfamiliar angle. When a student’s habits or customs seem odd to you, remember that they aren’t odd. You are just standing somewhere new.

You are not exempt from what you study. Teachers who study culture, worldview, and conformity are still inside those forces. This chapter is about your culture as much as anyone else’s.

Culture and worldview

Culture is the set of shared customs, beliefs, values, and practices a group passes from one generation to the next. It tells people what is polite and what is rude, what counts as success, and what we owe one another. Most of the time it works without announcing itself. The amount of eye contact you consider respectful, how you think about being on time, and which emotions you feel free to show in public all came from your culture. People raised in different cultures can see the same situation and honestly perceive different things.

Culture also changes. Sometimes it drifts slowly across generations. Sometimes it shifts quickly, through contact with other cultures, new technology, or people inside it pushing back. Every tradition was once a new idea. Every norm was once a choice.

Inside every culture, each person also carries a worldview: their own lens for what is true, what is good, and what life means. Culture shapes worldview, but so do faith, family, temperament, and experience. Two siblings raised in the same home can see the world differently. That means knowing a student’s cultural background gives you useful context, but never the full picture.

Chapter 16 asked whether people are just following a program someone else wrote for them. Culture is where much of that program comes from. How much of what you do is a real choice, and how much is simply what your culture taught you to see as normal?

Traditions of the fathers

The subtitle of this chapter, “Tradition is just peer pressure from dead people,” is a popular saying whose author is unknown. It treats tradition as something done to you. Notice, though, that the saying does to you what it accuses tradition of doing: it nudges you to go along, this time toward doubting tradition. The Book of Mormon takes tradition seriously in both directions. Some traditions led whole peoples away from truth for generations; King Benjamin taught his sons that the Lamanites rejected the truth even when it was taught to them, “because of the traditions of their fathers, which are not correct” (Mosiah 1:5). Other traditions saved lives. The stripling warriors went into battle without fear because “they had been taught by their mothers” (Alma 56:47).

So a tradition can’t be judged by its age. Each one is worth examining in the light of truth.

Worth remembering

Tradition is just peer pressure from dead people.

Traditions, just like peer pressure, can be positive or negative. Examine them in the light of truth. Then, keep the good ones and let the negative ones go.

Problem or difference?

You can’t be culturally neutral in a classroom. Your ideas about what good behavior looks like, how a student should show respect, and what engagement sounds like all came from somewhere. A teacher who assumes that eye contact means respect, that speaking up means engagement, or that a quiet student is a disengaged one is seeing through a lens they haven’t examined yet. Before you interpret what you see, pause and ask: is this a problem, or is it a difference?

When content meets conviction

When a student resists or pushes back on what you’re teaching, it isn’t always confusion or defiance. Sometimes it’s a worldview rubbing against the content. Skilled teachers learn to tell the difference between “this student doesn’t understand” and “this student understands, but it conflicts with something they hold deeply.” The second calls for a different response: not dropping the content, but naming the tension honestly. “I can see this pushes against something important to you. That’s worth thinking about. Can you tell me more about what’s bothering you?”

Retrieval practiceMiner describes tooth brushing as a mouth ritual performed with a bundle of hog hairs and magical powders. What is the point of describing it this way, and what does it teach that a plain description wouldn’t?►

Describing a familiar habit as if from outside the culture makes readers see their own routine as strange. That is the lesson: culture isn’t hidden, it’s too close to see. A plain sentence like “Americans brush their teeth twice a day” wouldn’t have that effect. For teachers, it works in both directions. Your students’ practices aren’t strange; you’re just standing in an unfamiliar spot. And your own practices aren’t simply natural; they only feel that way because you stopped noticing them.

Major concept 2 · The Group’s Quiet Authority

Conformity

Culture doesn’t just sit in the background. Groups actively keep people in line, and most of us are far more influenced by the people around us than we think. Conformity means changing what you say, do, or believe to match a group. It isn’t a character flaw. Humans have always depended on their groups to survive, so we are built to notice what the group expects and to feel the sting of standing apart.

ResearchAsch’s lines

You met Solomon Asch’s experiments in Chapter 3. In the 1950s, Asch asked people to judge which of three lines matched a target line, an easy task that almost no one gets wrong alone. But each real participant sat with a group of actors who had been told to give the same wrong answer on certain trials. About three out of four participants went along with the group at least once, and overall they conformed on roughly a third of those trials. When Asch interviewed them afterward, most said they knew the group was wrong. They went along because they doubted themselves or didn’t want to stand out. Only a few said they had actually seen the lines the group’s way. And when even one other person in the room gave the right answer, conformity dropped sharply.

Classrooms run on the same forces. Some peer pressure pushes in good directions: friends who study together, a team that makes hard work normal, a class where asking questions is admired. Some pushes the other way: students who hide their effort to avoid looking too eager, go along with exclusion to keep their place, or stay silent when something is wrong.

Most of this pressure is never spoken. It shows up in a look across the room, in warmth that cools when someone breaks an unwritten rule, or in a sense of what the group respects. Students read these signals quickly. That is why the norms in a room either support or undercut everything a teacher is trying to do. A teacher who says she values curiosity, but whose room actually rewards quiet compliance, will get quiet compliance.

Building the norms you want

You can’t simply ban negative peer pressure. You have to build the norms you want: notice and publicly recognize the behavior you hope will spread, design activities where the kind choice is also the socially rewarded one, and model curiosity and kindness so they look like strength.

Shared routines help too. In studies by Scott Wiltermuth and Chip Heath, people who walked in step or sang together cooperated more afterward in a group task and said they trusted each other more, even though they didn’t report feeling happier. Research on synchrony has since faced the replication questions common in social psychology, so treat this as a promising finding, not a proven classroom result. Classrooms have used this kind of shared rhythm for a long time: the same opening question every Monday, call-and-response, reading key terms aloud together. These routines cost almost nothing and may help build a sense of “we.” Chapter 18 returns to this idea in its discussion of belonging.

Worth remembering

Peer culture is a design problem, not a discipline problem.

The norms in your room will exist whether you plan them or not. Decide what you want them to be, then build them on purpose.

Retrieval practiceIn Asch’s experiments, most people went along with a wrong group answer at least once. What does this mean for a teacher who runs whole-class discussions or peer review?►

When students agree with the first confident voice in a discussion, many of them may privately disagree. They are going along so they don’t stand out, or they have started doubting themselves. Two practical moves follow. First, collect students’ thinking before the group can shape it: have them write a response first, use an anonymous poll, or call on students in random order instead of starting with the most vocal. Second, remember that one dissenter made a big difference in Asch’s studies. A teacher who openly questions a consensus, or says “I used to think that, but here’s what changed my mind,” makes it easier for students to say what they actually think.

Major concept 3 · The Culture of the Authentic Self

Expressive Individualism

“Just be yourself.” “Follow your heart.” “You do you.” These phrases are everywhere in modern Western culture, and they rest on an idea sociologists call expressive individualism: the belief that each person has a true inner self, and that the highest good is to discover that self and live by it, even when that means setting aside the expectations of family, tradition, or community. Robert Bellah and his colleagues gave the idea its name in 1985, and the philosopher Charles Taylor traced how it grew out of the Romantic movement into what he called an age of authenticity.

If this sounds familiar, it’s because it’s the plot of nearly every Disney Channel movie. In High School Musical, a basketball star and a top student risk their friends’ approval by trying out for the school musical, and by the end the whole school learns to stop sticking to the status quo. Follow your heart, even when everyone expects something else: that’s expressive individualism with a soundtrack.

Expressive individualism has done real good. It has helped people live more honestly, resist harmful pressure to conform, and hold institutions accountable when their demands hurt people. It also has costs. When being true to yourself becomes the highest value, commitments and shared obligations can start to feel like threats. “Is this really me?” can become a reason to leave any relationship, tradition, or responsibility that feels confining, including ones worth keeping.

Where you’ll see it in a classroom

Many of your future students will carry this idea without ever having heard its name. Watch for it in moments like these:

  • A student explains that she can’t do group work, give presentations, or write poetry because “that’s just not who I am.”
  • Students engage deeply when they get choice and a voice, and check out when work feels imposed or pointless.
  • Pushback against shared rules, like dress codes, phone policies, or assigned seats, is framed as a matter of self-expression.
  • A parent advocates for their child’s individual path over what the rest of the class is doing.

Many students at BYU-Idaho grew up in a culture built around covenants, family, and obligations to a community. Many of the students you will teach did not. It’s easy to read a student’s appeal to authenticity as disrespect, laziness, or entitlement when it is really a different kind of water. Noticing the difference is the first step to responding well.

What to do with it

Expressive individualism is part of your students’ culture, and you can work with it:

  • Connect learning to meaning. “Why might this matter to you?” does more than “because it’s on the test.”
  • Explain your norms. Students raised in this culture rarely accept rules because “that’s how it’s done,” but many will accept a rule whose purpose they understand.
  • Separate preference from identity. “This feels uncomfortable” is different from “this isn’t me.” Help students notice the difference without dismissing the feeling.
  • Offer real choice inside clear structure. Concept 6 of Chapter 16 shows how to do this well.
Retrieval practiceA student tells you, “I just can’t do group projects. It’s not who I am as a learner.” Using expressive individualism as a lens, what is this student expressing, and what would a good response look like?►

The student is treating a preference as part of their identity, which makes sense inside expressive individualism, where claims about who you are carry real moral weight. They aren’t being dishonest; they’re using the language their culture gave them. A good response neither dismisses the claim nor simply grants an exemption. It takes the feeling seriously while gently separating preference from identity: “I hear that group work feels uncomfortable. A lot of people feel that way. The goal isn’t to ignore that, but to help you build skills for situations you can’t always choose. What part feels hardest?” That treats the student as someone who can grow, not as a fixed self with fixed limits.

Major concept 4 · What Another Culture Can Teach Us

Stoicism

One of the best ways to see your own water is to spend time in someone else’s. Stoicism, a philosophy from ancient Greece and Rome, starts from nearly the opposite assumptions of expressive individualism. Where expressive individualism says your feelings are a guide to who you are, Stoicism says your feelings are something to examine before you act on them.

According to an ancient biography, Stoicism began around 300 BC when Zeno of Citium, a merchant, lost his cargo in a shipwreck. Stranded in Athens, he wandered into a bookshop, read about Socrates, and began studying philosophy. Whether or not every detail is true, the story captures the Stoic idea: the world can take a great deal from you, but it can’t take your response.

The dichotomy of control

The heart of Stoicism is the dichotomy of control: some things are within our control and some are not. Our judgments, choices, and efforts are ours. Other people’s opinions, the weather, and most outcomes are not. The Stoic teacher Epictetus, who was born a slave, built much of his teaching on this distinction.

You met a version of this idea in Chapter 16. Students who explain setbacks with causes they can control, like strategy or preparation, keep trying. The Stoics turned the same insight into a daily practice.

Stoicism is often mistaken for hiding your feelings or not caring. It is neither. The Stoics didn’t deny that suffering is real. They argued that we often make it worse through how we interpret events, by demanding that the world be different than it is or tying our peace to outcomes we can’t guarantee. Where expressive individualism says feel it and express it, Stoicism says examine it, then choose your response.

The four virtues

The Stoics measured a good life by character, not by circumstances or feelings:

  • Courage: facing difficulty without abandoning your values, like trying a hard task or admitting you don’t know.
  • Temperance: self-control and balance; the pause between what happens and how you respond.
  • Justice: treating others fairly and caring for the good of the community.
  • Wisdom: telling what is in your control from what isn’t, and responding to each well.

(The Stoics also taught that every human being shares the same dignity, regardless of tribe or nation. Chapter 18 explores that idea, called cosmopolitanism.)

Stoicism in the classroom

The Stoic lens helps teachers understand some common student struggles. A student who falls apart when plans change may be tying their peace to something they can’t control. A student frozen by fear of failure is treating a grade as the measure of their worth. A student who lashes out when frustrated hasn’t yet learned to pause before responding. Teachers can teach these skills directly instead of waiting for the feelings to pass.

One of the most useful questions a student can learn is simple: Is this within my control? Sorting worries into what they can act on and what they can’t helps students reduce anxiety and focus their effort where it counts.

Retrieval practiceA student falls apart whenever a project earns a lower grade than expected. She is upset for days, won’t look at the feedback, and says things like “I’m just bad at this.” Using the Stoic framework, what is happening, and what might a teacher do?►

She is treating the grade, an outcome she can no longer control, as the measure of her worth. What she can control is what she does next: how she responds to the feedback and how she prepares for the next project. Avoiding the feedback protects her from a painful feeling but blocks the very thing that would help. Instead of insisting the grade doesn’t matter or that she’s actually great, a teacher can help her separate the grade from the learning: “The grade is done. What’s in your control now is what you do with this feedback. Let’s look at it together.” Over time, asking “What here is within your control?” becomes a habit she can use on her own.

Major concept 5 · When Culture Becomes a Battlefield

Culture Wars

A culture war is what happens when competing worldviews stop living side by side and start fighting over who gets to shape shared institutions. These fights are usually about values: which histories, beliefs, and ways of seeing the world should be taught and passed on. Schools are almost always in the middle, because schools are where a society hands its culture to the next generation.

This isn’t new. American schools have been contested ground over evolution, sex education, history standards, prayer, and library books for as long as there have been public schools. What has changed is the speed. A curriculum decision that once took years to become controversial can now become national news in a weekend, and teachers can find parents, administrators, legislators, and advocacy groups pulling in different directions at once.

Three kinds of questions

The most useful thing a teacher can do with a “controversial” topic is figure out what kind of question it actually is.

Kind of questionWhat it isExamplesThe teacher’s job
Genuinely contestedInformed, reasonable people disagree because they hold different values, not just different facts.Abortion, capital punishment, immigration policyPresent the strongest versions of the main positions without advocating for one.
Edgy but answerableThe topic feels uncomfortable, but nearly everyone agrees on the answer after reflection.Was slavery wrong? Was the Holocaust evil?Answer clearly. Treating these as “both sides” questions isn’t neutrality; it’s a failure of moral clarity.
Empirically contestedExperts are still debating what the evidence shows.Open questions in nutrition science or education researchShow what we know, what we think we know, and what remains open.

Age matters too. A kindergartner doesn’t need to hear about the latest school funding fight. A high school government teacher who never discusses live issues isn’t doing the job. Most decisions fall somewhere in between and call for judgment rather than rules.

The slow drift into advocacy

Even when discussing a hard topic is appropriate, watch for a quieter risk: drifting from teaching into advocacy. A science teacher who presents the evidence on climate change is doing her job. A history teacher who teaches students to read the founding documents closely is doing his. Either one crosses a line when the small choices pile up: sharing clips that mock one political party, making asides about current politicians, or nudging students toward their own political identity. No single moment feels dramatic, but students notice whose side you’re on.

Culture wars also put pressure on teachers to signal loyalty to a side instead of thinking clearly. When a topic is politically charged, saying something that doesn’t fit either side’s story can feel risky. That is conformity again, the same force Asch measured, operating among adults. The best defense is a clear sense of your job: to help students think well, not to tell them what to think.

Finally, conduct matters. Teachers rarely lose their jobs for teaching a hard topic carefully. They lose them for a social media post, a comment that becomes a headline, or behavior the school can’t defend. You don’t have to give up your faith, opinions, or cultural life. But you are a public-facing professional, and your conduct reflects on your students and your school.

Two questions before you respond

When a topic becomes charged in your school or community, ask two questions. First: is this genuinely contested, edgy but answerable, or empirically contested? Second: what would it look like to teach students how to think about this, rather than what to think?

Retrieval practiceA colleague says, “I never take sides on anything. I just present all perspectives equally.” Using the three kinds of questions, what does this stance get right, and where does it fail?►

For genuinely contested questions, the colleague is right. Presenting the main positions without pushing your own respects students and avoids using your authority to recruit them. But for edgy-but-answerable questions, “all perspectives equally” becomes false balance. Treating “was slavery wrong?” as an open debate teaches students that moral clarity is suspect. For empirically contested questions, equal time can also mislead, because some positions have much more evidence behind them than others, and students deserve to know that. Applied to everything, the colleague’s rule erases the differences between three very different kinds of questions.

Major concept 6 · Context, Not Conclusions

Student Cultures

In Chapter 14 you learned to take the dimensions of a student’s identity seriously without reducing the student to them. Culture works the same way. Knowing that students come from different cultures is useful. Treating a student as a representative of their culture is not. A teacher who learns a few facts about a student’s background and now believes they understand that student hasn’t gained cultural awareness. They’ve gained a more confident kind of assumption.

Cultural knowledge gives you context. A student who rarely makes eye contact with adults may be showing respect, not avoiding you. A student who always defers to the group may come from a worldview that places wisdom in the group rather than the individual. A family that sends concerns through a grandparent or community elder instead of coming to a parent-teacher conference may be following a protocol that makes perfect sense to them. Knowing these possibilities helps you read situations more accurately.

But cultural knowledge doesn’t give you conclusions. Knowing that a student’s culture values strong family hierarchy tells you something worth knowing. It doesn’t tell you whether this student embraces that hierarchy, quietly resists it, or feels both ways at once. It doesn’t tell you what she needs from you today. The category describes a group. The student in front of you is a person, shaped by culture and also by temperament, family, experience, and her own growing sense of who she is.

The practical habit is to use cultural knowledge to ask better questions, not to reach quick answers. Not “students from this background usually value X, so this student must too,” but “I noticed she seems uncomfortable when I praise her in front of the class. I wonder if that’s cultural, personal, or something I haven’t seen yet. I should find out.” That is the difference between cultural assumption and cultural curiosity.

Students’ cultures as a resource

The richest things your students’ cultures bring to class aren’t foods and holidays. They are different ways of thinking, valuing, and making sense of life. A student from a family that puts the group ahead of the individual has something to add to a discussion of what we owe one another. A student whose faith treats suffering as meaningful has something to add to a discussion of resilience. You can learn a great deal from cultural difference, as long as you are curious enough to find out what each student actually brings.

Retrieval practiceYou learn that a student comes from a culture with strong family hierarchy. A colleague says, “Now I understand why she never speaks up in class. It’s cultural.” What is right and wrong about this conclusion?►

What’s right: the colleague is using cultural knowledge as context instead of ignoring it, which keeps them from reading her silence as laziness or indifference. As a starting guess, it’s better than the default. What’s wrong: “Now I understand why” treats the explanation as finished. She may embrace her family’s norms, resist them, or feel both ways. She might also be quiet for other reasons entirely: anxiety, a hard week, a class dynamic she’s still reading, or simply not having been asked a question she cares about. The better move is to hold the cultural explanation as one possibility and find out, by watching and asking, what actually fits. “I wonder if” is more accurate than “now I understand why.”

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

Every classroom holds many cultures, including yours. Each idea in this chapter has a concrete classroom expression.

  • Ask “problem or difference?” before you interpret. A student who avoids eye contact, defers to the group, or stays quiet may be showing respect or reading the room, not checking out. And examine your own cultural lens as often as you examine your students’. (Concept 1\)
  • Build the peer norms you want. Recognize the behavior you hope will spread, collect students’ thinking before discussion, and use shared routines to build a sense of “we.” (Concept 2\)
  • Connect learning to meaning, and explain your norms. Students who value authenticity respond to “why this matters” more than “because I said so.” (Concept 3\)
  • Teach “Is this within my control?” Help students sort what they can act on from what they can’t, especially after a setback. (Concept 4\)
  • Sort a controversial question before you respond. Is it genuinely contested, edgy but answerable, or empirically contested? Each calls for a different response. (Concept 5\)
  • Use cultural knowledge to ask better questions. “I wonder if” is more accurate than “now I understand why.” (Concept 6\)
Chapter synthesis
The old fish asked a simple question, and the young fish couldn’t answer it. By now, you should be able to. Culture is the water every person in your classroom swims in, including you. No one can get out of the water, but you can learn to notice it, so you can see what you’re assuming, what you might be missing, and what each student brings that your own lens hasn’t yet shown you.
Culture is invisible from the inside. What feels most natural is often most cultural. Teachers can’t be culturally neutral, but they can be culturally aware.
Groups enforce culture quietly. Conformity is a normal human tendency, and peer norms in a classroom have to be built on purpose.
Expressive individualism shapes many of your students. Work with it: connect learning to meaning, explain your norms, and help students separate preference from identity.
Stoicism offers a different way to live. “Is this within my control?” is a skill students can learn, and it connects directly to the agency in Chapter 16.
Not every controversy is the same. Sort the question before you respond, and teach students how to think, not what to think.
Cultural knowledge gives context, not conclusions. The student in front of you is always more than any category.

The traditions your students carry are not obstacles to their education. They are invitations to think more broadly than any one culture can manage alone.

Worth remembering
  1. Tradition is just peer pressure from dead people. Sometimes. Examine it, then keep the good ones on purpose.
  2. Peer culture is a design problem, not a discipline problem.
