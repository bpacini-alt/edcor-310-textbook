Attention, Memory & Cognitive Load

Way Less Is Way More

Chapter 22~ 50 min read

There’s a popular story that phone numbers are seven digits long because of a psychology paper. In 1956, George Miller argued that people can hold about seven items in mind at once, and Bell Labs supposedly built phone numbers to fit. The story isn’t quite right, since seven-digit numbers came first. It has stuck around anyway because it matches our experience: a phone number is about as much as most of us can hold in our heads.

The real limit is smaller than seven. The space where you hold and work with new information is called your working memory. When researchers stop people from grouping or repeating what they see, most adults can hold only about four items at once, give or take one. You can remember a phone number because you don’t hold seven separate digits. You hold a group of three and a group of four.

Four slots isn’t much room, and every new term, direction, slide, and side story in a lesson competes for them. When a lesson asks students to hold more than that at once, something gets dropped, and it’s often the thing the lesson was about.

The big picture
Before students can learn something, they have to notice it, hold it in mind, and store it where they can find it later. This chapter follows that path, from selective attention through working memory to long-term memory. Then it turns to the practical question: how do you design lessons and practice so that more of what you teach makes it all the way through?

Major concept 1 · The First Gate

Selective Attention

Right now, your eyes are on this page, but your brain is also tracking the noise around you, the time, and maybe a text you haven't answered. It can't fully process all of it. So it picks. This process is called selective attention: the brain's process of choosing what to focus on while filtering out everything else.

Selective attention is the first gate in learning. The brain doesn't take in the world like an open window. It lets a narrow slice through and drops almost everything else. If a lesson doesn't make it through that gate, it can't be learned, no matter how good the lesson is.

Classic finding — The Cocktail Party Effect

Picture a loud party. You're deep in conversation and not listening to anyone else. Then someone across the room says your name, and you turn around. This is the cocktail party effect. In the 1950s, Colin Cherry played two messages at once, one in each ear, and asked people to repeat only one of them. They could tell almost nothing about the other message. Neville Moray (1959) then slipped people's names into the ignored message. About a third of them noticed their name. Later studies found about the same rate (Wood & Cowan, 1995; Röer & Cowan, 2021).

Both halves of that result matter. The brain does keep some watch on what it's ignoring, which is why a buzzing phone or a whisper can pull a student out of a lesson. But most people in those studies didn't notice their own name at all. Attention filters out far more than we think it does.

There is a difference between seeing and perceiving. A student can look straight at you and not take in a word. A cluttered slide, a noisy hallway, or a worry from home can pull attention away before the lesson ever registers. The classroom isn't a neutral backdrop. Everything in it competes for the same limited attention the lesson needs.

Think of attention as a spotlight that teachers can help aim. They can do it in two ways. They can change the room, by putting phones away and cutting visual clutter. They can also teach students metacognitive habits, like noticing when their attention drifts and steering it back.

The attention tax: phones and the evidence

In 2017, a widely shared study found that just having a phone on the desk, even face-down and silent, lowered people's scores on a working memory task (Ward et al., 2017). Later studies produced mixed results. A careful replication found no effect at all (Ruiz Pardo & Minda, 2022), and a 2024 meta-analysis that pooled dozens of studies found only a small effect on working memory (Parry, 2024).

The evidence on school-wide bans is newer. In November 2026, a Harvard team compared test scores before and after schools began locking phones in pouches for the whole day. Scores grew a little faster, about one to two extra weeks of learning a year, and the gains held for up to three years (Kane et al., 2026). An earlier national study of the same kind of policy found average test-score effects near zero, with small gains in high schools and small losses in middle schools (Allcott et al., 2026). Both are working papers, which means neither has been through peer review yet.

So the payoff looks small and is still debated. Even small gains may add up over time, but schools should weigh cost and implementation alongside the uncertain academic effects. A scholar-practitioner weighs the evidence honestly and keeps watching for better data. That teacher can reasonably adopt a strict phone policy while being honest about what it can and can't promise. Frame the policy as protecting attention, not as punishment, and teach students healthy habits for the hours they do have their phones.

Both colleagues overstate the evidence.

  • What's solid: Attention is limited, and anything that pulls at it, like a buzzing phone, competes with the lesson. Even the cocktail party research shows the brain keeps some watch on what it's ignoring.

  • What's not settled: The famous "phone on the desk" study mostly hasn't replicated. The newest study of school bans found small gains, about one to two weeks of learning a year. An earlier national study found average test-score effects near zero, and neither study has been peer reviewed yet.

  • A leader's recommendation: The likely payoff is small, but it may add up over several years, so a strict ban is reasonable to adopt if the school can handle the cost and enforcement. Present it honestly: it protects attention during class, but it won't fix everything. Suggest the school track its own data, like grades, discipline referrals, and student reports, for a year and revisit the decision.

Major concept 2 · When Attention Is Too Busy to Wander

Perceptual Load

Selective attention explains what the brain lets in. Psychologist Nilli Lavie asked a related question: how much room is left over for distraction? Her answer is called perceptual load. The brain has only so much attention to spend. When a task uses most of it, there's little left for distractions. When a task uses very little, the leftover attention doesn't sit still. It goes looking for something else to do (Lavie, 2005).

This is why a student working on a hard, interesting problem barely notices the hallway noise, while the same student drifts during a dull worksheet. The difference isn't character or motivation. It's how much of the student's attention the task is actually using.

A famous experiment shows how full attention can be. Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris (1999) asked people to watch a video of players passing basketballs and to count the passes.

Partway through, a person in a gorilla suit walked through the scene, thumped their chest, and walked off. About half of the viewers never saw the gorilla. They were paying close attention, just to the passes. Attention works like a spotlight, and the gorilla was outside the beam.

For teachers, perceptual load points to two things.

  • The room matters. Posters, borders, and displays that have nothing to do with today's lesson all compete for attention.

  • The task matters. A lesson that's too easy doesn't free up attention for learning. It frees it up for distraction. The goal is work that is demanding enough to hold the spotlight without overwhelming students.

Lavie's research mostly used lab tasks, like spotting letters on a screen. Applying it to whole lessons is a reasonable extension, but it is an extension. It also has limits. In a 2026 trial in a summer program for children with ADHD, a low-distraction workspace, with noise-canceling headphones and a desk divider, didn't improve their behavior or schoolwork (Fabiano et al., 2026). Reducing distractions alone is not a guaranteed fix; teachers still need to see whether the change helps their students learn.

Flow and calibrated challenge

Psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (1990) spent decades studying flow. He found that flow tends to happen when a task's challenge matches a person's skill. When the task is too easy, people get bored. When it's too hard, they get anxious. Between those two is where people do their best work and often enjoy it most. Perceptual load points to the same middle ground: hard enough to hold attention, not so hard that the mind floods. Both ideas suggest that matching challenge to skill is one of the most useful things a teacher can do.

The classroom decoration debate

Many teachers, especially in elementary schools, see a richly decorated room as a sign of warmth. Two lines of research complicate that.

The HEAD Project followed 3,766 students in 153 classrooms over a school year (Barrett et al., 2015). Students made less progress in rooms with very high visual complexity, but bare rooms didn't do best either. The best results came from a middle level: displays that were organized, purposeful, and tied to current learning. Because the study observed real classrooms instead of changing them, it shows a link, not proof of cause.

A Carnegie Mellon experiment tested the question directly (Fisher et al., 2014). Kindergartners took six science lessons in a lab classroom, sometimes with heavily decorated walls and sometimes with bare ones. In the decorated room, children spent more time off task and learned less. They answered 42% of test questions correctly, compared with 55% in the bare room. The lead researcher didn't recommend stripping classrooms. She suggested teachers ask whether some displays might be distracting. A later study found that children didn't get used to the decorations even after two weeks (Godwin et al., 2022).

The question to ask isn't "Does this make my room feel welcoming?" It's "Does this need to be visible right now?"

Retrieval practiceA second-grade teacher has covered every wall with colorful posters, student art, and charts from every unit this year. Her students seem distracted during lessons. Using perceptual load and the decoration research, explain what might be happening. What would you suggest, and what would you avoid?►

The walls may be using up attention the lesson needs. Every display that isn't tied to today's learning competes for the same limited attention. Young children are especially pulled by what they see, and the Carnegie Mellon study found kindergartners learned less in a heavily decorated room and stayed distracted even after two weeks.

Suggest an audit instead of a bare room. Keep what supports current learning, like this unit's anchor charts, and take down or rotate the rest. Student work can stay, displayed where it doesn't sit behind the teacher during instruction. Avoid the opposite extreme: the HEAD Project found that rooms with too little visual interest didn't do best either. Then watch whether attention improves, since the evidence comes mostly from young children and one large correlational study.

Major concept 3 · What Happens After the Gate

Memory

Once attention lets something in, memory decides what happens to it. Memory isn't a single storage box. It's a set of processes:

  • Encoding turns what we notice into a form the mind can work with.

  • Storage keeps it over time.

  • Retrieval brings it back when we need it.

A breakdown at any step means forgetting, and instruction can cause a breakdown at each one.

Information moves through three stores, and each works differently.

Memory store How much it holds How long it lasts What it means for teaching
Sensory memory A lot, unfiltered Less than a second Students notice what's new or stands out, so open lessons in a way that grabs attention on purpose.
Working memory About 4 chunks A few seconds unless rehearsed This is the bottleneck. Every part of a lesson either uses this space well or wastes it.
Long-term memory No known limit Can last a lifetime This is the goal. Information gets here through thinking, connecting, and retrieving.

Working memory is the chokepoint. It's where all conscious thinking happens, and it is small.

How small is working memory?

In 1956, psychologist George Miller published a famous paper called "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two." He argued that people can hold about seven items in mind at once. Later research lowered that estimate. When people can't group or rehearse items, Nelson Cowan (2001) found the limit is closer to four. Without rehearsal, items also fade within seconds. Four is the number to plan around. Working memory is much smaller than most teachers assume.

That raises a puzzle. If students can only hold about four things, how does anyone read a paragraph or solve a multi-step problem?

Chunking

Miller also described the brain's workaround. Chunking groups pieces of information into one meaningful unit. Letters chunk into words, and words chunk into phrases. A phone number chunks into a group of three digits and a group of four. Single math facts chunk into a procedure. A familiar pattern can be handled as one unit rather than many separate pieces.

The pieces have to be learned first. Chess experts can glance at a board from a real game and rebuild it almost perfectly, while beginners remember only a few pieces. But when the pieces are placed randomly, experts do barely better than beginners (Chase & Simon, 1973). The experts don't have bigger working memories. They have years of patterns stored in long-term memory, so they see a few familiar groups where a beginner sees dozens of separate pieces.

Psychologists call these organized patterns of knowledge schemas. The more connected schemas a student has for a topic, the less working memory new material on that topic costs.

This is why fluency matters. When a skill is practiced until it runs on its own, it stops taking up working memory. A sixth grader who still counts on her fingers to multiply uses working memory on 7 × 8, and has less left for the word problem the lesson is about. A student who reads fluently has room to think about what the story means. So practice until the skill is automatic, not just until the answer is right.

Two opposite meanings of “chunking”

Teachers use the word “chunking” in two ways that run in opposite directions. Both protect working memory.

Breaking down is what the teacher does. A big task gets split into small steps so students hold less at once. That’s how the supports in Chapter 21 work, like handing out an essay assignment one step at a time.

Building up is what happens in the learner. With practice, separate steps fuse into one unit, so more fits in each slot. This is Miller’s meaning, and it’s how this chapter uses the word.

Learning to drive shows both. A new driver handles every move on its own: check the mirror, signal, look over your shoulder, ease into the lane. Each one takes up a slot. After enough practice, “change lanes” becomes one smooth action, and there’s working memory left over to watch the road. Good teaching follows the same path. Isolate each move, practice it until it runs on its own, then put the moves together. You break a skill down so students can build it back up.

Exposure isn't learning

Hearing information once only exposes students to it. Getting it into long-term memory takes attention, connection to what students already know, time, and repeated retrieval. That's why students who quiz themselves usually remember more than students who reread their notes.

Retrieval practiceA ninth grader reads her biology notes three times the night before a test. She scores well, but two weeks later she remembers almost nothing. Using the three memory stores, explain what went wrong. Then explain why a classmate who knows the parts of a cell well might find the next unit, on cell processes, easier to follow.►

What went wrong: Rereading kept the material in working memory long enough to feel familiar and to work for a test the next morning. But familiar isn't the same as stored. Moving information into long-term memory takes thinking about it, connecting it to what you know, and pulling it back out over time. She did none of that, so the memories faded once the test was over. She should have read once, then quizzed herself from memory across several days.

Why her classmate has an easier time: The classmate's knowledge of the cell is stored in long-term memory as chunks. When the next unit mentions mitochondria or the membrane, each term takes up one slot in working memory instead of many, which leaves room to think about the new process. Prior knowledge works like extra working memory.

Major concept 4 · Making It Stick

Learning Strategies

In 2009, cognitive scientist Daniel Willingham wrote a book that became popular with teachers, Why Don't Students Like School? His answer starts with how the brain works.

Key aphorism

The brain is not designed for thinking. It’s designed to save you from having to think.

Daniel Willingham, Why Don’t Students Like School? (2009)

Thinking is slow, effortful, and uncertain, so the brain leans on memory and habit whenever it can. That's why students drift toward study strategies that let them feel busy without thinking hard, like rereading and highlighting. It also gives teachers a simple test for any lesson: if students can get through an activity without thinking, most of them will. Two principles from Willingham's work sit underneath nearly everything in this section.

Key aphorism

Memory is the residue of thought.

Daniel Willingham, Why Don’t Students Like School? (2009)

That's the first principle. What a student remembers depends on what they actually thought about, not on what they were shown. A student who spends class coloring a diagram is thinking about colors. A student asked to explain the diagram from memory is thinking about the content.

The second principle is harder to accept. The strategies that feel most productive, like rereading, highlighting, and cramming, tend to produce the weakest long-term memories. Rereading makes material feel familiar, but feeling familiar isn't the same as knowing it. The strategies that feel harder, like retrieval, spacing, and interleaving, tend to produce the strongest.

About the Aphorism

Where it comes from: cognitive scientist Daniel Willingham uses this line in his column for teachers, “What Will Improve a Student’s Memory?” (American Educator, Winter 2008–2009), and in Why Don’t Students Like School? (2009). His practical advice follows from it: look at each lesson plan in terms of what students are likely to think about.

What it means here: a lesson leaves behind whatever students’ minds were busy with. If an activity has them thinking about color choices, glue, or a funny video, that’s what sticks.

Where it stops: thinking about something once doesn’t guarantee you’ll remember it next month. Retrieval, spacing, and what students already know also shape what lasts, which is why the rest of this concept covers them.

The problem: forgetting

In the 1880s, German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus memorized lists of nonsense syllables, like DAX and BUP, and tested himself after different delays. He found that memory drops quickly at first and then levels off. This pattern is called the forgetting curve. A researcher repeated his experiment in 2015, spending 70 hours learning and relearning lists, and found nearly the same curve (Murre & Dros, 2015). The exact shape of forgetting varies, but coming back to material and retrieving it can strengthen later recall.

The exact numbers from Ebbinghaus don't transfer to a classroom. He was memorizing nonsense, and meaningful material is easier to keep. But the shape of the curve holds up. It's why the course workbook asks you to do a warm-up at the start of class and a 48-hour check two days later: each one catches the memory before it drops too far.

The fix: desirable difficulties

The strategies that fight the forgetting curve share something odd: they all make learning feel harder. Psychologist Robert Bjork calls these desirable difficulties, learning conditions that make practice feel harder in the moment but make learning last longer (Bjork & Bjork, 2011). The struggle of pulling an answer from memory, or of coming back to material after you've started to forget it, is part of what makes the memory stronger.

This connects to antifragility, the idea that some things grow stronger through challenge, as long as the challenge is at the right level. Memory works that way. Not every difficulty helps, though. A confusing worksheet is hard too, and it doesn't build anything. The difficulties worth keeping are the ones that make students do the remembering.

It also helps explain why students resist these strategies. Think of the elephant and the rider: the fast, emotional elephant and the slow, deliberate rider who tries to steer. The elephant goes by feel. Rereading feels smooth, so the elephant decides it's working. Retrieval feels like failing, so the elephant wants to stop. The rider has to trust the evidence over the feeling.

Retrieval practice

Retrieval practice means pulling information out of memory, without looking at notes, in order to strengthen it. Trying to recall makes memory stronger, even when it is hard. When the first answer is wrong, checking and correcting it helps students learn the right one. Researchers call this the testing effect, and it is one of the best-supported findings in learning research (Rowland, 2014; Adesope et al., 2017).

A well-known study by Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke (2006) shows both the benefit and why students miss it. College students read short science passages. Then they either reread them or took a recall test with no feedback.

  • Five minutes later, the rereaders did better (81% vs. 75%).

  • One week later, the result flipped. The tested group remembered 56%, and the rereaders remembered 42%.

  • In a second experiment, students who read once and then tested three times remembered 61% after a week. Students who read four times remembered 40%. Yet the students who reread felt the most confident they would remember.

That is the whole problem in one study. The strategy that won in the short run, and felt best, lost in the long run.

1Findings

Dunlosky and colleagues’ review of common learning techniques rated highlighting and rereading as low-utility strategies compared with practice testing and distributed practice. That does not mean either is forbidden; it means they are weak foundations for durable learning when used alone.

Retrieval practice asks learners to close the book, answer a question, explain an idea, or solve a problem before checking the answer. In Roediger and Karpicke’s experiments, testing produced better delayed retention than simply restudying, even though restudying could produce higher immediate confidence. The uncomfortable method was the more reliable one.

2Nuance and evidence strength

Evidence map

1Retrieval practice usually beats passive restudy for delayed retention.strong
2Fluency and confidence can mislead learners about what they know.strong

Difficulty alone is not proof that a strategy is working.

Highlighting and rereading can still help with first exposure, orientation, locating a detail, or correcting an error after retrieval. The stronger point is about sequence and dosage: retrieve first, then review what you missed. Retrieval is also most useful when learners receive feedback and revisit the material later.

There is a metacognitive mismatch here. Familiarity can feel like mastery, while trying to recall exposes gaps. That is why effective learning can feel less productive than passive review. The signal to trust is not “this feels smooth”; it is whether you can produce, explain, and use the idea later.

3Classroom application

Make retrieval the first move: close the notes and brain-dump what you remember, answer a few questions, teach the idea aloud, or solve a problem. Then check the source, correct the gaps, and try again later. Use highlighting or rereading as targeted repair, not as the whole study session.

Sources, methods, and limitations

  • Roediger & Karpicke (2006), “Test-enhanced learning: taking memory tests improves long-term retention.” Shows delayed-retention benefits of retrieval over restudying, with confidence sometimes pointing the wrong way.
  • Koriat & Bjork (2005), “Illusions of competence in monitoring one’s knowledge during study.” Explains why conditions that make material easy to recognize can inflate judgments of learning.

Spaced practice

Spaced practice means spreading study and review out over time instead of cramming it into one session. Picture two students who each study for three hours. One studies for three hours the night before the test. The other studies for an hour on each of three different days. On a test a few weeks later, the second student will usually remember much more, even though both spent the same amount of time (Cepeda et al., 2006). The forgetting curve shows why. Each spaced session comes back just as the memory has started to fade, and pulling it back strengthens it.

The catch is that spacing feels worse. Coming back after a few days, students realize how much they've forgotten, and that feels like failure. Cramming feels productive because everything is fresh. How learning feels is not a reliable guide to how well it's working.

Interleaving

Interleaved practice means mixing different but related types of problems during practice, so learners have to decide which approach each one needs. Picture a math homework set on volume. In a blocked set, all ten problems are about cylinders, so the student knows the formula before reading the problem. In an interleaved set, cylinders, cones, and spheres are mixed together, and every problem starts with a question: what kind of shape is this, and what does it call for? In one study, students who practiced this way did far better on a test a week later, even though they did worse during practice (Rohrer & Taylor, 2007).

That first step, figuring out what kind of problem you're facing, is what interleaving trains. Real problems don't come sorted by type. A blocked set skips that step, because the answer to "what kind of problem is this?" is printed at the top of the page.

The evidence is strong but uneven. A review of 59 studies found a moderate benefit overall, with the largest gains when the categories were easy to confuse with each other, like paintings by similar artists or similar math problem types (Brunmair & Richter, 2019). The benefit was smaller, or even reversed, for other materials, like word lists. So interleave things students need to tell apart. Mixing unrelated subjects, like math and history, isn't what the research tested. Many teachers also introduce a new skill on its own first and mix it in once students have the basics. That's a sensible approach, though the research doesn't settle exactly when to start mixing.

Retrieval practiceA seventh-grade math teacher assigns 20 practice problems each night, all on the day's lesson. Students do well on homework but poorly on the unit test, which mixes problem types. Using spacing, interleaving, and desirable difficulties, explain what's happening. Redesign the homework, and predict how students will react.►

What's happening: Each homework set is blocked and massed. Students always know which method to use, because it's whatever was taught that day, so they never practice choosing. And they never return to older material, so it slides down the forgetting curve before the test. The homework scores measure short-term performance, not long-term learning.

Redesign: Cut the nightly set to about 10 problems. Make half of them today's lesson. Make the other half a mix of earlier problem types, especially ones students tend to confuse, like finding area versus perimeter, or adding versus multiplying fractions. That adds both spacing and interleaving without adding time.

Prediction: Homework scores will probably drop at first, and students may complain that it feels harder. That's the desirable difficulty at work. The teacher should explain the reason in advance and judge the change by the unit test and by how much students remember a month later, not by how the homework feels.

Major concept 5 · Design for the Brain You Have

Cognitive Load Theory

Everything so far leads to one practical conclusion. If attention is narrow and working memory holds only a few things, then every lesson is a decision about how to spend that space. Cognitive load theory is the idea that working memory can only handle so much at once, so instruction should spend that space on what students need to learn. Psychologist John Sweller developed it in the late 1980s, and it has become one of the most useful frameworks in educational psychology (Sweller et al., 2019).

The theory separates two kinds of load.

Two kinds of cognitive load

Intrinsic load comes from the content itself. It depends on how many pieces students have to hold together at once, and on how much they already know. A cell diagram is simple for a biology teacher and overwhelming for a freshman. It's the same diagram with a different intrinsic load. Teachers can't remove intrinsic load without changing what students learn, but they can manage it by teaching parts before wholes and by building background knowledge first.

Extraneous load comes from how the lesson is designed: cluttered slides, confusing directions, or information split between a handout and the board that students have to piece together. Extraneous load is the teacher's to cut.

Whatever happened to germane load?

Older versions of the theory, and many teacher resources, list a third type: germane load, the "good" effort of building understanding. In 2019, Sweller and his colleagues revised that. Germane load is no longer treated as a separate type of load. It now describes the working memory a student actually spends on the content itself (Sweller et al., 2019). Other researchers have made the same change (Kalyuga & Plass, 2025). The practical advice is the same, and simpler. Every bit of extraneous load you cut frees up working memory for the learning that matters.

Worked examples

One of the best-tested ways to manage intrinsic load is the worked example: a problem with every step already solved. A beginner who solves problems from scratch spends most of their working memory searching for what to do next. Studying a worked solution spends that working memory on how the solution works. Sweller and Cooper first showed this with algebra students in 1985. A 2023 meta-analysis of 55 randomized studies found that worked examples moderately improved math performance, from elementary school through college (Barbieri et al., 2023).

The benefit fades as students gain skill. Once students know how to solve a type of problem, more worked examples add little and can even get in the way. Researchers call this the expertise reversal effect (Kalyuga et al., 2003). So start a new skill with full worked examples, then fade them. Leave the last step blank, then the last two, until students solve whole problems on their own.

Research Side-Quest: Active Learning Can Feel Like Less Learning Students can learn more in an active class while feeling less certain that they learned anything. ▶ Watch + research notes
Watch on YouTube
1Findings

In a controlled college-physics study, Deslauriers and colleagues compared lecture-style and active-learning sessions using the same course content and materials. Students in the active condition performed better on the assessment, but they rated their own learning lower; students in the lecture condition felt they had learned more even when their scores were lower.

The broader pattern is not limited to one classroom. Freeman and colleagues’ meta-analysis of 225 undergraduate STEM studies found that active learning improved exam performance and reduced failure compared with traditional lecturing. The important contrast is between what learners can do and what the experience feels like while they are doing it.

2Nuance and evidence strength

Evidence map

1Active learning improves average performance in undergraduate STEM courses.strong
2Active learning can create a feeling-of-learning mismatch.strong, specific

The evidence does not say that lectures are useless or that discomfort guarantees learning.

Here, active learning means cognitively active learning: predicting, retrieving, explaining, solving, comparing, and getting feedback. It does not require students to move around the room, and “busy” is not the same as mentally engaged. Short explanations can be part of an active sequence when students then have to use the idea.

The most precise takeaway is not “lecture bad, activity good.” It is that teachers should measure learning with independent questions, explanations, and problems rather than with students’ immediate confidence, visible enthusiasm, or silence.

3Classroom application

Explain productive struggle before you ask for it. Use short explanations followed by prediction, individual problem solving, explanation to a partner, comparison of answers, and feedback. Check what students can do independently; do not use how comfortable or entertained they look as the main measure of learning.

This means a lesson can feel engaging and still block learning. A packed, flashy lesson can use up working memory on things that have nothing to do with the content. Interesting but irrelevant material does more than fail to help. A funny video, a surprising story, or a vivid example that's off the point can pull attention away from the content that needed it. Researchers call these seductive details. Across many studies, adding them lowered learning (Sundararajan & Adesope, 2020).

That points to a common misconception: the engagement trap, which is mistaking busy, happy students for students who are learning. Students can be busy, excited, and enjoying themselves while remembering almost nothing. Enjoyment matters. But the better test of a lesson is what students will remember in three weeks.

Choice and cognitive load

Psychologist Barry Schwartz made the "paradox of choice" famous: the idea that too many options cause stress and wasted mental energy. The evidence is weaker than the idea's fame suggests. Across 50 experiments, more options made little difference on average (Scheibehenne et al., 2010). But choice overload does show up when people know little about what they're choosing, which describes students learning something new. When students get open-ended choices, like any topic, any format, or any approach, deciding can use up working memory that learning needed. A student stuck at that point can look unmotivated when the real problem is overload. Two or three meaningful options respect students' independence while keeping the load manageable.

Before any lesson, ask three questions:

  • Does every part of this slide, handout, or activity support what I want students to learn?

  • Are my directions in one place, or do students have to hold information from several sources at once?

  • Could I break this task into smaller steps, so there's less to hold at any moment?

A gospel lens: line upon line

The Lord describes his own way of teaching in 2 Nephi 28:30: "I will give unto the children of men line upon line, precept upon precept." The verse goes on to say that those who listen to his counsel learn wisdom and receive more.

The verse is about how the Lord gives knowledge to his children, not about working memory. Cognitive load theory comes to a similar practical conclusion by a different road. Students can't take in everything at once, so a wise teacher sequences instruction, building each new piece on one that's already secure. A teacher who follows this pattern isn't lowering expectations. The goal is still the whole truth. The pace is set so students can actually receive it.

Retrieval practiceA first-year teacher on your team asks for feedback. To make her lesson on the water cycle more engaging, she plans to open with a funny viral video, add animated transitions and sound effects to every slide, and end with a free-choice project ("Show what you learned any way you want!"). Using cognitive load theory, what feedback would you give her? Keep in mind that she's new and you want her to keep trying new ideas.►

Start with what's right: she wants students engaged, and that matters. Then look at each addition by what students will spend working memory on.

  • The viral video: If it isn't about the water cycle, it's a seductive detail. Students will remember the video, because memory is the residue of thought. Suggest swapping it for a short, striking clip that is about the content, like a time-lapse of clouds forming.

  • The animations and sound effects: These are extraneous load. Each one asks students to process motion and sound instead of the idea. Suggest building slides one step at a time only when the order of the steps matters, like evaporation, then condensation, then precipitation.

  • The free-choice project: Unlimited choice can overload students who are new to the topic. Suggest two or three structured options, like a labeled diagram, a short explanation, or a model with a written key.

As a leader, frame it as a shared question instead of a correction: "What do we want students thinking about in each part of this lesson?" Then offer to compare results. You could each give the same quick quiz a week later and look at the results together.

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

The chapter comes down to a single design question asked before the lesson: what will students actually think about, and is there enough working memory space to think about it well?

  • Audit the room. Phones on desks, displays unrelated to the current lesson, and general clutter all compete for the same limited attention the lesson needs. Put away what doesn’t need to be there right now, then watch whether students’ focus actually improves.
  • Aim for the right level of challenge. A task that’s too easy leaves attention free to wander. A task that’s too hard floods working memory. Work that is demanding but doable holds attention best, and it’s also where flow tends to happen.
  • Build retrieval practice into every week. Keep it low-stakes and ungraded so it feels normal. Start class with three questions from the last lesson, or end with a brain dump. Pulling information out of memory strengthens it; rereading mostly makes it feel familiar.
  • Space your reviews. Plan to come back to material from two weeks ago in a warm-up, and to last month’s material in a mixed problem set. Spacing can feel slow, so judge it by what students remember later, not by how the practice feels.
  • Cut extraneous load. Look for irrelevant images on slides, directions split between the board and a handout, and tangents that pull attention off the content. For each one, ask whether it’s worth the working memory it costs.
  • Offer structured choices. Two or three meaningful options respect students’ independence while keeping the load manageable. Wide-open choice can use up working memory on deciding, especially for students who are new to the topic.
Chapter synthesis
Learning takes effort, and some of that effort is what makes it last. Attention lets in a narrow slice of the world. Working memory holds about four new things at once, for a few seconds. Long-term memory is vast, but information gets there only when students think about it, connect it to what they know, and pull it back out over time. Teachers who understand those limits design differently: less clutter, more retrieval, practice that is spaced and mixed, and fewer, better choices. When retrieval and spaced review feel hard, that alone is no sign that something has gone wrong.
Selective attention is the first gate. The brain filters out most of what reaches it, and a lesson that doesn’t get through can’t be learned. Some unattended information still breaks through, like your own name, which is why a buzzing phone or a whisper can pull a student away.
Perceptual load and flow point to the same advice: match the challenge to students’ skill. Too easy leaves attention free to wander; too hard floods working memory.
Exposure isn’t learning. Getting information into long-term memory takes attention, connection, time, and retrieval, not a single presentation. Memory is the residue of thought: students remember what they actually thought about.
Retrieval, spacing, and interleaving feel harder than rereading and cramming, and they usually produce stronger long-term memory. The useful difficulty is the work of remembering and of telling problems apart. Confusing directions and distractions are hard too, but they don’t help.
Cognitive load theory gives teachers a design job: cut extraneous load, manage intrinsic load, and protect working memory for learning. Watch for the engagement trap. Busy, happy students aren’t always learning, so judge a lesson by what students remember later.

  2. Memory is the residue of thought.
