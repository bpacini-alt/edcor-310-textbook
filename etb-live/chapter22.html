How the Brain Learns, Part II

Lend Them Your Systems

Chapter 22~ 45 min read

In 1956, the psychologist George Miller published a paper with an unusually catchy title: “The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two.” He argued that people can hold about seven items in mind at once. The number caught on, and you can still hear it quoted today.

The real limit is both much smaller and much larger than seven. The space where you hold and work with new information is your working memory. When researchers stop people from grouping or repeating what they see, most adults can hold only about four new items at once, give or take one (Cowan, 2001).

The larger part was in Miller’s paper too. He noticed that people get around the limit by grouping pieces into bigger units, which he called chunks. A phone number is ten digits, but you remember it as three chunks. The more you know, the bigger your chunks can be.

If you hand a beginner a scrambled Rubik’s Cube, it feels overwhelming: every sticker is its own piece to track, and four slots fill up fast. An expert looks at the same cube and sees a few familiar patterns. Each pattern is one chunk, with its sequence of moves already stored in long-term memory. Both people have the same four slots, but the expert’s slots hold far more. This chapter is about how that happens: how knowledge gets into long-term memory, how it stays there, and how teachers can help students build it.

The big picture

Chapter 21 followed information from perception to encoding. This chapter picks up in long-term memory, which has no known limit but loses things fast unless students come back to them. What students already know makes working memory go further. The strategies that build lasting memory, like retrieval practice, spacing, and mixing problem types, feel harder than rereading and cramming. They work better anyway, and students need help trusting them.

The second half of the chapter returns to the CEO. When executive function struggles, a teacher sees behavior: a student who doesn’t start, loses the paper again, or blurts out the answer for the third time. Often the cause is a skill that isn’t there yet, and ADHD is one of the most common reasons. The classroom tools with the strongest evidence all work the same way. Build the system into the room, then hand it to the student a piece at a time. Teachers support, observe, and refer. They don’t diagnose.

Major concept 1 · What students know changes what they can think about

Long-Term Memory

Chapter 21 showed how small working memory is: about four new things at a time. Long-term memory is the opposite. It has no known limit, and it can hold what we learn for a lifetime.

That raises a puzzle. If students can only hold about four things, how does anyone read a paragraph or solve a multi-step problem?

Chunking

Miller also described the brain’s workaround. Chunking groups pieces of information into one meaningful unit. Letters chunk into words, and words chunk into phrases. A phone number chunks into a group of three digits and a group of four. Single math facts chunk into a procedure. A familiar pattern can be handled as one unit instead of many separate pieces.

The pieces have to be learned first. Chess experts can glance at a board from a real game and rebuild it almost perfectly, while beginners remember only a few pieces. But when the pieces are placed randomly, experts do barely better than beginners (Chase & Simon, 1973). The experts don’t have bigger working memories. They have years of patterns stored in long-term memory, so they see a few familiar groups where a beginner sees dozens of separate pieces.

Speedcubers, people who can solve a Rubik’s Cube in seconds, show the same thing. A beginner solves the cube one piece at a time and has to think through every turn. Most fast solvers use a method called CFOP, which includes 78 memorized patterns for the last layer alone, each paired with its own sequence of moves. When an expert spots one of those patterns, it takes up one slot in working memory, and the moves run from long-term memory almost on their own. That’s chunking and fluency working together.

Those stored patterns are the schemas you learned about in Chapter 11. The more connected schemas a student has for a topic, the less working memory new material on that topic costs. Prior knowledge works almost like extra working memory.

Chunking: two meanings►

In schools, “chunking” usually means breaking something big into smaller pieces: chunk the essay into steps, chunk the lecture into short segments. Chapter 21 called this breaking a task into steps, the same move that made Fryer’s book reward work. Psychologists usually mean the reverse: combining small pieces into one larger unit until it is familiar enough to handle as one.

Both are useful, and both come from the same fact about working memory: it is small. Breaking a task into steps keeps today’s load small enough to manage. Building chunks through practice makes each piece cost less tomorrow. Good teaching does both. Break it down so students can start, then practice until the pieces fuse.

Fluency

Fluency is when a skill has been practiced until it runs with little working memory, because long-term memory can supply it quickly and automatically. A sixth grader who still counts on her fingers to multiply spends working memory on 7 × 8, and has less left for the word problem the lesson is about. A student who reads fluently has room to think about what the story means.

The problem: forgetting

In the 1880s, German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus memorized lists of nonsense syllables, like DAX and BUP, and tested himself after different delays. He found that memory drops quickly at first and then levels off. This pattern is called the forgetting curve. A researcher repeated his experiment in 2015, spending 70 hours learning and relearning lists, and found nearly the same curve (Murre & Dros, 2015).

The exact numbers from Ebbinghaus don’t transfer to a classroom. He was memorizing nonsense, and meaningful material is easier to keep. But the shape of the curve holds up. It’s why the course workbook asks you to do a warm-up at the start of class and a 48-hour check two days later. Each one catches the memory before it drops too far.

Retrieval practiceA ninth grader reads her biology notes three times the night before a test. She scores well, but two weeks later she remembers almost nothing. Using the three memory stores, explain what went wrong. Then explain why a classmate who knows the parts of a cell well might find the next unit, on cell processes, easier to follow.►

What went wrong: Rereading kept the material in mind long enough to feel familiar and to work for a test the next morning. But moving information into long-term memory takes thinking about it, connecting it to what you know, and pulling it back out over time. She did none of that, so the memories faded once the test was over. She should have read once, then quizzed herself from memory across several days.

Why her classmate has an easier time: The classmate’s knowledge of the cell is stored in long-term memory as chunks. When the next unit mentions mitochondria or the membrane, each term takes up one slot in working memory instead of many, which leaves room to think about the new process.

Major concept 2 · Harder now, stronger later

Desirable Difficulties

If cognitive load theory says to cut the load, should learning always be made as easy as possible? No. Some difficulty helps. Psychologist Robert Bjork calls these desirable difficulties: learning conditions that make practice feel harder in the moment but make learning last longer (Bjork & Bjork, 2011). You met the idea in Chapter 1 as part of antifragility. The struggle of pulling an answer from memory, or of coming back to material after you’ve started to forget it, is part of what makes the memory stronger.

Cognitive load theory and desirable difficulties fit together once you ask what the effort is for. Effort spent on confusing directions, a cluttered slide, or an off-topic story builds nothing, so cut it. Effort spent recalling an idea, telling similar problems apart, or returning to material after a few days builds memory, so keep it. There is one condition. Students need enough knowledge to succeed at the hard part. If they’re only guessing, give them more explanation or a worked example first.

The rest of this concept covers the difficulties worth keeping. Together they serve two goals: retention, remembering what was learned weeks and months later, and transfer, using it in a new situation.

1Findings

In a controlled college-physics study, Deslauriers and colleagues compared lecture-style and active-learning sessions using the same course content and materials. Students in the active condition performed better on the assessment, but they rated their own learning lower; students in the lecture condition felt they had learned more even when their scores were lower.

The broader pattern is not limited to one classroom. Freeman and colleagues’ meta-analysis of 225 undergraduate STEM studies found that active learning improved exam performance and reduced failure compared with traditional lecturing. The important contrast is between what learners can do and what the experience feels like while they are doing it.

2Nuance and evidence strength

Evidence map

1Active learning improves average performance in undergraduate STEM courses.strong
2Active learning can create a feeling-of-learning mismatch.strong, specific

The evidence does not say that lectures are useless or that discomfort guarantees learning.

Here, active learning means cognitively active learning: predicting, retrieving, explaining, solving, comparing, and getting feedback. It does not require students to move around the room, and “busy” is not the same as mentally engaged. Short explanations can be part of an active sequence when students then have to use the idea.

The most precise takeaway is not “lecture bad, activity good.” It is that teachers should measure learning with independent questions, explanations, and problems rather than with students’ immediate confidence, visible enthusiasm, or silence.

3Classroom application

Explain productive struggle before you ask for it. Use short explanations followed by prediction, individual problem solving, explanation to a partner, comparison of answers, and feedback. Check what students can do independently; do not use how comfortable or entertained they look as the main measure of learning.

Retrieval practice

Retrieval practice means pulling information out of memory, without looking at notes, in order to strengthen it. Trying to recall makes memory stronger, even when it is hard. When the first answer is wrong, checking and correcting it helps students learn the right one. Researchers call this the testing effect, and it is one of the best-supported findings in learning research (Rowland, 2014; Adesope et al., 2017).

A well-known study by Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke (2006) shows both the benefit and why students miss it. College students read short science passages. Then they either reread them or took a recall test with no feedback.

  • Five minutes later, the rereaders did better (81% vs. 75%).
  • One week later, the result flipped. The tested group remembered 56%, and the rereaders remembered 42%.
  • In a second experiment, students who read once and then tested three times remembered 61% after a week. Students who read four times remembered 40%, yet they felt the most confident they would remember.

The strategy that won in the short run, and felt best, lost in the long run.

A gospel lens: remember, remember►

Remembering is something the scriptures ask of disciples again and again. In the sacrament prayers, members promise to “always remember him” (Moroni 4:3). Helaman told his sons, “remember, remember” (Helaman 5:12). These passages aren’t about study strategies, and the research doesn’t explain them. But they share a premise with this concept: remembering is something a person chooses to do, again and again.

Spaced practice

Spaced practice means spreading study and review out over time instead of cramming it into one session. Picture two students who each study for three hours. One studies for three hours the night before the test. The other studies for an hour on each of three different days. On a test a few weeks later, the second student will usually remember much more, even though both spent the same amount of time (Cepeda et al., 2006). The forgetting curve shows why. Each spaced session comes back just as the memory has started to fade, and pulling it back strengthens it.

The catch is that spacing feels worse. Coming back after a few days, students realize how much they’ve forgotten. Cramming feels productive because everything is fresh.

Interleaving

Real problems don’t come labeled. A student facing a word problem on a test first has to figure out what kind of problem it is, and only then which method to use. That first step is a skill students have to learn, and interleaved practice is one of the best ways to practice it. Interleaving means mixing different but related types of problems during practice, so learners have to decide which approach each one needs.

Picture a math homework set on volume. In a blocked set, all ten problems are about cylinders, so the student knows the formula before reading the problem. The answer to “what kind of problem is this?” is printed at the top of the page. In an interleaved set, cylinders, cones, and spheres are mixed together, and every problem starts with that question. In one study, students who practiced this way did far better on a test a week later, even though they did worse during practice (Rohrer & Taylor, 2007).

The benefit is largest when the problem types are easy to confuse. A review of 59 studies found a moderate benefit overall, with the biggest gains for categories that look alike, like paintings by similar artists or similar math problems (Brunmair & Richter, 2019). So mix the things students need to tell apart. Many teachers introduce a new skill on its own first, then mix it in once students have the basics.

Retrieval practiceA tenth grader studies vocabulary flashcards until she gets every card right, then stops for the night. A week later she has forgotten about half. She says, “I knew them all. Studying doesn’t work for me.” Using the forgetting curve and desirable difficulties, explain what happened and what she should change.►

What happened: Getting every card right showed the words were in memory that night. It didn’t show they would last. Memory drops fastest in the first days after learning, so a week later much of it was gone. That’s the forgetting curve doing what it always does, not proof that studying fails for her.

What to change: Stop treating “I got them all right” as the finish line. Come back to the cards a day later, then a few days after that, and try to recall each word before flipping the card. Those sessions will feel worse, because she’ll miss words she knew before. That struggle is the desirable difficulty, and each return slows the next drop.

Retrieval practiceA seventh-grade math teacher assigns 20 practice problems each night, all on the day’s lesson. Students do well on homework but poorly on the unit test, which mixes problem types. Using spacing, interleaving, and desirable difficulties, explain what’s happening. Redesign the homework, and predict how students will react.►

What’s happening: Each homework set is blocked and massed. Students always know which method to use, because it’s whatever was taught that day, so they never practice recognizing the kind of problem. And they never return to older material, so it slides down the forgetting curve before the test.

Redesign: Cut the nightly set to about 10 problems. Make half of them today’s lesson and the other half a mix of earlier problem types, especially ones students tend to confuse, like area versus perimeter or adding versus multiplying fractions. That adds both spacing and interleaving without adding time.

Prediction: Homework scores will probably drop at first, and students may complain that it feels harder. That’s the desirable difficulty at work. The teacher should explain the reason in advance and judge the change by the unit test and by what students remember a month later.

Major concept 3 · The rider teaches the elephant

Metacognition

If retrieval, spacing, and interleaving work so well, why don’t students use them on their own? Part of the answer is in another line from Willingham.

Key aphorism

The brain is not designed for thinking. It’s designed to save you from having to think.

Daniel Willingham, Why Don’t Students Like School? (2009)

Thinking is slow, effortful, and uncertain, so the brain leans on memory and habit whenever it can. That’s why students drift toward study strategies that let them feel busy without thinking hard, like rereading and highlighting. Those strategies feel productive. The ones that work, like retrieval and spacing, feel like failing. So students judge their learning by how it feels, and the feeling points them the wrong way.

The fix is metacognition, thinking about your own thinking. It has two parts. Monitoring is judging how well you know something: could I explain this tomorrow without my notes? Control is what you do with that judgment: keep studying, switch strategies, or move on. Monitoring and control are executive function aimed at your own learning.

Why monitoring goes wrong

When material looks familiar, it feels learned, so students stop studying too early (Koriat & Bjork, 2005). Rereading makes the page familiar, and familiarity passes for knowing it. The honest check is retrieval. Trying to recall an idea without looking gives a much truer reading of what you know.

Think back to the elephant and the rider from Chapter 2. The elephant goes by feel. Rereading feels smooth, so the elephant decides it’s working. Retrieval feels like failing, so the elephant wants to stop. The feeling of familiarity doesn’t go away, even for experts. What the rider can do is learn not to trust it, check with retrieval instead, and practice that check until it becomes a habit. Habits belong to the elephant. Over time, closing the book and testing yourself can start to feel like the normal way to study.

1Findings

Dunlosky and colleagues’ review of common learning techniques rated highlighting and rereading as low-utility strategies compared with practice testing and distributed practice. That does not mean either is forbidden; it means they are weak foundations for durable learning when used alone.

Retrieval practice asks learners to close the book, answer a question, explain an idea, or solve a problem before checking the answer. In Roediger and Karpicke’s experiments, testing produced better delayed retention than simply restudying, even though restudying could produce higher immediate confidence. The uncomfortable method was the more reliable one.

2Nuance and evidence strength

Evidence map

1Retrieval practice usually beats passive restudy for delayed retention.strong
2Fluency and confidence can mislead learners about what they know.strong

Difficulty alone is not proof that a strategy is working.

Highlighting and rereading can still help with first exposure, orientation, locating a detail, or correcting an error after retrieval. The stronger point is about sequence and dosage: retrieve first, then review what you missed. Retrieval is also most useful when learners receive feedback and revisit the material later.

There is a metacognitive mismatch here. Familiarity can feel like mastery, while trying to recall exposes gaps. That is why effective learning can feel less productive than passive review. The signal to trust is not “this feels smooth”; it is whether you can produce, explain, and use the idea later.

3Classroom application

Make retrieval the first move: close the notes and brain-dump what you remember, answer a few questions, teach the idea aloud, or solve a problem. Then check the source, correct the gaps, and try again later. Use highlighting or rereading as targeted repair, not as the whole study session.

Sources, methods, and limitations
  • Dunlosky et al. (2013), “Improving Students’ Learning With Effective Learning Techniques.” A review that rates highlighting and rereading low utility and practice testing and distributed practice more highly.
  • Roediger & Karpicke (2006), “Test-enhanced learning: taking memory tests improves long-term retention.” Shows delayed-retention benefits of retrieval over restudying, with confidence sometimes pointing the wrong way.
  • Koriat & Bjork (2005), “Illusions of competence in monitoring one’s knowledge during study.” Explains why conditions that make material easy to recognize can inflate judgments of learning.

Teaching it

Metacognition can be taught, and the evidence is encouraging. The Education Endowment Foundation’s review of hundreds of school studies rates teaching metacognition and self-regulation as one of the highest-impact, lowest-cost approaches, worth about seven to eight months of extra progress on average. A review of 48 programs found the effects held up after the programs ended (de Boer et al., 2018).

Two cautions keep those numbers honest. First, “metacognition” covers many different programs, and an average hides a wide spread. The programs that work teach specific strategies for planning, monitoring, and evaluating a particular kind of task, inside a subject. A general “learn how to learn” lesson doesn’t earn those results. Second, the impact depends heavily on how well the strategies are taught.

In a classroom, that can look like a teacher thinking aloud while she solves a problem: “Before I start, what kind of problem is this? Halfway through, does this answer make sense? At the end, how would I check it?” Students then ask themselves the same questions about their own work. Like self-monitoring, which comes later in this chapter, it starts with the teacher modeling the checks and ends with students running them on their own. A short retrieval quiz a few days later helps too, because it shows students whether their sense of “I’ve got this” was right.

Retrieval practiceBefore a Friday quiz, an eighth-grade teacher asks students to rate how ready they feel from 1 to 5. Then she gives a short practice quiz. Many students who rated themselves a 5 score poorly. What’s going on, and how could she use this moment?►

What’s going on: Their monitoring is off. Most students probably studied by rereading, so the material felt familiar, and familiarity felt like knowing it. The practice quiz was the first honest check.

How to use it: Show students the gap between their ratings and their scores, without shaming anyone. Name what happened: familiar isn’t the same as known. Then teach the check. Before deciding they’re ready, students close their notes and try to answer a few questions from memory. Repeat the rating-and-quiz routine before each test so students can see their predictions getting more accurate over time.

Major concept 4 · Look for the missing skill first

Executive Dysfunction and ADHD

In Chapter 21, executive function was the CEO that keeps the plan in mind, holds back the impulse, and shifts gears when things change. This concept is about what happens when the CEO struggles.

Will and skill

When a student doesn’t do what we asked, it’s easy to decide they don’t care. Sometimes that’s true. Students make real choices, and motivation matters; Chapters 19 and 20 are about it. But a lot of what looks like a choice is a skill that isn’t there yet. Will and skill call for different responses. A student who doesn’t want to needs a reason to try. A student who doesn’t know how needs teaching, practice, and support. Pressure aimed at a missing skill usually backfires, because the student learns to avoid the task instead of learning to do it.

So when a student isn’t doing it, get curious before you conclude anything. What happens right before the struggle? Does it show up in every class, or just one? Does the student know the steps? The answers usually point to what to try next.

Key aphorism

Kids do well if they can.

When a student keeps struggling, look first for a skill that isn’t there yet.

About the Aphorism

Where it comes from: “Kids do well if they can” is the title of Chapter 2 of Lost at School (2008) by Ross Greene, a clinical psychologist who works with children with serious behavior problems. Greene sets it against a more common belief: kids do well if they want to.

What it means here: when a student keeps failing at something, start by looking for a skill that’s missing. For executive function problems, that’s usually the right first guess. Doing well feels better than doing badly, so a student who keeps doing badly may not yet know how to do anything else.

Where it stops: Greene goes further than this book does. He argues that rewards and consequences should be replaced with a collaborative problem-solving process. The evidence in Concept 5 doesn’t support dropping them. Point systems and the Daily Report Card are among the best-supported classroom tools for ADHD. And will matters too, because students make real choices. So this chapter borrows Greene’s starting assumption, not his whole program. Look for the skill first, and let what you see tell you if you were wrong.

When executive function struggles

Everyone’s executive function breaks down sometimes. Try planning anything after a night without sleep, during finals week, or with the flu. When the trouble lasts and gets in the way of daily life, psychologists call it executive dysfunction. The term describes a pattern, not a diagnosis. Three questions help separate a real pattern from a bad week:

  • Does it last? Months, not a rough week.
  • Does it show up across settings? At home, at school, and with friends, not only in one class.
  • Does it interfere? It keeps the student from doing things they otherwise could.

In a classroom, executive dysfunction can look like a backpack that stays a mess, work that never gets started, or poor time management even after it’s been taught. It can also look like big reactions when plans change, or missing assignments that don’t match what the student knows.

It has many possible sources, including ADHD, autism, brain injury, depression, anxiety, learning disabilities, chronic stress, and poor sleep. That overlap is useful to know. The supports that help students with ADHD, like written directions, predictable routines, and smaller steps, help many other students too, with or without a diagnosis.

A gospel lens: look on the heart►

When the prophet Samuel went to anoint a new king, he was impressed by the appearance of Jesse’s oldest son. The Lord corrected him: “for the Lord seeth not as man seeth; for man looketh on the outward appearance, but the Lord looketh on the heart” (1 Samuel 16:7).

The Church’s guide for teachers, Teaching in the Savior’s Way, applies that principle to the classroom. It asks teachers to see learners the way God sees them, and it counsels, “Be careful not to make assumptions based only on what you see” (The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, 2022). The Church’s guidance for Primary teachers of children with disabilities makes the same point about behavior. It asks teachers to “pay careful attention to what is happening when challenging behaviors arise” (The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, 2023).

The research in this chapter reaches a similar warning by a different road. Behavior is what we see, and the cause is often hidden. Scripture teaches a disciple to look past outward appearance. The research gives a teacher practical ways to do it: watch for patterns, check across settings, and hold off on the quick verdict.

Retrieval practiceYou’re on a seventh-grade team. A colleague says about a shared student: “He does fine in PE and in shop. He can focus when he wants to. It’s just laziness.” Using the three pattern questions, how would you respond? What would you suggest the team do next?►

Start with what your colleague noticed, because it’s real. Different tasks make different demands on executive function. Work that is active, hands-on, or quickly rewarding asks less of a struggling system. Slow, routine work assigned by an adult asks the most. So doing well in PE and poorly on a worksheet fits executive dysfunction. It doesn’t rule it out.

Then ask the pattern questions. Has the struggle lasted? Does it show up in other classes and at home? Is it keeping him from doing work he knows how to do? Your team is in a good position to answer, because each of you sees him in a different setting.

Suggest that the team compare notes using specific behaviors (“started independent work without a reminder in 2 of 10 class periods”) instead of labels. If the pattern holds, bring it to the student support team. Pressure alone is a poor bet. If the problem is skill, he needs a system and practice, and you can start building one now.

ADHD

ADHD (attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder) is one of the most common reasons that a student’s attention and executive function both struggle. It is a neurodevelopmental disorder marked by ongoing patterns of inattention, hyperactivity, and/or impulsivity that interfere with daily life. In a 2022 national survey, parents reported that about 1 in 9 U.S. children ages 3 to 17 had been diagnosed at some point (Danielson et al., 2024). Boys were diagnosed about twice as often as girls. In a class of 25, two or three students with ADHD is typical.

What we know about causes

Researchers still don’t know exactly what causes ADHD or exactly how it works in the brain. No blood test or brain scan can diagnose it. Clinicians identify ADHD by its symptoms: what a person does, how often, and how much it gets in the way.

We do know that ADHD is partly genetic. Twin studies compare how alike identical and non-identical twins are. Across 37 of them, genetic differences accounted for about 74% of the differences between people in ADHD traits, on average (Faraone & Larsson, 2019). That number describes a group, not a person. It doesn’t mean a student’s ADHD is 74% genes, and it doesn’t mean the environment can’t help.

You may hear that ADHD is simply “low dopamine.” Dopamine is involved, and most ADHD medications act on it. But a 2024 review found limited evidence that ADHD is a simple dopamine shortage, and called that idea one of the most common misconceptions about ADHD (MacDonald et al., 2024). Be wary of any classroom strategy sold as a way to “boost dopamine.”

We also know that conditions matter. Poor sleep, stress, and chaotic routines make attention and self-control harder for anyone, and especially for students with ADHD. Medication, behavioral supports, and predictable structure tend to help. You can’t change a student’s genes, but you can change a lot about the conditions around them.

Three features, three presentations

ADHD has three core features:

  • Inattention is trouble steering attention. A student with ADHD may lock onto an interesting task for hours and still drift away from a routine one within minutes.
  • Hyperactivity often eases with age. In teens and adults, it may show up as inner restlessness instead of running around the room.
  • Impulsivity is acting before thinking it through, a brake that catches too late. Of the three, it is the one most often mistaken for deliberate misbehavior.

Clinicians describe three presentations, depending on which features stand out.

Predominantly Inattentive
Trouble sustaining focus, following through, and staying organized, without much hyperactivity. It often looks like a quiet daydreamer and is often missed, especially in girls. This is what people used to call ADD.
Predominantly Hyperactive-Impulsive
Lots of movement and acting on impulse, with fewer obvious attention problems. Most often seen in young children.
Combined Presentation
Significant symptoms of both. Usually needs support across many settings and skills.

A student’s presentation can change over time. A child diagnosed as hyperactive-impulsive at age 7 may meet the criteria for the combined presentation in middle school, when schoolwork asks more of attention. ADHD also tends to last. One long-term study followed children with ADHD into their mid-twenties. Only about 9% fully recovered and stayed that way, and most had symptoms that came and went over the years (Sibley et al., 2022).

ADHD often looks different in girls. In childhood, girls meet the criteria at a little under half the rate of boys, but the gap narrows to nearly even by adulthood (Hinshaw et al., 2022). Girls with ADHD more often show inattention, along with anxiety or low mood, and many work hard to hide their struggles. A quiet girl who daydreams, loses track of assignments, and worries in silence doesn’t stand out the way a boy out of his seat does, so she is more likely to be overlooked or diagnosed years later. Teachers are often the first adults in a position to notice.

What ADHD is and isn’t

Because ADHD is so common, a lot of what people “know” about it is wrong. Some of these ideas are close cousins of neuromyths, which you met in Chapter 4: a real finding stretched past what the evidence shows.

What people sayWhat the evidence says
“He can focus on video games for hours, so it isn’t ADHD.”This fits ADHD. Games give fast, frequent feedback and rewards toward a goal the player chose. The struggle shows up on slow, routine work.
“It’s just laziness.”ADHD is a real condition, and it is partly genetic. Effort matters, but effort alone doesn’t make the symptoms go away.
“Sugar causes it.”A review of controlled studies found that sugar didn’t affect children’s behavior or thinking (Wolraich et al., 1995).
“Bad parenting causes it.”Parenting doesn’t cause ADHD, though a calm, structured home can make it easier to manage.
“Screens cause it.”Not established. In one study of about 2,600 teens, heavier digital media use had a modest link with new ADHD symptoms two years later, but the study couldn’t show that screens were the cause (Ra et al., 2018).
“Kids outgrow it.”Hyperactivity often eases, but for most people symptoms continue in some form into adulthood.
“ADHD is mostly a boy thing.”Boys are diagnosed more often as children, but girls are more often missed. Their symptoms tend to be quieter, and the gap nearly closes by adulthood.
“Everyone’s a little ADHD.”Everyone loses focus sometimes. A diagnosis requires symptoms that last, show up across settings, and interfere with life.
“ADD is a different condition.”ADD is an outdated name. Today it’s ADHD, predominantly inattentive presentation.
“ADHD means you can’t pay attention.”The attention is there. It’s hard to steer, and hard to sustain on low-interest tasks.

Diagnosis and the teacher’s role

Identifying ADHD can’t be done from one behavior, one setting, or a quick checklist. Clinical guidelines require the same things as the three pattern questions earlier in this concept: symptoms that last, show up in more than one setting, and interfere with daily life. Three terms come up often:

  • A screener is a short checklist or rating scale that flags patterns worth a closer look. It is a starting point, not an answer.
  • A diagnostic tool is a formal assessment used to decide whether a person meets the criteria for a specific diagnosis.
  • A diagnosis is a decision made by a qualified professional using several kinds of evidence gathered over time: observations, interviews, developmental history, and standard criteria.

Students and families now learn about ADHD from social media, online quizzes, and friends. That can raise awareness, but many signs of ADHD overlap with ordinary stress, boredom, tiredness, and anxiety, and an online quiz can’t rule those out. A label can also change how a student sees the future. People who come to see a struggle as fixed in their brain or genes tend to expect less change and feel less in control (Lebowitz et al., 2013). If someone thinks they may have ADHD, the right next step is an evaluation by a qualified professional.

For school-age children, the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends FDA-approved medication together with behavioral treatment, preferably both parent training and classroom behavioral interventions (Wolraich et al., 2019). For preschoolers, behavioral treatment comes first. The evidence for medication is strong, and so is the evidence for the behavioral tools in Concept 5, which is why the guideline pairs them. Decisions about treatment belong to families and their doctors. Teachers help most by running strong classroom systems and sharing clear observations.

The professional line►

Teachers play an important role in this process, but a limited one, and the limits are firm.

What you never do:

  • Tell a family, or hint, that their child has ADHD or any other condition.
  • Recommend medication.
  • Discourage medication. “I don’t think he needs pills” crosses the line too.
  • Make medication a condition for anything.

What you do:

  • Describe what you’ve seen in plain, specific terms.
  • Share your observations with the family and the school’s student support team.
  • Point families to the school team or their child’s doctor for anything about diagnosis or treatment.

If a parent asks you directly, you can say, “That’s a question for his doctor. What I can share is what I see in class.”

Why the line is firm: Most ADHD medications are controlled substances. Federal special education law requires every state to forbid school staff from requiring a child to get a prescription for one. That holds for attending school, being evaluated, and receiving services (Individuals with Disabilities Education Act, 2004). Some states go further. Texas, for example, bars school employees from recommending a psychotropic drug or suggesting any particular diagnosis (Tex. Educ. Code § 38.016). Rules vary by state and district, so this book teaches the strictest version. When you’re hired, learn your own state’s and district’s rules too.

Clear observations are your best contribution. Compare “He shows ADHD symptoms” with “He started independent work without a reminder in 2 of the last 10 class periods, and he lost track of multi-step directions 4 times this week.” The second gives the evaluation team something to work with, and it keeps you on your side of the line.

Retrieval practiceMaya is a quiet sixth grader who never causes trouble. She often loses track of multi-step assignments, stares out the window during independent work, and seems anxious about grades. Her last two report cards say she “needs to apply herself.” Using what you know about ADHD, what might be going on? What should her teacher do, and not do?►

What might be going on: Maya’s pattern fits how inattentive ADHD often looks in girls: quiet, disorganized, and anxious, easy to overlook because she isn’t disruptive. It could also be anxiety, poor sleep, or something else entirely. The teacher can’t tell, and doesn’t need to. Notice, too, that “needs to apply herself” is a verdict about will. The pattern points at least as much to skill.

What to do: Start with supports that help any student, like written steps, a quick check-in at the start of independent work, and an organization routine. Keep specific notes on what you see. If the pattern lasts and shows up in other classes, share your observations with her family and the student support team.

What not to do: Don’t suggest to Maya or her family that she has ADHD. Describe what you see and let the professionals do the diagnosing.

Retrieval practiceA parent tells you her daughter “definitely has ADHD” because of an online quiz. She asks you to note it in your records, and she wants to know whether you think medication would help. What do you say and do? What do you avoid?►

What to say and do: Thank her for raising the concern, and take it seriously. Share your own observations in specific terms (“I’ve noticed she often stops after the first step of multi-part directions”). Explain that a formal evaluation is the way to get a real answer, and offer to connect her with the school’s student support team. You can also suggest she talk with her daughter’s doctor. On medication: “That’s a question for her doctor. What I can share is what I see in class.”

What to avoid: confirming or doubting the quiz, writing “ADHD” in your records when no professional has diagnosed it, and giving any opinion on medication, for or against.

Why: You’re protecting the family’s chance to get an accurate answer, and you’re staying inside the law and your professional role. Your observations are valuable because they describe what you saw instead of what you think it means.

Major concept 5 · Tier 1 supports help all students

Building and Supporting Executive Function

Schools usually organize support in three tiers. Tier 1 is what every student in a classroom gets. Tier 2 adds small-group help, and Tier 3 adds intensive, individual help. This concept is about Tier 1: what a classroom teacher can build into the room for everyone, including students with ADHD.

Research on these supports is thinner than it should be, and a few well-known findings haven’t held up when other teams tested them again. The table shows where the common supports stand right now. Expect some ratings to change as better studies come in.

How to use the strongest tools

Strong evidenceBehavioral classroom management►
Clear rules, specific praise, and point or token systems that reward the behaviors you want. Reviews of treatment research rate behavioral classroom management a well-established treatment for ADHD, which means it has worked in multiple studies by independent research teams (Evans et al., 2018). Game-style point systems belong here, because what makes them work is frequent feedback and reward. So does the Good Behavior Game from Chapter 19, in which teams earn small privileges by keeping disruptive behavior low. It has a long record of success, though one large replication in English schools found no effect (Ashworth et al., 2020).
Example: The class earns points for starting work within two minutes and for turning in finished work. Points add up to a privilege the class picks each Friday.
Strong evidenceThe Daily Report Card►
A Daily Report Card is a behavior system built for one student. You saw it in Chapter 19, and it does executive function work too. The teacher picks 2 to 5 specific target behaviors, rates them during the day, and sends the card home, where parents follow up with praise or a small reward. Like the book reward in Fryer’s Dallas trial, each target is a step the student knows how to take, with feedback the same day. In a randomized trial with elementary students with ADHD in special education, the card improved classroom behavior, progress on IEP goals, and how much work students got done (Fabiano et al., 2010). It did not raise achievement test scores. That’s a realistic picture: the card changes daily behavior, and better grades may take longer.
Example (a 4th grader’s card for the math block):
Target✓ or ✗
Started work within 2 minutes 
Had materials ready 
Turned in the assignment 
Goal: 7 of 9 checks across three class periods earns 15 minutes of a chosen activity at home.
Strong evidenceOrganization training►
Organization training teaches a system directly and practices it until it sticks: a homework checklist, a binder with a set place for everything, a planner routine at the end of each class. It is also rated a well-established treatment for ADHD (Evans et al., 2018). Students aren’t told to “be more organized.” They’re shown exactly what organized looks like, then checked and praised until the habit holds.
Example: At the end of every class, a 7th grade science teacher runs the same 90-second check: planner open, assignment written, papers in the right folder. In September she checks every binder. By October, students check a partner’s. By December, they check their own.
Strong evidence (mostly small studies)Self-monitoring►
Self-monitoring teaches students to track their own behavior. A quiet signal goes off every few minutes, and the student marks a card: Was I working? In a review of self-regulation strategies for students with ADHD, self-monitoring produced large gains in on-task behavior and in how much work students finished and got right (Reid et al., 2005). Most of these studies were small, so we can be surer that it helps than about how much. Self-monitoring is where the hand-off happens. A Daily Report Card puts the teacher in charge of tracking. Self-monitoring moves that job to the student.
Example: A 9th grader with a 504 plan wears a watch set to vibrate every 6 minutes during independent work. Each time it buzzes, he marks a plus or minus on a sticky note. At the end of class, he and his teacher compare notes for 30 seconds.
Good evidenceRoutines, written directions, and small steps►

These supports take load off working memory, so students don’t have to hold the plan in their heads. Chapter 21 explains why that matters so much. Post the daily schedule in the same place every day. Give directions in writing as well as out loud. Break big assignments into small, labeled steps, and hand out one step at a time when a student is stuck at the start. Teach transitions as a routine and practice them, the way you’d practice a fire drill. The Church’s guidance for Primary teachers makes a similar point about children with disabilities: a posted class schedule “can help reduce feelings of uncertainty, which may increase anxiety in some children” (The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, 2023).

Academic supports such as peer tutoring and direct instruction have shown some of the largest effects on schoolwork for students with ADHD (DuPaul et al., 2012).

Some teachers worry that supports like these lower the bar. Ask what the lesson is for. If the goal is persuasive writing, remembering six spoken directions isn’t part of the rigor. Removing that load lets the student spend effort where you want it.

Example: Instead of “Write your essay,” a 10th grade teacher hands out step one only: “Write three possible claims on this sticky note. You have six minutes.” Step two comes after.
Good evidence, smaller than claimedMovement and exercise►
Physical activity can help executive function in students with ADHD. Exercise programs have shown benefits for attention and self-control, but when researchers set aside the weaker studies, the effect shrinks to small-to-moderate (Chen et al., 2026). Movement breaks are cheap and good for everyone. Just don’t expect them to do the work of the strategies above.
Example: A “movement menu” by the door lists quick options (wall push-ups, a lap to the drinking fountain, stretches) that students can use with a hand signal.
Key aphorism

Lend them your systems until they can run their own.

Supports should make the work possible now and fade as the skill grows.

The strongest tools share a pattern. They make the task possible now, then fade as the student’s skill grows. A Daily Report Card starts with the teacher keeping track. Organization training starts with the teacher checking every binder. Self-monitoring hands the checking to the student. If supports never come down, a student never learns to carry the load. This is antifragility from Chapter 1: students grow stronger through challenge pitched at the right level, with support that steps back over time.

Popular, but not yet tested

The untested ideas in the table may still work. Most are cheap and low-risk, so it’s reasonable to try one with a student who needs help. Do it the way a scholar-practitioner would: pick one strategy, track the student’s on-task behavior or finished work for a couple of weeks, and keep it only if the numbers improve.

Tested, and didn’t help

Social skills groups show why it matters to match the support to the problem. Students with ADHD usually know the social rules. Their trouble is using those rules in the moment, which is an executive function problem (Aduen et al., 2018). Teaching the rules again doesn’t fix that. Social skills programs can help when knowledge really is what’s missing. The PEERS program, for example, has good evidence from randomized trials with autistic teens (Laugeson et al., 2012).

Brain-training games deserve a closer look because they sound so scientific. They promise to strengthen working memory the way a gym strengthens a muscle. It’s a neuromyth of the kind you met in Chapter 4: a real finding (practice improves the practiced task) stretched into a claim the evidence doesn’t support (practice on one task improves everything). Compare it with organization training. Drilling a skill on its own doesn’t carry over to schoolwork. Teaching a real system, in the setting where students need it, does.

Retrieval practiceDuring independent work, a 5th grader with ADHD sits still and produces nothing for the first ten minutes, every day. Using the evidence in this concept, describe a plan that starts with the strongest strategies. Then name one untested strategy you might add, and explain how you’d find out whether it helps.►

Start with the stronger evidence.

  • Shrink the start (good evidence). The stall is likely a task initiation problem. Hand out only the first step, make it small and concrete, and set a short time limit: “Answer number one. You have four minutes.”
  • Reward the start (strong evidence). Add “started within two minutes” as a target on a Daily Report Card or a class point system. Now starting is a step he knows how to take, and it earns something.
  • Plan the hand-off (strong evidence). Once starting improves, teach him to self-monitor. He makes a check mark when he begins, and you compare it with yours at the end of class. Over the weeks, your check fades and his stays.

Then, if you like, test something untested. You might sit beside him for the first two minutes (body doubling). Track how long it takes him to start for two weeks with it and two weeks without, and keep it only if the data show a difference.

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

Two jobs run through this chapter: help what students learn stick, and help students run the systems that learning depends on. These practices do both.

  • Practice past the first right answer. Keep practicing until skills are fluent, so they stop using up working memory.
  • Clear their path, but make them climb. Keep the effort of recalling, telling problems apart, and coming back after a delay.
  • Build retrieval into every week. Low-stakes quizzes, brain dumps, and teaching a spot in the room all work.
  • Space and mix your practice. Return to older material in warm-ups, and mix problem types students tend to confuse.
  • Teach students to check instead of trusting the feeling. Model the questions out loud, then have students test themselves before deciding they’re ready.
  • Get curious before you conclude. When a student isn’t doing it, look for a missing skill first. A skill problem needs teaching and support, and pressure on a skill problem usually backfires.
  • Start with the strongest evidence: clear rules, specific praise, and point systems for the class, and a Daily Report Card for individual students.
  • Lend your systems, then fade them. Teach checklists, binders, and planners, and check them until they stick. Self-monitoring moves the tracking from you to the student.
  • Test before you trust. Try popular but untested tips with one student, track the results, and keep what works. Skip what’s been tested and failed, like fidget spinners and brain-training games.
  • Observe, describe, and refer. Never suggest a diagnosis, and never recommend or discourage medication.
Chapter synthesis
Working memory is small, and long-term memory is the goal. Good teaching clears away the effort that doesn’t help, keeps the effort that does, and teaches students to tell the difference. When the skills that run learning are still growing, teachers lend students their systems until students can run their own.
Working memory holds about four new things at once. Chunks, schemas, and fluent skills let students handle much more, because long-term memory does part of the work.
Some difficulty is desirable. Memory is the residue of thought. Retrieval, spacing, and interleaving feel harder than rereading and cramming, and they produce stronger, more usable memories.
Students can’t trust the feeling of familiarity. Metacognition teaches them to check what they know with retrieval, until checking becomes a habit.
Will and skill are different problems. Look for a missing skill first. When executive function struggles in a way that lasts, shows up across settings, and gets in the way, that pattern is executive dysfunction.
ADHD is a partly genetic condition that makes attention hard to steer. It is identified by symptoms, it often looks quieter in girls, and the right conditions can make symptoms better or worse.
The best-supported classroom tools are behavioral and organizational. Rules, praise, points, Daily Report Cards, organization training, and self-monitoring have the strongest evidence.
Teachers observe, describe, and refer. They never suggest a diagnosis, and they never recommend or discourage medication.

On the first day, a teacher often can’t tell whether a struggle is about will, skill, or both. You can still act. Watch for the pattern, start with the supports that have the strongest evidence, and keep handing the system back to the student until they can run it on their own.

An expert solving a Rubik’s Cube uses every step from these two chapters. Perception and attention take in the scramble. Working memory holds the next move. Patterns encoded through years of practice sit in long-term memory, retrieval finds the right one in a fraction of a second, and the expert applies it to a scramble they’ve never seen before. Executive function keeps the goal in mind the whole time. Every one of those experts started as a beginner who turned one piece at a time and forgot moves they thought they knew. Your students are on the same road. Clear their path, make them climb, and lend them your systems until they can run their own.

Key aphorisms
  1. The brain is not designed for thinking. It’s designed to save you from having to think.
  2. Kids do well if they can.
  3. Lend them your systems until they can run their own.
