How the Brain Learns, Part I

Clear Their Path, but Make Them Climb

Chapter 21~ 30 min read

In the late 2000s, the economist Roland Fryer set out to answer a blunt question: if you pay students to do well in school, will they? He ran experiments in more than 200 city schools. In New York, students earned money for scores on regular tests. In Chicago, ninth graders earned money for grades. In Dallas, second graders earned $2 for every book they read, as long as they passed a short quiz on it (Fryer, 2011).

Paying for scores and grades didn’t raise state test scores. Paying for books was the one that showed gains, though only for some groups of students. Then Fryer’s team asked New York students how they planned to earn more money. The students were excited about the rewards, but the question stumped them. They talked about test-taking tricks, like reading the questions more carefully. None of them mentioned studying, doing homework, or asking for help.

Fryer’s explanation was that the students wanted the reward but didn’t know how to get it. “Raise your test score” is a goal with no instructions attached. “Read this book and pass the quiz” is a step a seven-year-old knows how to take, and the quiz tells her right away whether she did it. The takeaway is simple. When students did the inputs, like reading books, the outputs started to improve. But some students needed to be rewarded for the inputs before they did them.

The book reward did something powerful: it broke a big, vague goal into small, clear steps. Students had less to plan and less to hold in mind at once. This chapter comes back to that idea again and again.

Wanting something is not the same as being able to plan it, start it, and stick with it. The mental skills that turn a goal into steps are called executive function, and they are the subject of this chapter.

The big picture

Learning happens in steps. Information comes in through perception, gets past the filter of attention, gets worked on in working memory, and is encoded into long-term memory. Over the whole process sits a manager, executive function, which keeps the plan in mind and decides where attention goes. This chapter covers the manager and the first four steps. Chapter 22 covers the rest.

A few lessons run through the chapter. Executive function is a set of skills, and like other skills it grows slowly and can be taught. Attention is limited, so anything in the room that doesn’t serve the lesson competes with it. Working memory holds only about four new things at a time, so every slide, direction, and side story spends some of that space. And students remember what they actually think about. Together, these ideas point to one job for teachers: clear away the effort that doesn’t help learning, so students can spend their effort on what does.

Major concept 1 · The skills that turn goals into steps

Executive Function

Before we get to how the brain learns, we need to introduce you to the CEO managing the process. It keeps the plan in mind, holds back the impulse, and shifts gears when things change. That manager is executive function.

In the early 1970s, researchers in Dunedin, New Zealand, began following every child born in the city over a single year, about 1,000 children in all. Through early and middle childhood, parents, teachers, trained observers, and the children themselves reported how well each child could wait, stick with a task, and keep impulses in check. Then the researchers kept following them until they were 32.

Children with more self-control grew into adults with better physical health, fewer drug and alcohol problems, steadier finances, and fewer criminal convictions. Among the fifth of children with the most self-control, about 11% had serious health problems as adults. Among the fifth with the least, 27% did. The pattern held after the researchers accounted for IQ and family social class, and it wasn’t limited to the children who struggled most. Each step up in self-control went with better outcomes (Moffitt et al., 2011).

The finding teachers should remember most is the hopeful one. Children whose self-control improved over the years did better as adults than their early scores predicted. Self-control can grow, and school is one of the places it grows.

Self-control is one part of a larger set of skills called executive function: the skills that let a person plan, focus, hold back an impulse, and adjust when things change. Executive function relies heavily on the prefrontal cortex and its connections to the rest of the brain. Researchers usually describe three core skills (Diamond, 2013).

SkillWhat it doesWhen it struggles, you might see
Working memoryHolds information in mind while you use itA 3rd grader who finishes step one of the directions and has no idea what came next. A 10th grader who loses the start of a long question before reaching the end.
Inhibitory controlStops an automatic response so a better one can happenBlurting out answers. Grabbing a classmate’s pencil. Quitting a problem the moment it gets hard.
Cognitive flexibilitySwitches between tasks, rules, or ideasA meltdown when an assembly changes the schedule. Using last week’s math rule on this week’s problems.

The three skills work together. A student writing an essay has to keep the prompt in mind, tune out the conversation at the next table, and move back and forth between planning, drafting, and fixing. Starting work on your own, called task initiation, draws on all three. That’s one reason so many students get stuck right at the beginning.

It develops slowly

Executive function starts growing in the preschool years and keeps developing through adolescence and into the twenties. You may have heard that the brain is “fully developed at 25.” Treat that as a rough guide. Brain imaging shows the prefrontal cortex maturing well into the twenties, and a 2025 study of brain scans from nearly 3,800 people found that the brain’s wiring keeps reorganizing in an adolescent-like pattern until about age 32, on average (Mousley et al., 2025). People also differ. Some settle earlier and some later.

StageWhat typically develops
Early childhood (3–5)Basic impulse control begins to appear. Children can follow simple two-step directions. Pretend play builds early flexibility.
Middle childhood (6–12)Flexibility improves, and children can stick with a goal longer. Planning and organization begin to form but still need adult support.
Adolescence (13–18)Planning and self-control grow, but risk-taking and impulsivity stay high, especially around peers.
Early adulthood (18 to late 20s)Decision-making and emotional control keep improving. Development tapers off gradually instead of stopping at one age.

This should shape what you expect. A sixth grader who loses track of a two-week project may not be choosing to fail. Without help, that may be about as much as a sixth-grade brain can manage.

Retrieval practiceA fourth grader has three habits. She starts the directions but forgets the later steps. She shouts answers before being called on. Major concept 2 · Only what gets noticed can be learned

Perception and Attention

The brain senses far more than it can use, and attention lets only a small slice through. Right now your eyes are on this page, but your brain is also picking up the sounds around you, the feel of your chair, and maybe a text you haven’t answered. It can’t process all of it, so it picks. Executive function helps decide what gets picked. Keeping your attention on a lesson while ignoring the hallway calls on inhibitory control, the same skill that helps a student wait before blurting out an answer (Diamond, 2013). But attention can also be grabbed without permission, by a slammed door or by hearing your own name.

Selective attention

Selective attention is the brain’s process of choosing what to focus on while filtering out everything else. It is the first gate in learning. The brain doesn’t take in the world like an open window. It lets a narrow slice through and drops most of the rest. A lesson that doesn’t get through that gate can’t be learned, however good the lesson is.

Classic finding: the cocktail party effect►

Picture a loud party. You’re deep in conversation and not listening to anyone else. Then someone across the room says your name, and you turn around. In the 1950s, Colin Cherry played two messages at once, one in each ear, and asked people to repeat only one of them. They could report almost nothing about the other message. Neville Moray (1959) then slipped people’s names into the ignored message. About a third of them noticed their name, and later studies found about the same rate (Wood & Cowan, 1995; Röer & Cowan, 2021).

Both halves of that result matter. The brain keeps some watch on what it’s ignoring, which is why a buzzing phone or a whisper can pull a student out of a lesson. But most people didn’t notice their own name at all. Attention filters out far more than we think it does.

So a student can look right at you and not take in a word you said. Anyone who has called on a student in the middle of a daydream has seen it happen. Everything in the room competes for the attention the lesson needs, and teachers can help in two ways. They can change the room, by putting phones away and cutting visual clutter. And they can teach students to notice when their attention drifts and bring it back, a skill called metacognition.

Phones and attention: what we know►

Phones are one of the most studied distractions in schools, and the research is still coming in. Here is where it stands:

  • School-wide bans may help a little. A Harvard team studied schools that locked phones in pouches for the whole day. Afterward, test scores grew slightly faster, about one to two extra weeks of learning a year, and the gains lasted up to three years (Burland et al., 2026).
  • Not every study agrees. A national study of the same kind of policy found average effects on test scores near zero, with small gains in high schools and small losses in middle schools (Allcott et al., 2026).
  • A phone nearby may cost some attention. One widely shared study found that a face-down, silent phone on the desk lowered scores on a working memory task (Ward et al., 2017). A later meta-analysis found a smaller effect (Parry, 2024), and other studies, including a careful replication, have found mixed results (Ruiz Pardo & Minda, 2022).

The likely benefit of a ban is small, and research is ongoing. A strict phone policy is still a reasonable choice. Frame it as protecting attention rather than as punishment, be honest about what it can and can’t promise, and teach students healthy habits for the hours they do have their phones.

Perceptual load

Psychologist Nilli Lavie asked a related question: once a task has taken its share of attention, how much is left over for distraction? Her answer is called perceptual load. The brain has only so much attention to spend. A demanding task uses most of it and leaves little for distractions. An easy task leaves plenty, and the leftover attention goes looking for something to do (Lavie, 2005). Lavie worked with lab tasks, but the pattern will be familiar to any teacher. A student working on a hard, interesting problem barely notices the noise in the hallway, and the same student drifts during a dull worksheet.

A famous experiment shows how completely a demanding task can fill attention. Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris (1999) asked people to watch a video of players passing basketballs and to count the passes.

Partway through, a person in a gorilla suit walked into the scene, thumped their chest, and walked off. About half of the viewers never saw the gorilla. They were paying close attention, just to the passes.

For teachers, perceptual load is mostly about the level of challenge. Work that’s too easy doesn’t free up attention for learning. It frees it up for distraction. Work that’s too hard overwhelms students. The target is work that is demanding enough to hold their attention and still within reach. Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (1990) found something similar in his research on flow, the state of deep focus when time seems to disappear. Flow tends to happen when the challenge of a task matches a person’s skill.

Classroom decorations: the Goldilocks problem►

The research on classroom walls points to a middle ground: not too much, not too little.

The HEAD Project followed 3,766 students in 153 classrooms in the United Kingdom over a school year (Barrett et al., 2015). Students made less progress in rooms with very high visual complexity, but bare rooms didn’t do best either. The best results came from a middle level of display: organized, purposeful, and tied to current learning. Because the study observed classrooms instead of changing them, it shows a link, not proof of cause.

A Carnegie Mellon experiment tested the question directly (Fisher et al., 2014). Kindergartners took science lessons in a lab classroom, sometimes with heavily decorated walls and sometimes with bare ones. In the decorated room, they spent more time off task and learned less, answering 42% of test questions correctly compared with 55% in the bare room. A follow-up study found that children didn’t get used to the decorations, even after two weeks (Godwin et al., 2022).

This runs against a lot of school culture. Many schools treat a room covered in color as a sign of a caring, skilled teacher, and new teachers often feel pressure to fill every wall. The research suggests that many classrooms would work better with a little less on them. Keep what students are using now, like this unit’s anchor charts, and rotate or retire the rest.

Major concept 3 · Spend working memory on what matters

Working Memory and Cognitive Load

Once attention lets something in, memory takes over. Information moves through three stores, and each works differently.

Memory storeHow much it holdsHow long it lastsWhat it means for teaching
Sensory memoryA lot, unfilteredLess than a secondMost of it never reaches attention. Students notice what’s new or stands out.
Working memoryAbout 4 new itemsA few seconds unless rehearsedThis is the bottleneck. Every part of a lesson either uses this space well or wastes it.
Long-term memoryNo known limitCan last a lifetimeThis is the goal. Information gets here through thinking, connecting, and retrieving.

How small is working memory?

Miller’s paper became one of the most cited in psychology, and “seven, plus or minus two” became one of the few research findings most people can quote. The title helped. So did everyday life. A local phone number was seven digits, and seven felt about right. Miller himself was less sure of his number. He closed the paper by wondering whether all the sevens he kept running into meant anything, and suspected they were “only a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence” (Miller, 1956).

Later research lowered the estimate. Miller’s seven came from studies where people could group items and repeat them under their breath. When researchers block those tricks, the limit is closer to four, and items fade within seconds unless they’re rehearsed (Cowan, 2001). Four is the number to plan around, and it’s smaller than most teachers assume.

Cognitive load theory

If students can hold only about four new things at once, then every lesson is a decision about how to spend that space. Cognitive load theory is the idea that working memory can only handle so much at once, so instruction should spend that space on what students need to learn. Psychologist John Sweller developed it in the late 1980s, and it has become one of the most useful frameworks in educational psychology (Sweller et al., 2019).

The theory separates two kinds of load. Intrinsic load comes from the content itself. It depends on how many pieces students have to hold together at once, and on how much they already know. A cell diagram is simple for a biology teacher and overwhelming for a freshman. Teachers can’t remove intrinsic load without changing what students learn, but they can manage it. Extraneous load comes from how the lesson is designed, and it is the teacher’s to cut.

Intrinsic load: manage itExtraneous load: cut it
Balancing a chemical equation for the first timeDirections given once, out loud, while students are still finding a pencil
Reading a sonnet written in 1600s EnglishA diagram on one slide and its labels on the next
Solving a two-step equation with negative numbersA worksheet that changes its format halfway through
Comparing three causes of the Civil War at onceA slide full of animations, sound effects, and decorative pictures

Many of the executive function supports in Chapter 22 work the same way. A posted schedule, written directions, and a big task handed out one step at a time all take the job of holding the plan off the student. Each one cuts extraneous load and leaves more working memory for the content.

Putting memory to work: implications of cognitive load theory

Cognitive load theory has practical implications for nearly every lesson. Here are five:

  1. Ruthlessly cut extraneous load
  2. Practice beyond mastery
  3. Build an intuition for how much to teach at once
  4. Adjust based on mastery
  5. Put everything they need in one place

Principle 1: Ruthlessly cut extraneous load

Look at every slide, handout, and activity and ask whether each part helps students learn what you want them to learn. Decorative pictures, animated transitions, background music, and “fun fact” tangents all cost working memory. So do directions that live in three places at once. If something doesn’t earn its place, take it out.

Example: A teacher trims a 22-slide lesson to 12 slides by cutting clip art, splitting crowded slides, and moving the directions onto the same page as the task.

Watch for seductive details. The hardest extraneous load to cut is the kind that’s fun. A funny video, a surprising story, or a vivid example that’s off the point can pull attention away from the content that needed it. Researchers call these seductive details: interesting material that isn’t about what students are supposed to learn. Across many studies, adding them lowered learning (Sundararajan & Adesope, 2020). Students remember the funny video. They just don’t remember the water cycle that came after it.

That points to a common mistake, the engagement trap: mistaking busy, happy students for students who are learning. Students can be busy, excited, and enjoying themselves while remembering almost nothing. Enjoyment matters, and a lesson students like is easier to teach. But the better test of a lesson is what students will remember in three weeks.

Choice can be extraneous load too►

Psychologist Barry Schwartz made the “paradox of choice” famous: the idea that too many options cause stress and wasted mental energy. The evidence is weaker than the idea’s fame suggests. Across 50 experiments, more options made little difference on average (Scheibehenne et al., 2010). But choice overload does show up when people know little about what they’re choosing, which describes students learning something new. Wide-open choices, like any topic, any format, or any approach, can use up working memory on deciding. Two or three meaningful options respect students’ independence while keeping the load manageable.

Principle 2: Practice beyond mastery

Getting the right answer once is not the finish line. Keep practicing until the skill is fluent, so it runs from long-term memory and leaves working memory free for whatever comes next. Daniel Willingham puts it this way: “Practice until you are perfect and you will be perfect only briefly” (Willingham, 2004). Extra practice works best when it’s spread out and mixed in with other material, which Chapter 22 explains.

Example: Students who can solve one-step equations keep a few in every warm-up for the rest of the unit, so solving them is automatic by the time two-step equations arrive.

Principle 3: Build an intuition for how much to teach at once

Teachers are experts in their subject, and experts forget how hard it was to learn. That’s the curse of knowledge from Chapter 2. A topic that feels like one idea to you may be six new pieces for a student. Great teachers plan around this. They count the new terms and steps in a lesson, teach parts before wholes, build background knowledge first, and check for understanding before they add the next piece. Over time, that planning becomes an intuition for how much a class can take in at once.

Example: Before teaching photosynthesis, a teacher lists every new term the lesson needs. There are eleven. She moves four of them to the day before and teaches them with pictures.

A gospel lens: line upon line►

The Lord describes his own way of teaching in 2 Nephi 28:30: “I will give unto the children of men line upon line, precept upon precept.” The verse goes on to say that those who listen to his counsel learn wisdom and receive more.

The verse is about how the Lord gives knowledge to his children, not about working memory. Cognitive load theory reaches a similar practical conclusion by a different road. Students can’t take in everything at once, so a wise teacher sequences instruction, building each new piece on one that’s already secure. A teacher who follows this pattern isn’t lowering expectations. The goal is still the whole truth, and the pace is set so students can actually receive it.

Principle 4: Adjust based on mastery

One of the best-tested ways to manage intrinsic load is the worked example: a problem with every step already solved. A beginner who solves problems from scratch spends most of their working memory searching for what to do next. Studying a worked solution spends that working memory on how the solution works. Sweller and Cooper first showed this with algebra students in 1985, and a 2023 meta-analysis of 55 randomized studies found that worked examples moderately improved math performance, from elementary school through college (Barbieri et al., 2023).

The benefit fades as students gain skill. Once students know how to solve a type of problem, more worked examples add little and can even get in the way. Researchers call this the expertise reversal effect (Kalyuga et al., 2003). So the right support depends on where students are now, and it should change as they improve.

Example: Start a new skill with full worked examples, then fade them. Leave the last step blank, then the last two, until students solve whole problems on their own.

Principle 5: Put everything they need in one place

When students have to piece together information from two places, like a diagram and a separate legend, or a handout and the board, the effort of holding one while reading the other is pure extraneous load. Researchers call this split attention, and putting the pieces together removes it (Chandler & Sweller, 1991). Put labels on the diagram itself. Put directions on the same page as the task. Keep the example problem visible while students work the next one.

Example: Instead of a map with a numbered key in the corner, a geography teacher writes each label directly on the map.

Key aphorism

Clear their path, but make them climb.

Cut the effort that doesn’t build learning. Keep the effort that does.

Cognitive load theory clears the path. It doesn’t say learning should feel easy. Once the wasted effort is gone, students still have to do the hard work of thinking about the content, and the next concept is about that climb.

Retrieval practiceA first-year teacher on your team asks for feedback. To make her lesson on the water cycle more engaging, she plans to open with a funny viral video, add animated transitions and sound effects to every slide, and end with a free-choice project (“Show what you learned any way you want!”). Using cognitive load theory, what feedback would you give her? Keep in mind that she’s new and you want her to keep trying new ideas.►

Start with what’s right: she wants students engaged, and that matters. Then look at each addition by what students will spend working memory on.

  • The viral video: If it isn’t about the water cycle, it’s a seductive detail. Students will remember the video, not the lesson. Suggest a short, striking clip that is about the content, like a time-lapse of clouds forming.
  • The animations and sound effects: These are extraneous load. Each one asks students to process motion and sound instead of the idea. Suggest building slides one step at a time only when the order of the steps matters, like evaporation, then condensation, then precipitation.
  • The free-choice project: Unlimited choice can overload students who are new to the topic. Suggest two or three structured options, like a labeled diagram, a short explanation, or a model with a written key.

Frame it as a shared question instead of a correction: “What do we want students thinking about in each part of this lesson?” Then offer to compare results. You could each give the same quick quiz a week later and look at the results together.

Major concept 4 · New ideas stick to what students already know

Encoding

Memory isn’t only a set of places to hold information. It’s also a set of processes:

  • Encoding turns what we notice into a form the mind can keep.
  • Storage keeps it over time.
  • Retrieval brings it back when we need it.

A breakdown at any step means forgetting, and instruction can help or hurt at each one. This concept is about the first step. Chapter 22 takes up the other two.

Encoding works a bit like saving photos to an online album. The photos you label, tag, and file in albums with related pictures are the ones you can find later. A photo dumped in with ten thousand others is technically still there, but good luck finding it. Memory works much the same way, with one difference. It doesn’t store a copy of the photo. It stores your version of it, shaped by what you were thinking about at the time, and connected to what you already knew.

Those albums work a lot like the schemas you learned about in Chapter 11. A new idea that connects to a schema the student already has is easier to store and easier to find later. A new idea with nothing to connect to is the photo lost among ten thousand others.

Memory is the residue of thought

Hearing information once only exposes students to it. Getting it into long-term memory takes attention, connection to what students already know, time, and repeated retrieval. Cognitive scientist Daniel Willingham, whose book Why Don’t Students Like School? (2009) became popular with teachers, sums up the first part in five words.

Key aphorism

Memory is the residue of thought.

Daniel Willingham, Why Don’t Students Like School? (2009)

What a student remembers depends on what they actually thought about, not on what they were shown. A student who spends class coloring a diagram is thinking about colors. A student asked to explain the diagram from memory is thinking about the content. That gives teachers a simple test for any activity: what will students be thinking about while they do it?

About the Aphorism

Where it comes from: cognitive scientist Daniel Willingham uses this line in his column for teachers, “What Will Improve a Student’s Memory?” (American Educator, Winter 2008–2009), and in Why Don’t Students Like School? (2009). His practical advice follows from it: look at each lesson plan in terms of what students are likely to think about.

What it means here: a lesson leaves behind whatever students’ minds were busy with. If an activity has them thinking about color choices, glue, or a funny video, that’s what sticks.

Where it stops: thinking about something once doesn’t guarantee you’ll remember it next month. Retrieval, spacing, and what students already know also shape what lasts, which is why the rest of this concept covers them.

Explain it to learn it

Explaining an idea forces students to put the pieces in order and notice the gaps, which makes it one of the best ways to get them thinking hard about the content. These activities fit best once the basics are in place. A student who doesn’t know the steps yet has nothing to explain, so start with worked examples.

  • Platypus teaching. Students explain an idea out loud to an object, like a stuffed animal on the desk, or to an imagined classmate. In experiments, students who explained material on camera to an imagined student learned more than students who simply restudied it (Fiorella & Mayer, 2013; Hoogerheide et al., 2014).
  • Teaching a partner. Teaching works even better with a real listener who can ask questions. A meta-analysis of 28 studies found that preparing to teach helped learning, and actually teaching helped more, especially when the teaching was interactive (Kobayashi, 2019).
  • Reciprocal teaching. Annemarie Palincsar and Ann Brown developed this method for reading comprehension. Small groups take turns leading the discussion of a text using four moves: predict, question, clarify, and summarize (Palincsar & Brown, 1984). A review of 16 studies found real but modest gains, largest on tests built to match the lessons and smaller on standardized tests (Rosenshine & Meister, 1994).
Retrieval practiceTwo seventh-grade science teachers introduce the word photosynthesis. One puts the definition on a slide and has students copy it. The other asks students what a plant needs to grow, writes their answers on the board, and then connects the new word to those answers. A week later, which class is more likely to remember the word? Why?►

The second class. Encoding works best when a new idea connects to something students already know. The second teacher hooked the word onto a schema students already had (plants need sunlight, water, and air), and students had to think about what the word meant.

Copying a definition keeps students busy with spelling and handwriting, not meaning. Memory is the residue of thought, so ask what students will be thinking about. In the first class, the answer is mostly letters.

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

The chapter comes down to two questions to ask before any lesson: what will students actually think about, and is there enough working memory space to think about it well?

  • Protect attention. Put away what doesn’t need to be visible right now, from phones to last month’s posters, then watch whether students’ focus improves.
  • Aim for the right level of challenge. Too easy leaves attention free to wander. Too hard overwhelms. Demanding but doable holds attention best.
  • Reward the steps. Tie rewards and feedback to steps a student knows how to take, not only to the final result.
  • Plan for about four. Count the new terms and steps in a lesson, and spread them out if there are too many.
  • Cut extraneous load ruthlessly. Remove decorative images, split directions, and fun but off-topic tangents. Offer two or three meaningful choices instead of unlimited ones.
  • Put everything in one place. Labels on the diagram, directions on the page, the example visible while students practice.
  • Ask what they’ll think about. Before any activity, ask what students’ minds will be busy with, and connect new ideas to something they already know.
Chapter synthesis
Fryer’s students wanted the reward. What many of them lacked was a set of steps they knew how to take. Executive function is the set of skills that turns a goal into those steps, and teachers can build it.
Executive function is the brain’s management system. Working memory, inhibitory control, and cognitive flexibility develop slowly, into the twenties, and they predict a great deal about adult life. They can also grow.
Attention is the first gate. The brain filters out most of what reaches it. Phones, cluttered walls, and work at the wrong level of challenge all compete for the attention a lesson needs.
Working memory holds about four new things at once. Every slide, direction, and side story spends some of that space.
Cognitive load theory is a design job. Manage intrinsic load, cut extraneous load, watch for seductive details, and adjust support as students gain skill.
Students remember what they think about. New ideas stick best when they connect to what students already know.
Key aphorisms
  1. Clear their path, but make them climb.
  2. Memory is the residue of thought.
