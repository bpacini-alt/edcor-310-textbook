How to Get the Most Out of This Book!

A short guide to learning educational psychology instead of merely reading about it

Read this before Chapter 1~ 10 min readPractice-heavy guide

Reading is not the same thing as learning. This book asks you to notice claims about people, weigh evidence, interpret frameworks, and use what you learn with humility.

You are not reading only to collect information for an exam. You are learning how to become a more capable teacher, leader, disciple, and evidence-based practitioner.

The big picture
Use this book as a training ground. Learn the ideas, practice the moves, and then practice deciding which idea or move fits the situation in front of you. The aim is not to turn every human problem into a formula. It is to help you think more clearly, notice more carefully, and act more wisely.
The curious case of the highlighter

How Learning Works

Researchers in 2013 found something surprising: some of the most popular study strategies were also among the least effective. Consider just two: rereading and highlighting.

Rereading works in a similar way. The second or third time through a paragraph feels smoother because the words are familiar. That smoothness can feel like understanding, even when you would struggle to explain the idea without the page in front of you.

The highlighter is a curious case. Open a textbook, find an important sentence, and draw a bright line beneath it. It feels like studying. It looks like studying. When you finish, the page gives you visible evidence that you did something.

There are two reasons, actually: they are ineffective because they require no effort, and they are ineffective because they leave the impression of having learned.

Familiarity is not learning. An idea can feel recognizable without being retrievable, explainable, or usable.
Learning feels like effort. It does not always feel good or natural. Effective learning can feel slower, less smooth, and more frustrating than ineffective studying.

If you love your highlighter, here's a way to make it useful: never leave a highlight by itself. Grab your pen, and explain why you are highlighting.

Listen to the book on your walk to school. That is a great way to encounter the material. But if that’s all you do, the ideas will go in one ear and out the other. What you need is a chance to grapple with what you're learning, and that means retrieval, elaboration, teaching to someone else; in short, it means effort.

This pre-chapter will give you ideas for making the book’s ideas stick—for adding meaning, retrieving what you have read, choosing the right kind of practice, and returning to ideas until they stay in your head.

The rule

Practice is not an optional extra added after learning. Practice is how learning happens.

When studying feels effortful, ask: am I retrieving and thinking, or am I only recognizing something that is in front of me?

A common illusionWhy can highlighting feel productive without producing much durable learning?▸

Highlighting keeps your attention on the page and creates a sense of fluency, but by itself it usually does not make you retrieve, explain, or apply the idea. Turn the highlight into a question or explanation if you want the passage to do more work.

RetrievalWhat is the difference between familiarity and durable learning?▸

Familiarity means an idea feels recognizable. Durable learning means you can retrieve it, explain it, use it, and recognize when it applies—even after time has passed.

A Tour of This Book

One of the best practices of being an effective teacher is to create a rhythm. (We’ll learn why when we learn about cognitive load later on.) This book has a rhythm, and we’re going to give you a tour now: the common features you’ll see over and over again.

Hook and big picture

The hook: start with the story, problem, or question. Ask why this matters before you worry about every term.

The big picture: after reading, try to reconstruct the chapter’s central claim in your own words.

Your study toolkit

Effective Study Strategies

Jamie Lee Clark’s one-page guide to five effective study strategies gives us a useful starting toolkit. Study it as both content and design: it explains the strategies, and its visual organization gives you a concrete example of dual coding.

Meta-example. After viewing the guide, close it and retrieve the five strategies from memory. Then ask: What does the visual organization make easier to compare or remember? What would be lost if the same information appeared only as paragraphs? Open the PDF in a new tab.
Meta-exampleWhat are the five strategies on Jamie Clark’s one-pager?▸

Retrieve them before looking back: retrieval practice, spaced practice, interleaving, dual coding, and elaboration.

Retrieval practice

Try to remember something before looking at the answer. Close the book and ask: What was the main idea? What terms and relationships do I remember? How would I explain this to another student?

Spaced practice

Return to material over several days or weeks instead of studying everything in one long session. You do not need to forget everything before returning. You do need to make your memory do some work.

Interleaving

Mix related topics, theories, or problem types instead of practicing one kind repeatedly until it feels easy. Early on, blocked practice helps you learn how to perform the tool. Once you can use it consistently, start mixing so you practice deciding which framework helps you interpret a new situation.

A rule for interleaving

First, practice using a tool until you are consistent and competent. Then practice choosing the right tool from the toolkit for the job at hand.

Practice beyond mastery

Once you can use a tool consistently, practice beyond mastery by changing the conditions. Explain it without cues, compare it with a neighboring framework, teach it to someone else, or apply it to an unfamiliar situation. Mastery is a starting point for flexible use, not a signal to stop.

Spiral or interleaved practice helps you learn when and why to use a tool. Transfer practice asks whether you can adapt it to a new situation. Several frameworks may offer useful perspectives on the same situation; ask what each one helps you see and what it might leave out.

Dual coding

Represent an idea with both words and a useful visual. Draw a developmental timeline, map the relationship between concepts, sketch a model, or label a process. The one-pager above is itself a dual-coding example.

Elaboration

Connect a new idea to what you already know and ask why, how, what is an example and non-example, what evidence would support or weaken the claim, and what a framework might leave out.

Worked exampleTake notes that help you think

Weak note: Spaced practice means studying over time. It helps memory.

Stronger note: Spread study and retrieval across days or weeks instead of cramming. Returning after some forgetting makes recall effortful, which strengthens later access. Example: review a chapter today, tomorrow, and again several days later. Question: How could a teacher build spaced review into ordinary classwork?

High-quality notes preserve the main idea, support, relationships, limits, applications, and a question you can answer later without looking. The goal is not beautiful pages. The goal is notes that help you think when the chapter is no longer open.

Use AI to increase the effort

Ask: “Quiz me one question at a time. Do not show me the answer until I respond. If I am wrong, give me a hint. Ask me to explain my reasoning, compare two frameworks, and apply an idea to a teaching or discipleship situation. Mix this chapter with an older one so I must decide which framework applies.”

Do not ask AI to summarize the chapter and mistake recognition for learning. If the tool does all the thinking, you are practicing watching the tool think.

A repeatable study routine
Read or listen. Retrieve. Practice. Correct yourself. Return later. Then choose and apply the right tool in a new situation.
Practice bank
Close the book before you begin. Write or say your answer, then open the check. These questions practice both sides of learning: performing a tool and deciding which tool fits the situation.
1 · RetrieveWhy can rereading and highlighting feel effective while producing weak learning?▸

A strong answer distinguishes familiarity from retrieval. Rereading and highlighting keep the material visible and create fluency, but by themselves they do not require you to retrieve, explain, or apply the idea.

2 · ChooseA student can perform a framework when told which one to use, but gets stuck in a new case. What practice is missing?▸

The student needs practice choosing a tool, not only performing it. Interleaving, transfer, and varied applications help develop that conditional judgment.

3 · NoteTurn this weak note into a useful one: “Spaced practice means studying over time.”▸

Add the mechanism, an example, and a question: “Spread study and retrieval across days or weeks instead of cramming. Returning after some forgetting makes recall effortful, which strengthens later access. How could a teacher build this into ordinary classwork?”

4 · PlanDesign a 15-minute study loop for a chapter you finished yesterday.▸

Begin closed-book with a brain dump or a few questions; check and correct; explain one idea in your own words; apply it to a new case; then schedule another retrieval session several days later.

5 · AI coachWrite a prompt that makes an AI increase your effort instead of doing the thinking for you.▸

A strong prompt asks for one question at a time, no answer before your response, hints after an error, explanation of your reasoning, mixed chapters, and a new teaching or discipleship application.

6 · TeachDesign one activity that makes another learner retrieve, elaborate, and apply an idea.▸

For example: ask the learner to explain the idea without notes, connect it to something they already know, and use it to respond to a new classroom, leadership, or discipleship situation. Give feedback after the attempt.
