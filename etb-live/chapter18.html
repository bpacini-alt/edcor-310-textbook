Belonging, Social Structure & Tribalism

The Architecture of Belonging

Chapter 18~ 45 min read

In the summer of 1954, two buses brought 22 boys to a Boy Scout camp in Robbers Cave State Park, Oklahoma. They were about eleven years old, and none of them knew each other. Each busload thought it had the camp to itself.

For a week, each group hiked, swam, and worked side by side. They picked names for themselves, the Eagles and the Rattlers, and painted them on their shirts and flags. They built their own habits and their own rules.

Then the two groups found out about each other. Right away, the boys started talking about “us” and “them,” and both groups begged the staff to let them take on the outsiders.

The staff were actually researchers working with psychologist Muzafer Sherif, and they set up a contest. It didn’t stay friendly. The Eagles burned the Rattlers’ flag. The Rattlers burned the Eagles’ flag back. Then the Rattlers raided the Eagles’ cabin at night, flipped the beds, and stole comic books and a pair of jeans.

In less than two weeks, a group of strangers had become a tight community, and two communities had become enemies. The researchers’ next job was harder: could they bring the boys back together? (You’ll find out in Concept 4.)

This chapter is about how groups pull people in and push people out. By the end, our hope is that you’ll understand what makes a group worth belonging to, and how to build one in your own classroom where every student is inside the circle.

The big picture

People need to belong, and students who feel shut out have less left over for learning. Every group, from a nation to a friend group to a fourth-grade class, holds people together with shared connection and shared rules. But the same pull that draws people in also draws a line around them, and that line makes “us versus them” easy. Latter-day Saints, like many people of faith, believe every person is a child of God, which means no student should end up outside the circle.

Chapter 17 looked at the cultures students bring with them. This chapter looks at the culture you build together. We start with how any group works: why belonging matters, what holds a community together, how groups turn against outsiders, and how they can bring outsiders in. Then we turn to your own classroom and what it takes to build a class where every student is seen and needed.

Major concept 1 · The Need to Belong

Belonging

In 1995, psychologists Roy Baumeister and Mark Leary reviewed decades of research and argued that the need to belong is one of the basic human motivations. People form bonds quickly, even with strangers, and they fight to keep them. When those bonds are missing, people suffer, in their health as well as their mood.

ResearchBelonging at school

In 2020, Hanke Korpershoek and colleagues combined 82 studies of secondary students. Students who felt they belonged at school showed somewhat higher achievement, and the links were stronger for motivation, engagement, and confidence. They were also less likely to skip school or drop out. These studies measured belonging and outcomes at the same time, so they can’t prove which came first. But the direction is consistent across many schools and countries.

In plain terms: belonging doesn’t make students smarter. It keeps them in the room, willing to try, and able to put their energy into the work instead of into protecting themselves. That doesn’t mean waiting until a class feels ready before you ask for hard work. Belonging and challenge grow together.

Common misconception“Rejection hurts exactly like physical pain.”►

In 2003, Naomi Eisenberger and colleagues had people play a simple online ball-tossing game while their brains were scanned. Partway through, the other players (actually a computer) stopped throwing them the ball. Being left out lit up some of the same brain regions that respond to physical pain, and the idea spread quickly: rejection hurts like a broken bone.

Later work complicated that. In 2014, Choong-Wan Woo and colleagues scanned 60 people as they felt painful heat and as they looked at photos of an ex-partner who had broken up with them. A finer-grained analysis found that the two experiences used the same general areas but different brain patterns. Rejection really hurts. It just isn’t the same thing as physical pain, so take that line with a grain of salt when you see it in teacher trainings.

Connecting to Chapter 14

Chapter 14 described two kinds of belonging. Egoistic belonging is being seen: you are known and valued as an individual. Altruistic belonging is being needed: the group counts on what you contribute. Chapter 14 argued that being needed is the sturdier of the two, because it doesn’t depend on anyone’s approval on a given day. Concept 5 of this chapter is about how to build both in your classroom.

Retrieval practiceA student sits silently through every lesson. She never participates and never causes trouble. A colleague says, “She seems content. No issues there.” What question should you be asking instead, and why does it matter?►

The better question is “Does she feel like she belongs here?” A student can cause no problems and still feel completely outside the class. Silence can mean comfort, but it can also mean she has decided it isn’t safe to be seen.

The question matters because the response is different. If the problem were behavior, you would manage behavior. If the problem is belonging, you change her experience: learn something about her, notice her as an individual, and give her a task where the group actually needs her contribution.

Major concept 2 · What Holds a Group Together

Norms and Anomie

Émile Durkheim, one of the founders of sociology, spent the late 1800s asking why some communities hold together better than others. His answer comes down to two things a group has to get right.

The first is social integration: how connected people are to the group. The second is social regulation: how clearly the group’s norms guide what people do. Durkheim thought both could go wrong in either direction.

Too littleAbout rightToo much
Integration (connection)People feel isolated and on their ownPeople feel part of something and responsible to each otherThe group swallows the individual; everyone must conform
Regulation (norms)Anomie: people don’t know what’s expectedClear, fair expectations people can count onRigid rules that crush growth and breed resentment

Durkheim’s best-known idea is anomie, what happens when regulation breaks down. Without clear norms, people don’t feel free. They feel unsure what the group expects, less connected to it, and more anxious. In a classroom, anomie shows up when rules are enforced one day and ignored the next, when expectations change without explanation, or when students can’t tell what the teacher actually wants. Students in that situation aren’t trying to cause chaos. They are reacting to a room that hasn’t given them solid ground.

Norms are how regulation actually works. Some are formal, like a rule posted on the wall: raise your hand before speaking. Others are informal and never written down: whether it’s okay to look like you’re trying, whether wrong answers get laughed at, whether the teacher’s word really means anything. Informal norms are often more powerful, because classmates enforce them with approval and disapproval. They decide whether the formal rules get followed at all.

Can you include people by removing norms?

One popular idea goes like this: if norms leave some students out, the way to include everyone is to loosen them. Lower the expectations, soften the rules, and no one will feel like an outsider. Sometimes a specific norm really is unfair and should change. But as a general plan, Durkheim would say it gets things backward. Norms aren’t a fence around a community; they are part of what makes it a community. Take them away and you don’t get a more welcoming group. You get a room full of individuals with nothing in common to belong to.

There is a real tradeoff. A group with strong norms offers strong belonging, but it asks more of its members. A group with few norms is easy to join but offers little to hold on to. A school with no shared expectations isn’t a community; it’s a building. A school with rigid, punishing rules isn’t a community either; it’s a compliance system.

Durkheim saw this most clearly in religion. In his last major book, The Elementary Forms of Religious Life (1912), he argued that shared beliefs and practices are what bind believers into a single moral community. Think of a Catholic family at Sunday Mass, a Muslim family fasting through Ramadan, a Jewish family keeping the Sabbath, or a Latter-day Saint ward serving a neighbor together. A faith’s norms about how to worship, eat, dress, rest, and care for each other aren’t just rules. They tell members who they are and that they belong to each other. That goes deeper than acceptance. Members aren’t merely tolerated; they share a way of life.

ResearchCommunities that ask more

Anthropologist Richard Sosis studied about 200 communes founded in 19th-century America, some religious and some secular. The religious communes lasted much longer on average. In a follow-up study of 83 of them, Sosis and Eric Bressler found that religious communes that asked more of their members, with rules about food, dress, possessions, and contact with outsiders, tended to last longer. For secular communes, asking more made no difference.

This is historical data, not an experiment, and scholars still debate why the pattern appears. But it fits Durkheim’s point: norms people believe in and share hold a community together. A classroom isn’t a faith community, but the lesson carries over. Students belong more deeply to a class whose norms they understand and share than to one that simply lets them in.

Which norms are load-bearing?

The question for a teacher is which norms are load-bearing. A load-bearing norm serves the class’s real purpose and is open to anyone willing to commit to it: “We don’t mock each other’s ideas.” Other norms are just familiar: one group’s habits treated as everyone’s standard. “Look the teacher in the eye when she’s talking to you” may feel like basic respect, but as Chapter 17 noted, in some cultures avoiding an adult’s eyes is the respectful thing to do. Hold load-bearing norms firmly. Look hard at familiar ones, and let them go when they exclude students for no good reason.

Retrieval practiceTwo teachers both say they value inclusion. Ms. A drops most of her classroom rules and lets students work things out day by day, so no one feels left out. Mr. B keeps a few firm rules and explains to students why each one matters for the class. Using Durkheim, predict which class is more likely to feel like a community, and why.►

Durkheim would predict Mr. B’s class. Ms. A’s class is set up for anomie. With no stable norms, students spend their energy figuring out what’s expected and who’s really in charge. Norms will still form, but they’ll be set by whoever has the most social pull, not by the teacher.

Mr. B’s class gives students something to belong to, and his explanations let them buy in instead of just obeying. One condition matters: his rules have to be load-bearing, serving the class’s purpose and open to everyone. If they only reflect one group’s habits, they will push students out instead of drawing them in.

Major concept 3 · Us and Them

Tribalism

Tribalism is the pull toward strong loyalty to your own group, often at the expense of people outside it. It isn’t a modern problem or a political buzzword. For most of human history, people lived in small groups and depended on them to survive, and being cast out was dangerous. Our minds still track who is in our group and who isn’t, quickly and without being asked. That instinct isn’t good or bad on its own. It can build a loyal, caring community, or it can tear people apart, depending on what activates it.

The engine of tribalism is ingroup bias: the automatic habit of trusting and favoring people in your own group. It starts with surprisingly little. In the early 1970s, psychologist Henri Tajfel told teenage boys at a school in Bristol, England, that they had been sorted into groups by which of two painters’ work they preferred. The sorting was actually random. The boys never met their groupmates and had nothing to gain personally. Yet when they handed out small rewards, they favored their own group, and some even gave up a bigger total payout just so their group would come out ahead of the other one. Later studies using this minimal group setup have found the same pattern many times.

The Robbers Cave boys showed the same thing. They were talking about “us” and “them” before the first contest had even started.

In-group preference is not the same thing as hatred or violence. The Robbers Cave conflict involved competition and deliberate experimental arrangements, and the study’s small, homogeneous sample limits how broadly we should generalize.

The most defensible conclusion is not “people inevitably become enemies.” It is that group boundaries are easy to establish, and competition can turn those boundaries into conflict. Cooperation, shared identity, and real interdependence can move groups in a different direction.

3Application

Build a whole-class “we” early. Use group identities to create belonging, but avoid making students’ success depend on another group’s failure. When students from different groups need one another to complete a meaningful task, the cooperation is doing more work than a lecture about tolerance can do.

Studies, methods, and limitations
  • Tajfel, Billig, Bundy, & Flament (1971), Social categorization and intergroup behaviour — Classic minimal-group experiments in which schoolboys were assigned to arbitrary categories and allocated real rewards to anonymous in-group and out-group members.
  • Brown (2020), The origins of the minimal group paradigm — Historical and conceptual review of the paradigm and what its findings do—and do not—show.
  • Sherif et al. (1954/1961), The Robbers Cave Experiment — Naturalistic study of two groups of boys at a Boy Scout camp; the sample was small and highly homogeneous.
  • Sherif et al., “Superordinate Goals in the Reduction of Intergroup Conflict” — Describes how shared goals requiring cooperation reduced hostility between the groups.

Ingroup bias doesn’t require hostility. At first, it’s just preference. But it can grow in steps:

  1. Ingroup: the group you belong to and feel loyal to, such as a friend group, a team, or a family.
  2. Outgroup: people seen as outside your group. They tend to be judged more harshly and trusted less. They also get seen as all alike (“they’re all the same”), a pattern called outgroup homogeneity.
  3. Othering: treating outsiders not just as different but as lesser. This is the step where preference becomes exclusion and discrimination.

The slide from one step to the next is most likely when resources are scarce, when a group feels threatened, or when differences between groups are made obvious.

In schools, tribalism ranges from ordinary to serious. Young children form friend groups that can harden into cliques with their own rules and borders. Older students add identity: ethnicity and culture, faith, sports, academic tracks, and politics. These identities aren’t problems to solve. They give students meaning and a place to belong. The teacher’s job isn’t to erase group identity. It’s to keep it from turning into exclusion and othering.

Politics deserves a brief mention, since students are growing up in a divided time. When a political side becomes someone’s main tribe, people start judging evidence by which team it helps. Helping students notice ingroup bias in themselves and listen to the other side with real curiosity are valuable skills, whatever students’ own views turn out to be.

A partisan difference does not prove that one side is lying or that the other side is objectively correct. People may experience different economic conditions, attend to different indicators, receive different information, or interpret ambiguous evidence through partisan identities.

The careful claim is that political identity can influence how people perceive and evaluate reality. That is narrower—and more defensible—than saying tribalism makes everyone unintelligent or that economic facts do not matter.

3Application

Before judging a political administration, decide which indicators you will use and examine them across several years. Ask whether you would use the same standard if the other party controlled the White House. Separate the question “What happened?” from the question “How much responsibility does this president deserve?”

Working with the instinct, not against it

Students will form tribes whether you plan for it or not. The real question is whether the tribes in your room are ones you helped shape or ones that formed on their own. If you ignore the instinct, the class’s identity gets set by whoever has the most social pull. If you work with it, you can give students a tribe worth belonging to.

The houses at Hogwarts in the Harry Potter books are a good picture of this. Fans happily sort themselves into houses and take pride in their house’s values. A class can work the same way. A name, a few shared values, routines that are yours, and some history together give students a group to be loyal to, and that group’s norms can be the ones you want in the room. The loyalty that might go into keeping a clique closed can go into something the whole class shares.

Worth remembering

Students will form tribes. Give them one worth belonging to.

The instinct will show up either way. A class identity you help shape gives that loyalty somewhere good to go.

Retrieval practiceA student tells you he doesn’t trust a classmate because “people like that are all the same.” Name the two patterns this shows, and describe one thing you could do about it.►

First, ingroup bias: he trusts people in his group more and people outside it less. Second, outgroup homogeneity: “they’re all the same” treats everyone in the other group as interchangeable instead of as individuals.

A lecture on bias usually won’t change much. A better move is to set up a task where the two students have equal roles, share a goal, and need each other to finish. That gives him a chance to know the classmate as a person instead of as a member of a group. Concept 4 explains why this works.

Major concept 4 · Widening the Circle

The Contact Hypothesis

If tribes are going to form anyway, the most useful question is how to widen the circle of who counts as “us.” Social psychologists have studied this for more than 70 years.

In 1954, psychologist Gordon Allport proposed what is now called the contact hypothesis: time spent with people from another group reduces prejudice, especially under four conditions.

ConditionWhat it meansIn a classroom
Equal statusNeither group is in charge of the other during the contactNo group starts with a built-in advantage in the task
Common goalsBoth groups are working toward the same outcomeOne shared product, not competing ones
CooperationThe work requires working together, not side by sideNo one can finish without the others
Support from authorityThose in charge clearly back the contactYou treat mixed groups as normal and expected
ResearchDoes contact work?

In 2006, Thomas Pettigrew and Linda Tropp combined 515 studies covering more than 250,000 people. Contact between groups usually reduced prejudice. When Allport’s conditions were present, the reduction was larger. But contact still helped on average even when some conditions were missing, so the four conditions make contact work better rather than being required for it to work at all.

One caution: only about 5% of those studies were true experiments, and the rest can’t fully rule out that less-prejudiced people simply seek out more contact. Pettigrew and Tropp found that the stronger studies showed larger effects, not smaller ones, which is a good sign. Still, putting students side by side isn’t enough. Contact with no shared purpose can just give two groups more chances to clash.

Back to Robbers Cave

Sherif’s team first tried simply bringing the Eagles and Rattlers together for pleasant things: meals, movies, fireworks. It didn’t work. The shared meals turned into food fights.

What worked were superordinate goals, problems both groups cared about that neither could solve alone. The camp’s water supply “broke” (the staff had blocked it), and both groups had to search the line together to fix it. The boys wanted to rent a movie, but neither group could afford it alone, so they pooled their money. A truck bringing food “stalled,” and all the boys pulled it with a rope together. Little by little, hostility gave way to cooperation, and cooperation to friendship. By the end of camp, the boys asked to ride home on the same bus.

Robbers Cave was one camp with 22 boys, so it can’t carry this lesson alone. But it fits the wider contact research, and it points to a clear design rule: give students from different groups work they truly need each other to finish. Sitting at the same table isn’t enough. A group project that one strong student can carry isn’t a superordinate goal; it’s a chance for one person to do all the work. The structure has to make the need for each other real.

Go deeperThe camp that failed►

Robbers Cave wasn’t Sherif’s first try. Historian Gina Perry found that in 1953, at a camp in Middle Grove, New York, Sherif ran a similar study with one big difference: the boys spent the first days together and became friends before being split into two groups. When the staff tried to stir up rivalry, the boys refused to turn on each other. Several figured out they were being set up, and their suspicion turned toward the adults instead. Sherif called the study off.

At Robbers Cave the next year, the groups never met until they were rivals, and Perry’s archive work shows the staff did more to fuel the conflict than the published account suggests, such as quietly helping one team win. So the famous result isn’t pure, natural hostility. But the failed camp may hold the more hopeful lesson for teachers: when a group bonds as one community first, it is much harder to split. Build the whole-class “we” early, before smaller tribes harden.

Why widen the circle at all?

The research shows how circles grow. It doesn’t say why the person outside my group should be inside it at all. Different traditions have given different answers. The Stoics, whom you met in Chapter 17, taught that every human being shares in reason, and that this gives everyone a dignity no tribe can grant or take away. That idea is called cosmopolitanism. The classical liberal tradition argues that every person has natural rights that come before any group membership. That idea is behind equal protection under the law, and behind the idea that a public school must serve every student, not just those in the majority.

The Latter-day Saint tradition that sponsors this university gives a third answer, one many faiths share in some form: every person is a child of God. That worth comes before every earthly label, and no group’s judgment can shrink it. These traditions disagree about plenty, but they agree on this: something in every person goes deeper than the groups they belong to, and that is the reason the circle should keep growing. Every teacher needs some answer to why each student in front of them matters. We, the authors, believe this one is true.

Retrieval practiceA teacher puts two rival friend groups together and says, “Make a poster about our school.” Using Allport’s four conditions, what’s present and what’s missing? How would you redesign the task?►

There is a common goal, though a weak one, and the teacher’s backing may count as support from authority. Equal status isn’t guaranteed: if one group is more artistic or more socially dominant, it will take over and the old pecking order will repeat. Real cooperation is missing, too, since one person could make the whole poster while the others watch. Forcing rivals together without a real reason to cooperate can even raise the tension.

A better design gives each student a piece the poster can’t do without, such as a different section only they can research or create, so the final product only comes together when everyone contributes. That is the structure that worked at Robbers Cave.

Major concept 5 · The Culture in Your Classroom

Building Belonging

So far, this chapter has been about groups in general. Now it’s about yours. Every classroom builds a culture, on purpose or by accident, and belonging is built the same way: in small, daily choices that add up to “I matter here” or quietly wear it down. Two questions from Chapter 14 can guide you. Do students feel seen as individuals? And do they feel needed by the group?

ResearchTelling students they belong isn’t enough

In the largest test of its kind, published in 2023, Gregory Walton and dozens of colleagues gave 26,911 incoming students at 22 colleges a short online exercise, under 30 minutes, before classes began. Students read survey results and stories from older students showing that worries about belonging are normal and usually fade. The exercise raised the share of students who finished their first year full-time, most of all for groups that had historically struggled at those colleges. The gains were small, but this was a brief, cheap exercise given to thousands of people.

The most important finding was about the setting. The exercise worked only at colleges that actually gave those students real chances to belong. A message about belonging helps students act on opportunities that are already there. It can’t create them. That is the teacher’s job.

Being seen

The simplest place to start is names. In a 2017 study of a biology course with 185 students, Katelyn Cooper and colleagues had every student use a folded name card at their desk. By the end, 78% of students thought the instructors knew their names, compared with 20% in their earlier large classes. The instructors actually knew only about half. Most students said it mattered to them, because it made it easier to ask for help and made them feel more invested in the class. Learning names quickly is a skill you can practice with seating charts, name cards, and using names every time you speak to a student.

Beyond names, being seen comes from noticing. Notice when a talkative student goes quiet, or when someone who was absent comes back. Remember something a student told you and ask about it later. A thirty-second check-in at the door or a short note on a returned paper tells a student that this teacher sees them as a person.

The room sends signals too, before anyone speaks. Whose work is on the walls? Whose names and lives show up in your examples? A student who never sees anyone like them in the room gets a quiet message about whose space it really is.

Being needed

Students feel needed when the work actually needs them. If one student can carry the group, if roles are just for show, or if anyone could have produced the final product alone, students learn that their presence is optional. Before any group task, ask: if this student were absent, would the group’s work really be weaker? If not, the need for each other isn’t real yet.

There’s a trap here, too. A student who only seeks to be seen can slide into what we might call a consumption mindset: “I need people to like me so I can get something from being around them.” It sounds backward, but the surest way to feel you belong is to ask “What can I give this group?” rather than “What can this group give me?” Jesus taught a version of this long ago: whoever loses his life for His sake will find it (Matthew 10:39), the idea at the heart of Chapter 14.

Shared routines build the “we” as well. A consistent opening, words and jokes that belong only to this class, and celebrating milestones together all mark the group as yours. Chapter 17 described research by Scott Wiltermuth and Chip Heath finding that people who walked in step or sang together afterward cooperated more and trusted each other more. A class that starts the same way every day and has its own history is building something a class without those things isn’t.

Worth remembering

Every student should be seen, and every student should be needed.

Names and noticing tell students they are seen. Work that truly depends on them tells them they are needed.

Norms that hold it together

Keep a small number of load-bearing norms (Concept 2), enforce them the same way on Friday as on Monday, and be able to explain why each one matters for the class.

One norm deserves special attention: mistakes have to be survivable. When errors bring visible disappointment, public correction, or a teacher’s cooling regard, students learn to protect themselves instead of taking risks. Whether it’s safe to be wrong is one of the strongest signals a class sends about whether it’s safe to be there at all.

A simple weekly check

At the end of each week, ask yourself two questions. Did students connect with me or with each other as individuals this week? Did they work on something together that needed every person’s contribution? The first checks whether students are seen. The second checks whether they are needed. If either answer keeps coming up no, you know where to invest.

Retrieval practiceDescribe the difference between being seen and being needed in a classroom. Then give one practice that builds each, and one common mistake that undermines each.►

Being seen means a student is known and valued as an individual. Being needed means the group counts on what that student contributes.

To build being seen, learn and use every student’s name in the first week. A mistake that undermines it is treating students as a category (“the quiet ones,” “the back row”) instead of as individuals.

To build being needed, design tasks where the group’s work is weaker if any one student is missing. A mistake that undermines it is group work one person can carry, which teaches the others that their presence is optional.

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

Belonging can be built on purpose. These practices come straight from the chapter.

  • Learn every student’s name in the first week, and use it. Name cards and seating charts help. It costs little and tells each student they aren’t interchangeable.
  • Build the whole-class “we” early. Give the class a name, a few shared values, and routines of its own before smaller cliques harden.
  • Design at least one task per unit that truly needs every student. Before assigning it, ask whether the group’s work would be weaker if any one student were absent.
  • Check Allport’s conditions before mixing groups that don’t get along. Look for equal status, a shared goal, real cooperation, and your visible support. The more of these you have, the better contact works.
  • Keep a few load-bearing norms, and enforce them consistently. Be able to explain why each one matters. Let go of rules that are only familiar.
  • Look at your room the way a new student would. Whose work is on the walls, and whose lives show up in your examples?
  • Make mistakes survivable. Reward effort and revision, not just getting it right the first time.
Chapter synthesis
Every classroom builds a culture. The only question is whether it builds one where every student belongs.
Belonging is a basic need. It doesn’t make students smarter, but students who feel they belong are more engaged and more likely to stay.
Groups hold together through connection and norms. Too little connection leaves people isolated. Too little regulation produces anomie: not freedom, but anxiety and drift.
Including people doesn’t mean emptying the group of norms. Keep the load-bearing norms. Look hard at the merely familiar ones.
Tribes will form. Ingroup bias starts from almost nothing. Design a class identity worth being loyal to before one forms without you.
Contact helps, and the right conditions help more. Equal status, shared goals, real cooperation, and support from those in charge make contact work better. Shared goals that need everyone are the strongest tool.
Students need to be seen and needed. Names and noticing build the first. Work that truly needs every student builds the second.

Paul wrote to the Ephesians, a church made of two groups who had long kept apart: “Now therefore ye are no more strangers and foreigners, but fellowcitizens with the saints, and of the household of God” (Ephesians 2:19). That is the kind of community this chapter describes. It has real standards and a real identity, and its circle keeps growing until no one is a stranger.

We believe every student who walks into your room is a child of God. Build a class where each of them can feel it.

Worth remembering
  1. Students will form tribes. Give them one worth belonging to.
  2. Every student should be seen, and every student should be needed.
