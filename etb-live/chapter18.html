Belonging, Social Structure & Tribalism

The Architecture of Belonging

Chapter 18~ 50 min read

The year is 1997. Researchers at Case Western Reserve University want to understand what happens to people when they are excluded from a group. They recruit participants to meet in small groups, then quietly pull some aside and tell them — falsely — that none of the other participants wanted to work with them. No one actually said this. The participants believed it anyway. Within minutes, their self-regulation declined. Their performance on subsequent tasks deteriorated. They became simultaneously more aggressive and more withdrawn. One follow-up study described the cognitive aftermath as resembling a state of mild brain damage.

Social exclusion doesn’t just hurt feelings. It disrupts the machinery of thought.

This chapter is about what that means for teachers — which is to say, for anyone who stands in a room with 25 or 30 people who need to feel, at some level, that they are welcome there. The same social instincts that make exclusion so devastating also make inclusion so powerful. They are the same wiring. Understanding both — what pulls communities together and what tears them apart, what makes a tribe worth belonging to and what makes tribalism destructive, what norms produce community and what norms produce only compliance — is some of the most important social science a teacher can know. And it turns out the research is not just sobering. It is practically useful in ways that can change how you design a classroom from day one.

The big picture
Humans are wired for belonging — it is not a preference but a fundamental need, as basic as food and shelter. The same social instincts that bind communities together also draw the boundaries that divide them. This chapter explores how belonging shapes learning, how social structures hold communities together and what happens when they break down, how tribalism operates in schools, and — most importantly — what it looks like to build communities whose circle keeps expanding rather than contracting.

Major concept 1 · Belonging

A Primal Need

Belonging is not a nice-to-have feature of a good classroom — it is a prerequisite for learning. Research in developmental psychology and education consistently shows that students who feel a genuine sense of belonging engage more deeply with content, take more intellectual risks, attend school more regularly, and demonstrate stronger academic outcomes. Students who do not feel they belong — who feel invisible, excluded, or tolerated rather than welcomed — redirect enormous cognitive and emotional energy toward social monitoring and self-protection, leaving far less available for learning. A student who does not feel safe in a classroom cannot fully occupy that classroom as a learner.

Social psychologists describe belonging as a fundamental human motivation — not a personality trait or a luxury, but a core drive as basic as hunger. When belonging needs are unmet, the consequences are not merely social; they are physiological and cognitive. Chronic exclusion activates the same neural pathways as physical pain. Students experiencing social rejection show measurable declines in cognitive performance, self-regulation, and motivation. Understanding this is not about lowering expectations — it is about recognizing that belonging is infrastructure. Without it, everything else the teacher builds sits on an unstable foundation.

Belonging in the classroom manifests in two distinct but complementary forms. Egoistic belonging is the sense that you are seen and valued as an individual — that your presence specifically matters, not just any warm body in a seat. This is built through personal recognition: teachers who learn names quickly, notice absences, remember details from conversations, and respond to students as individuals rather than as a category. Altruistic belonging is the sense that you are part of something larger than yourself — that the group depends on your contribution and would be genuinely diminished without it. This is built through meaningful collaborative work, shared goals, and classroom cultures where each student’s perspective is understood to make the group stronger. The most powerful learning environments cultivate both.

For educators

Belonging is not produced by a single grand gesture — it is built through accumulated small moments. Greeting students by name at the door. Noticing when someone is off. Designing collaborative tasks where every voice is genuinely needed. Acknowledging mistakes without shame. These are not soft additions to rigorous instruction; they are the conditions that make rigorous instruction possible. A teacher who waits until the classroom “feels ready” to push academic rigor has it backwards — belonging and challenge grow together.

Retrieval practiceA student sits silently through every lesson, never participates, never causes trouble. A colleague says: “That student seems content — no issues there.” What question should you actually be asking, and why does the distinction matter?►

The right question is not “is this student causing problems?” but “does this student feel they belong here?” Those are completely different questions, and a student can score zero on the first while scoring zero on the second. Silence is not evidence of belonging — it can be evidence of withdrawal, of the decision not to risk being seen, of energy being spent on social monitoring rather than learning.

The distinction matters because the intervention is different. If the issue is disruption, you manage behavior. If the issue is absent belonging, you change the environment — you design tasks that require this student’s specific contribution, you notice them as an individual rather than as the quiet one, you engineer the conditions that make it safe to be present. The colleague’s framing — “no issues there” — misses the most important issue entirely.

Major concept 2 · Social Structure

What Holds Communities Together

Émile Durkheim, one of the founding figures of sociology, argued in the late 19th century that individuals cannot be understood apart from the social structures they inhabit. For Durkheim, society is not simply the sum of its individuals — it is a force that shapes, constrains, and gives meaning to individual lives in ways that individuals themselves rarely perceive. His central insight for educators is this: the health of a community — including a classroom community — depends on getting two variables right: integration and regulation.

Social Integration
The degree to which individuals are connected to and embedded in the group. High integration produces cohesion, mutual responsibility, and shared purpose. Too little leads to isolation and alienation. Too much can suppress individuality and demand conformity at the cost of personal identity.
Social Regulation
The degree to which norms, rules, and expectations structure behavior within the group. Well-calibrated regulation creates the predictability and fairness that allow communities to function. Too little produces anomie. Too much produces rigidity, resentment, and the suppression of growth.

Anomie — Durkheim’s most influential concept — describes what happens when regulation breaks down. In a state of anomie, individuals lack clear norms to guide their behavior and lose their sense of connection to the social order. In a classroom context, anomie can emerge when rules are inconsistently enforced, expectations shift without explanation, or students cannot read the social environment around them. The result is not freedom — it is anxiety, disengagement, and sometimes acting out. Students are not misbehaving because they want chaos; they are responding to a norm structure that has failed to give them solid ground.

Norms are the visible mechanism of social regulation. They can be formal — written rules posted on the wall — or informal, existing only in the understood expectations of the group. Informal norms are often more powerful than formal ones because they are enforced socially, through approval and disapproval, inclusion and exclusion. In any classroom, both types operate simultaneously. The formal norm says “raise your hand before speaking.” The informal norm says whether it is actually socially acceptable to raise your hand, whether effort is respected or mocked, and whether the teacher’s authority feels legitimate in students’ eyes. Effective classroom community-building attends to both.

Retrieval practiceA new teacher decides to run a “no-rules” classroom — everything is negotiated in the moment, expectations shift day to day, no formal norms are established. Using Durkheim’s framework, predict what will likely happen in this classroom over the first few weeks.►

Durkheim would predict anomie: anxiety, disengagement, and behavioral acting out — not because students want chaos, but because the absence of stable norms deprives them of the social ground they need to function. The “no-rules” framing assumes that norms are constraints on students’ natural freedom. Durkheim argues the opposite: norms are constitutive of community, and without them students don’t get freer — they get more anxious about what the social environment actually requires of them.

The practical prediction: students will spend cognitive and social energy trying to figure out what the rules actually are, who has informal authority, and what will happen if they push boundaries. Informal norms will emerge anyway — but designed by whoever has the strongest social gravity in the room, not by the teacher. The irony of the no-rules classroom is that it doesn’t eliminate regulation; it just hands control of it to whoever would have used it least wisely.

Major concept 3 · Tribalism

The Double-Edged Instinct

Tribalism is not a modern pathology or a political talking point — it is one of the most ancient and deeply wired features of human social cognition. For most of human evolutionary history, survival depended on group membership. Individuals who were expelled from their group faced death. This is still manifest in adolescent psychology today: for teenagers, social death can feel worse than physical death. The neural systems that track group membership, detect threats from outsiders, and reward ingroup loyalty are not recent additions — they are among the oldest parts of our social brain. Understanding tribalism begins with accepting that it is not a bug in human nature. It is a feature — one that can be enormously constructive or devastatingly destructive depending on the conditions that activate it.

The psychological engine of tribalism is ingroup bias — the automatic tendency to evaluate, trust, and support members of one’s own group more favorably than members of outgroups. Ingroup bias does not require hostility; it operates through preferential treatment of the ingroup. But the line between preference and prejudice is thin and easily crossed, particularly when resources are scarce, identities feel threatened, or differences between groups are made salient. Research shows that ingroup bias can be triggered by the most arbitrary distinctions — participants randomly assigned to groups based on trivial criteria quickly develop ingroup loyalty and outgroup skepticism.

Ingroup
The group you identify with and belong to. Membership produces loyalty, solidarity, shared norms, and preferential treatment of fellow members. In school settings: friend groups, sports teams, academic tracks, cultural communities.
Outgroup
Those perceived as outside your group. Outgroup members are often judged more harshly, trusted less, and attributed less complex inner lives — a phenomenon called outgroup homogeneity (“they’re all the same”).
Othering
The active process of defining outgroup members as fundamentally different or lesser — not just “not us” but “less than us.” Othering is how ingroup/outgroup thinking becomes discrimination, exclusion, and dehumanization.

Tribalism in schools spans from the mundane to the serious. At the elementary level, children naturally cluster into friendship groups that can quickly become exclusive cliques — informal tribes with their own norms, loyalties, and boundaries. At the secondary level, tribalism becomes overlaid with identity: racial and ethnic identity, political affiliation, athletic or academic track, religious community. These identities are not problems to be solved — they are sources of meaning and belonging. The challenge for educators is not to eliminate group identity but to prevent the exclusion and othering that tribalism makes so easy.

Political tribalism deserves particular attention given the social environment students are growing up in. When political identity becomes a primary tribal identity — when people evaluate evidence, choose friends, and form moral judgments primarily based on partisan loyalty rather than reason — the result is a public discourse increasingly resistant to facts, good faith, and genuine disagreement. Teaching students to recognize ingroup bias in themselves, to engage with outgroup perspectives with genuine curiosity, and to separate a person’s ideas from their tribal affiliation are among the most important civic skills a teacher can develop.

Working with the tribal instinct, not against it

Tribal instincts are not inherently bad — they are inevitable. The question for a teacher is not whether students will form tribes — they will, with or without your involvement — but whether the tribes that form in your room are ones you designed or ones that designed themselves. A teacher who ignores this hands the tribal instinct over to whoever has the strongest social gravity in the room. A teacher who works with it can create mission-aligned tribes — groups whose identity is tied to something worth being loyal to. Hogwarts houses are a useful model precisely because they work: students who have never read Harry Potter will still sort themselves, defend their house, and feel genuine pride in its values. A classroom that has its own identity, a set of values, shared rituals, and a sense of history gives students a tribe worth belonging to — one whose norms happen to be the ones you want operating in the room. The loyalty that might otherwise go into clique maintenance gets redirected toward something the whole group can invest in. Students are going to separate into tribes. The only question is whether you had anything to say about which ones.

Retrieval practiceA student confides that they don’t trust a classmate because “people like that are all the same.” Name the two specific phenomena this illustrates, and describe one concrete thing you could do as a teacher to address it.►

The two phenomena: First, ingroup bias — the student is automatically trusting ingroup members more and outgroup members less. Second, outgroup homogeneity — the student is treating all members of the outgroup as interchangeable, denying them individual complexity. “They’re all the same” is the cognitive signature of outgroup homogeneity: the ingroup is varied and complex; the outgroup is a type.

One concrete response: The most research-supported intervention is structured contact under Allport’s four conditions (covered in the next concept) — designing a task where both students have genuinely equal roles, work toward a shared goal, and genuinely need each other to succeed. This doesn’t require a lecture on bias. It requires an environment where the two students actually need to know each other as individuals rather than as representatives of their groups. The goal is not to convince the student their bias is wrong; it is to create conditions that generate disconfirming experience.

Major concept 4 · Expanding the Circle

How Good Tribes Grow

If tribalism is inevitable, the most important question is not how to suppress it but how to expand the circle of who counts as “us.” This turns out to be one of the most studied questions in social psychology — and the research is both sobering and practically useful. Simply putting people from different groups in the same room does not reduce prejudice. It can make things worse. What matters is the conditions under which contact happens.

Gordon Allport’s contact theory, developed in the 1950s, identified four conditions that must be present for intergroup contact to actually reduce hostility and build connection. All four matter — the presence of one or two without the others is often insufficient.

Equal status
Groups must meet as equals in the contact situation — not as dominant and subordinate. In a classroom, this means designing tasks where no group starts with a built-in advantage.
Common goals
Groups must be working toward the same outcome, not competing for different ones. Shared purpose reorients attention from group difference to shared investment.
Cooperation
The task must require working together, not parallel effort. Groups sitting in the same room but doing separate work are not in meaningful contact — they are just proximate.
Institutional support
The authority structure must visibly sanction and model the contact as normal and valued. Top-down endorsement matters more than most teachers realize.

The most powerful tool Allport’s framework points toward is the superordinate goal — a concept demonstrated vividly in Muzafer Sherif’s Robbers Cave experiment. Sherif took two groups of boys at a summer camp, allowed them to develop intense ingroup loyalty and outgroup hostility, and then tried various interventions to reduce the conflict. Simply bringing the groups together for pleasant activities did nothing. What worked was giving both groups a problem neither could solve alone: a broken water supply, a stuck truck, a movie both groups wanted to watch but neither could afford independently. When the groups needed each other to get something they both wanted, hostility gave way to cooperation — and cooperation gave way to genuine friendship. The enemy became a teammate, and the teammate became a friend.

The classroom application is direct: design tasks where students from different groups genuinely need each other to succeed. Not tasks where they happen to be seated together, but tasks where the goal is unreachable without each person’s contribution. A group project where one person can do all the work is not a superordinate goal structure — it is an opportunity for one person to do all the work. The structure has to make interdependence real.

But the research on contact theory describes the mechanics of how circles expand. It does not answer the deeper question of why they should — what grounds the belief that the person outside my tribe is worth including at all. Different traditions have offered different answers. The Stoic tradition argued for cosmopolitanism: the idea that every human being shares in universal reason, and that this shared rational nature is the basis of a dignity that no tribe can confer or revoke. The classical liberal tradition makes a parallel argument from a different starting point: every individual possesses natural rights that precede group membership — the philosophical foundation of equal protection under the law and the idea that a public school must serve every student, not just the ones who belong to the dominant group. The Latter-day Saint tradition offers a third account: that every person carries the identity of child of God, locating human worth in divine parentage — a worth that is unconditional, that precedes every earthly category, and that cannot be diminished by any group’s judgment. These traditions disagree about plenty, but they converge on this: there is something in every person that transcends their tribal affiliation, and that something is the basis of their dignity and the reason the circle should keep expanding. A teacher does not need to adopt any particular framework to act on this conviction — but having some answer to the question of why every student in front of you matters turns out to be one of the more important things a teacher carries into a room.

Contact theory in practice

Before designing a collaborative task, ask four questions. Are students meeting as equals, or does the structure advantage some over others? Are they working toward the same goal, or competing? Does the task genuinely require cooperation, or could one person carry it? And does the classroom culture — visibly, consistently — treat cross-group collaboration as normal and valued? If the answer to any of these is no, the contact may not do what you hope.

Retrieval practiceA teacher puts two rival friend groups together and says: “Make a poster about our school.” Using Allport’s four conditions, evaluate this plan. What is present and what is missing?►

What’s potentially present: Common goal (one poster, one outcome) — though only weakly, since any single person could make the poster alone. Institutional support may be implicit if the teacher’s authority is respected.

What’s missing: Equal status isn’t guaranteed — if one group has stronger visual skills, prior history, or social dominance, they’ll take over and the contact will replicate the existing hierarchy. Genuine cooperation is absent — the task doesn’t require interdependence; one person could produce the poster and the rest could observe. The Robbers Cave research is clear that this kind of proximity without genuine interdependence does little — and can actually increase tension by forcing students who don’t get along into close contact without a compelling reason to cooperate.

What would work better: Redesign so each student holds a piece the group genuinely cannot succeed without — different students responsible for different sections that only combine into the final product with all contributions. Make the task impossible to complete without every voice. That’s the structure that produces Sherif’s result.

Major concept 5 · Norms and Community

The Tradeoff Between Norms and Belonging

There is a version of the inclusion argument that goes like this: the way to make everyone feel welcome is to remove the norms that exclude them. Lower the expectations. Soften the requirements. Make the culture flexible enough that no one feels like an outsider. This argument has intuitive appeal, and there are genuine cases where specific norms are exclusionary in ways that should be challenged. But as a general principle, it gets the relationship between norms and community exactly backwards. Communities are not built by removing norms. They are built one norm at a time.

This is what Durkheim was pointing at with the concept of anomie. A community without shared norms is not an inclusive community — it is a collection of individuals with no shared identity, no shared expectations, and no shared investment in each other’s behavior. The norms are not incidental to the community. They are constitutive of it. Remove the norms and you do not get a more welcoming version of the community. You get a different thing entirely — something thinner, less coherent, and ultimately less capable of offering genuine belonging to anyone.

The tradeoff is real and worth naming honestly: norm-rich communities offer stronger belonging at the cost of some exclusion. Norm-poor communities are more accessible but offer weaker belonging. Neither extreme is obviously right. A school with no shared expectations is not a community — it is a building. A school with rigid, punitive norms that leave no room for individual difference is not a community either — it is a compliance environment. The goal is a norm structure that is clear, meaningful, consistently upheld, and genuinely connected to the community’s purpose.

This also means that the work of inclusion is not primarily about removing norms — it is about ensuring that the norms you have serve the community’s actual purpose rather than the comfort of whoever happens to be in the majority. A classroom norm that “students don’t interrupt each other” serves everyone. A norm that “we line up for lunch in alphabetical order by last name” inadvertently advantages one group in a way that may feel unimportant to some but very important to someone with a last name that starts with W. The first kind of norm builds community. The second kind mistakes a cultural preference for a universal expectation.

Expanding the circle does not mean emptying the circle of content. It means being honest about which norms are load-bearing — essential to the community’s purpose and available to everyone who commits to them — and which are merely familiar, encoding one group’s habits as everyone’s standard. The first category should be held firmly. The second should be examined and, where necessary, released.

For educators

When you design your classroom norms, you are not just setting rules — you are making a claim about what kind of community this is and what membership in it means. Norms that are arbitrary, inconsistently enforced, or disconnected from any larger purpose produce resentment and anomie. Norms that are meaningful, clearly explained, and visibly connected to the community’s shared values produce investment. The question to ask about any classroom norm is not just “does this maintain order?” but “does this build the kind of community I am trying to build, and can I explain why it matters to the students who will be asked to live by it?”

Retrieval practiceTwo classrooms each claim to “value inclusion.” Classroom A removes most behavioral norms to avoid excluding anyone. Classroom B maintains firm norms but explains why each one connects to the class’s shared purpose. Using Durkheim, predict which classroom is more likely to produce genuine belonging — and why.►

Durkheim would predict Classroom B — and the reasoning runs directly against the intuition behind Classroom A. Classroom A’s strategy (remove norms to increase access) produces anomie: without shared expectations, students lose the social ground that makes investment possible. The community has no substance to belong to. The “inclusion” on offer is the inclusion of a waiting room, not a community.

Classroom B’s strategy recognizes that norms are constitutive of community, not incidental to it. When those norms are explained and connected to shared purpose, they produce investment — students understand why the norms exist and can choose to buy into the community they create. This is closer to what genuine belonging requires: not the absence of expectations, but expectations that are meaningful enough to build something worth belonging to.

The crucial qualification: Classroom B’s norms must actually be load-bearing — serving the community’s purpose and available to everyone who commits to them. Norms that encode majority preferences as universal standards produce exclusion, not belonging. The goal is a norm structure that is both firm and genuinely inclusive of what it is trying to build.

Major concept 6 · Building Belonging in Practice

From Infrastructure to Daily Practice

Belonging is not a climate you set once at the start of the year and then maintain passively. It is a practice — a set of deliberate choices made daily, in small moments, that either accumulate into a felt sense of “I matter here” or quietly erode it. The research and frameworks in this chapter converge on two questions every teacher should be able to answer about their classroom: do students feel seen as individuals, and do they feel they matter to the group? Those are the two forms of belonging, and they require different kinds of attention.

Building egoistic belonging — being seen as an individual

The most foundational practice is name learning, and it matters more than most new teachers expect. A teacher who knows every student’s name by the end of the first week has communicated something before a single lesson has been taught: you are not interchangeable. You are here specifically. Learning names quickly is a skill that can be practiced — index cards, seating charts, name tents, making a point of using names every time you address a student. The investment is small. The signal is not.

Beyond names, egoistic belonging is built through noticing. Noticing when a usually talkative student goes quiet. Noticing when someone who was absent comes back. Noticing the small things students mention about their lives and remembering them later. None of this requires extensive time or a therapeutic skill set — it requires the habit of paying attention and the follow-through of acting on what you see. A thirty-second check-in at the door, a brief note on a returned assignment, a question that references something a student said two weeks ago — these are the accumulated evidence that this teacher sees this student as a person.

The physical environment also sends signals about who belongs before anyone speaks. Whose work is on the walls? Whose names appear in the examples used in class? Whose cultural references show up in the material? A student who never sees anyone like themselves in the room’s visual culture receives a quiet but consistent message about whose space this actually is.

Building altruistic belonging — from consumption to contribution

Altruistic belonging requires that students genuinely need each other — not in theory, but in the structure of the work. Tasks where one student can carry the group, where roles are cosmetic rather than essential, or where the final product could have been produced by anyone working alone do not build altruistic belonging. Tasks where each person holds a piece that the group cannot succeed without do. The design question to ask before any collaborative structure is: if this student were absent, would the group’s work be genuinely diminished? If the answer is no, the interdependence is not real.

When students lean too far into egoistic belonging, they can develop a consumption mindset: I need people to like me so I can derive benefit from my association with them. This ultimately limits their ability to experience belonging in its fullest sense. Paradoxically, the best way to feel that you belong is to ask “what can I offer the group?” rather than “what can the group give me?”

Classroom rituals also build altruistic belonging in ways that are easy to underestimate. A consistent opening routine, a shared vocabulary, an inside reference that belongs to this particular group of people — these create the felt sense of “we” that is distinct from the felt sense of “I.” The research on group synchrony covered in Chapter 17 is directly relevant here: shared rhythm, shared ritual, and shared experience produce neurochemical effects that individual experience does not. A class that begins the same way every day, that celebrates milestones together, that has its own history and its own language, is building something a class without those things is not.

Finally, belonging requires that mistakes be survivable. A classroom where errors are met with visible disappointment, public correction, or the quiet withdrawal of the teacher’s regard is a classroom where students learn to protect themselves rather than take risks. The norm around mistakes — be they academic, emotional, or behavioral — is one of the most powerful signals a classroom sends about whether it is safe to be genuinely present. Get that norm right and a great deal else follows.

A simple audit

At the end of any given week, ask yourself two questions: Did students connect with me or with each other as individuals this week? And did students engage in a common project this week that required genuine contribution from each person? The first question tracks egoistic belonging. The second tracks altruistic. If either answer is consistently no, you know where to invest.

Retrieval practiceDistinguish between egoistic and altruistic belonging. Then give one specific classroom practice that builds each — and one common mistake teachers make that undermines each.►

Egoistic belonging is the sense that you are seen and valued as an individual — that your specific presence matters. Altruistic belonging is the sense that you are part of something larger than yourself — that the group genuinely depends on your contribution.

Building egoistic belonging: Learn and use every student’s name within the first week. The mistake that undermines it: treating students as a category (“the quiet ones,” “the back row”) rather than as individuals with specific histories, interests, and ways of engaging.

Building altruistic belonging: Design tasks with genuine interdependence — where if any one student is absent, the group’s work is measurably weaker. The mistake that undermines it: group work where one person can carry the group, which rewards consumption (I benefit from being associated with capable people) rather than contribution (I offer something the group cannot succeed without). Students know when their presence is optional. And optional presence is not belonging.

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

Belonging is infrastructure — without it, everything else sits on sand. These are the practices that build it deliberately rather than accidentally.

  • Learn every student’s name in the first week. Use it every time you address them. This is the single most foundational egoistic belonging practice — small cost, large signal.
  • Design at least one significant task per unit with genuine interdependence — where the group cannot succeed without each person’s specific contribution. Ask before assigning: if this student were absent, would the work be genuinely weaker?
  • Before assigning cross-group collaborative work, check Allport’s four conditions: equal status, common goals, genuine cooperation, institutional support. If any are missing, the contact may not do what you hope — and may increase tension.
  • Establish and consistently enforce a small number of load-bearing norms. Be able to explain why each one connects to the community’s shared purpose. Inconsistently applied norms produce anomie; meaningful norms produce investment.
  • Audit your physical environment: whose work is on the walls? Whose examples appear in your material? These are belonging signals that operate before anyone speaks.
  • Design a shared classroom identity: a name, a set of values, a consistent opening routine, shared rituals. Give students a mission-aligned tribe worth being loyal to — before the tribal instinct builds one you didn’t design.
  • Establish a visible norm that mistakes are survivable — that effort and revision, not performance and protection, are what the classroom rewards. A student who learns to hide errors has learned something, but not what you intended.
Chapter synthesis
Every classroom is either building belonging or eroding it — there is no neutral ground. Belonging is not a climate feature that some students need and others do not; it is infrastructure, and without it, everything else the teacher builds sits on sand. The frameworks in this chapter — Durkheim’s integration and regulation, Allport’s contact theory, Sherif’s superordinate goals — give practical handles on something that can otherwise feel like a soft or intuitive domain. Tribalism is not an obstacle to community; it is the raw material. The question is what kind of tribe you design — and whether every student finds themselves inside the circle.
Belonging is not a preference — it’s a prerequisite. Chronic exclusion activates the same pathways as physical pain and measurably degrades cognitive performance. It is infrastructure, not sentiment.
Durkheim’s insight: community requires both integration (connectedness) and regulation (norms). Too little of either produces anomie; the result is not freedom but anxiety and disengagement.
Tribalism is inevitable — the question is not whether students will form tribes but whether the tribes in your room are ones you designed or ones that designed themselves. Mission-aligned tribes redirect the instinct toward something worth being loyal to.
Contact theory: proximity alone doesn’t reduce prejudice. Equal status, common goals, genuine cooperation, and institutional support all matter. Superordinate goals — problems neither group can solve alone — are the most reliable tool for turning rivals into collaborators.
Expanding the circle doesn’t mean emptying it of norms. Communities are constituted by their norms. The goal is norms that are load-bearing — essential to shared purpose and available to everyone who commits to them — rather than merely familiar.
Egoistic belonging (I am seen) and altruistic belonging (I matter to the group) both require deliberate design. The first is built through noticing; the second through genuine interdependence. Ask both questions weekly.
