Unit 3 · Motivational Psychology

Intrinsic Motivation

Lighting the Fire

Chapter 20~ 45 min read

Marcus hasn’t turned in a math assignment in three weeks. His teacher has tried the tools from Chapter 19: points, a call home, lunch detention. Nothing has worked.

On Monday, Marcus shows up with a notebook full of diagrams. Over the weekend, he taught himself to build a working calculator inside Minecraft, using online tutorials and a lot of trial and error. It took him eleven hours. Nobody assigned it. Nobody graded it. And now he wants to show you how it works.

So is Marcus unmotivated?

Rewards and consequences couldn’t get him through a worksheet. Something else got him through eleven hours of harder work. This chapter is about that something else: where it comes from, why it’s so hard to create on purpose, and how easily school puts it out.

The big picture

Chapter 19 was about motivation that comes from outside: rewards, consequences, and routines. This chapter is about motivation that comes from inside, the kind that keeps a student working long after anyone stops checking. For about a century, psychologists have tried to explain it, and each theory got a little closer. We’ll follow that story from drives to Maslow to Self-Determination Theory, testing each one against Marcus.

The big lesson is a humbling one: teachers can’t install motivation in a student. What we can do is stop putting it out, and build the conditions where it’s most likely to grow. By the end, we’ll come back to Chapter 19 and make the case that good teachers need both.

Major concept 1 · The fire, not the pail

Intrinsic Motivation

Extrinsic motivation asks, “What do I get for doing this?” Intrinsic motivation asks a different question: “Do I want to do this?” The first is a transaction. The second is about curiosity, identity, and meaning. When students are intrinsically motivated, their learning tends to go deeper, last longer, and keep going after the bell rings. That’s what Marcus had on Saturday.

Go deeperThe quote that’s on every poster►

“Education is not the filling of a pail, but the lighting of a fire.” It’s on posters, mugs, and graduation speeches, usually credited to the poet W. B. Yeats. There’s no evidence Yeats ever said it. The idea traces back almost two thousand years to the Greek writer Plutarch, who wrote that the mind is not a container to be filled but wood that needs kindling. The quote keeps spreading, with the wrong name attached, because people recognize it as true. Every teacher has seen the difference between a student filling a pail and a student on fire.

Intrinsic motivation isn’t the same as having fun. Marcus’s weekend wasn’t all fun. His calculator broke over and over, and he spent hours figuring out why. Intrinsically motivated people push through frustrating, tedious work because it matters to them. At its core, intrinsic motivation is ownership: the feeling that I’m doing this because it connects to something in me, not because someone is watching. When that ownership meets the right level of challenge, people can fall into what psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi called flow, so absorbed that eleven hours feel like two.

Here’s the hard news for teachers: you can’t install intrinsic motivation in someone else. You can’t make Marcus care about math. What you can do is create conditions where caring is more likely to grow, and stop doing the things that snuff it out. Every theory in the rest of this chapter is an attempt to describe those conditions.

Deci and Ryan found that motivation isn’t an on/off switch. It runs along a continuum. At one end, you do something only for a reward or to avoid trouble. At the other, you do it because it’s part of who you are. In between is what they called identified regulation: “I don’t love this, but I understand why it matters, so I choose to do it.” That middle ground matters for teachers. Not every student will love every subject, and that’s okay. Helping students reach “I see why this matters” is a realistic and worthwhile goal, and much more achievable than making everyone love fractions.

Retrieval practiceA student tells you, “I used to love science until we started doing it for grades.” Using the motivation continuum, describe what happened. What could a teacher do to help reverse it?►

The student slid from intrinsic motivation toward the external end of the continuum. Grades became the reason for doing science, crowding out the original interest. (Concept 4 calls this the overjustification effect.)

Removing grades usually isn’t possible, so the goal is to help the student reach identified regulation: seeing why the learning matters apart from the grade. The teacher can help the student connect topics to things they care about, create low-stakes chances to explore just out of curiosity, give feedback about how the student’s thinking is growing instead of only scores, and offer real choices within assignments. The interest was there before the grades. The job is to make it visible again.

Major concept 2 · Start with hunger, but don’t stop there.

Drive Reduction Theory

One of psychology’s first big explanations of motivation was drive reduction theory, developed most fully by Clark Hull in the 1940s. The idea grew out of behaviorism and early biology: our bodies have needs, those needs create uncomfortable tension called drives, and we act to make the tension go away. Hunger is a drive, so we eat. Thirst is a drive, so we drink. The goal, on this view, is to get back to a calm, balanced state and stay there.

Drive reduction explains a lot of survival behavior, and it gets something right: discomfort really does push us to act. A student who skipped breakfast or slept three hours is fighting a drive all morning, and learning loses that fight.

But try it on Marcus. He wasn’t hungry, cold, or uncomfortable on Saturday. If anything, he made himself more uncomfortable, choosing a frustrating problem when he could have relaxed. Drive reduction can’t explain that. It also can’t explain why kids play, why people watch scary movies, why scientists stay up late on unsolved problems, or why boredom feels bad instead of peaceful. All of those increase stimulation instead of reducing it.

Those gaps pushed psychologists toward better theories. One was optimal arousal theory, the idea that we seek a moderate level of stimulation, not zero. Others were Maslow’s hierarchy and, eventually, Self-Determination Theory. The lesson from drive reduction is still useful: meeting basic needs is the floor, not the ceiling. It removes barriers to learning, but it doesn’t create the pull Marcus felt.

Retrieval practiceGive two examples of student behavior that drive reduction explains well and two it can’t explain. What does that limitation tell teachers?►

Explains well: a hungry student who can’t focus before lunch, and an exhausted student who keeps drifting off. In both, a body need is competing for attention.

Can’t explain: a student who keeps working on a project long after class ends, or one who reads ahead because she wants to know what happens. Those students are seeking out challenge, not relief.

The lesson is that meeting basic needs matters, but it’s only the starting point. What drives students to do their best work is engagement, growth, and connection, which drive reduction doesn’t account for at all.

Major concept 3 · The Pyramid You’ve Seen—and Its Complications

Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs

In 1943, Abraham Maslow offered a bigger picture of motivation. He proposed that people have a hierarchy of needs, and that lower needs have to be reasonably met before higher ones take over. A hungry person isn’t thinking about personal growth. A person who doesn’t feel safe can’t focus on belonging. It’s one of the best-known ideas in psychology, and you’ve probably seen it as a pyramid on a classroom wall. From the top down:

Self-actualization
Becoming the fullest version of yourself: growth, creativity, and meaning. Maslow saw it as a direction, not a finish line. In school, it’s the long-range goal. We’re not just filling students with content; we’re helping them grow into who they can become.
Esteem
Confidence in your own abilities, and respect from others. Students who feel constantly disrespected or told they’re behind have a hard time reaching for anything higher.
Belonging
Friendship, acceptance, and feeling part of a group. For teenagers especially, belonging with peers is one of the strongest forces at school. (Chapter 18 covers belonging in depth.)
Safety
Physical and emotional safety, stability, and predictability. Students dealing with bullying, an unstable home, or violence in their neighborhood struggle to focus on learning. Research on Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) connects directly to this level.
Physical needs
Food, water, sleep, warmth, and shelter. A student who came to school hungry, cold, or exhausted is struggling at the most basic level, and no lesson plan can fully make up for that.

Maslow gets closer to Marcus than drive reduction does. Marcus’s basic needs were met. Wanting to show you his calculator hints at esteem and belonging. And eleven hours on a self-chosen challenge sounds a lot like self-actualization. But Maslow can’t answer the question that matters most for a teacher: why did that project light Marcus up when math class didn’t? His needs were met in both places.

Maslow’s model has also been seriously criticized. Research doesn’t support a strict ladder where each level must be filled before the next one switches on. People seek meaning and connection even in desperate circumstances, and creativity and love show up in poverty, prison, and war. It’s better to think of the levels as needs that are always present and interact, with some becoming more urgent depending on what’s happening in a person’s life.

A sixth level

In 1969, the year before he died, Maslow described a level beyond self-actualization: self-transcendence. Self-actualization is about becoming your best self. Self-transcendence moves past the self entirely, toward serving others, a cause, or God. Maslow noticed that the healthiest people he studied weren’t focused mainly on their own growth. They were focused on contributing. Most posters still show five levels, but the sixth matters for teachers. It suggests that the highest motivation may be giving yourself to something bigger than personal fulfillment.

For Latter-day Saint readers, this echoes the Savior’s teaching that “whosoever will lose his life for my sake shall find it” (Matthew 16:25), and King Benjamin’s reminder that “when ye are in the service of your fellow beings ye are only in the service of your God” (Mosiah 2:17). It’s also what the best teachers do every day.

Common misconception“I can’t change what’s happening at home, so I can’t really reach this student.”►

This reads Maslow too literally. The hierarchy describes what tends to take priority under different conditions. It never meant teachers are powerless when a student’s needs aren’t met at home.

The research points the other way. Harvard’s Center on the Developing Child reports that, across many kinds of hardship, “the single most common finding is that children who end up doing well have had at least one stable and committed relationship with a supportive parent, caregiver, or other adult.” Teachers can be that adult. You can’t fix food insecurity or a chaotic home, but you can offer safety, belonging, and respect in your room, and those matter even when they’re missing elsewhere. Knowing a student faces hardship is a reason to invest more in the relationship, not less.

Retrieval practiceA student who is usually curious and engaged suddenly becomes withdrawn, turns in incomplete work twice, and seems distracted in discussions. Using Maslow’s hierarchy, describe two possible explanations at different levels and the teacher response each one suggests.►

Safety: something at home or school may have become threatening or unstable, such as a family crisis or bullying. When safety is threatened, the brain focuses on the threat instead of learning. A private, low-pressure check-in (“You seem a little off lately. Is there anything I can do?”), a connection to the school counselor if needed, and easing academic pressure for a while make sense.

Belonging: a friendship may have ended or the student may feel left out. Then the teacher can rebuild connection through personal check-ins, group work where the student is truly needed, and small chances for social success.

Either way, the withdrawal is a signal, not laziness, and the response is relationship first, curriculum second.

Major concept 4 · Autonomy, Mastery, Purpose—and Daniel Pink’s Drive

Self-Determination Theory

The best-supported explanation of intrinsic motivation comes from Edward Deci and Richard Ryan’s decades of work on Self-Determination Theory (SDT). Their main finding is that people have three basic psychological needs. When those needs are met, motivation, well-being, and learning grow. When they’re blocked, motivation withers, no matter what rewards are offered.

In his book Drive, Daniel Pink turned this research into a framework for workplaces and schools: autonomy, mastery, and purpose. He mostly followed SDT, but swapped its third need, relatedness (feeling connected to people), for purpose (feeling connected to something bigger than yourself). Both belong in a classroom.

Now look at Marcus’s weekend again. Every need was met.

Autonomy. Marcus chose the project, decided how to tackle it, and worked on his own schedule. Pink describes autonomy as control over four T’s: task, time, technique, and team. Autonomy doesn’t mean students do whatever they want. It means they feel like the authors of their own work. Even required work can feel that way when students understand and agree with why it matters. Autonomy-supportive teachers explain their reasons, offer real options where they can, and listen to students’ views even when the answer is still no. Micromanagement drains motivation, however kindly it’s delivered.

Competence (mastery). The calculator was hard, but not impossible. Every time Marcus fixed a bug, the game showed him immediately that it worked. That’s the right level of challenge plus instant, honest feedback, which is exactly what the need for competence runs on. Pink points out that mastery never fully arrives; you can always get better, and that’s part of the pull. Tasks that are too easy bore students, and tasks that are too hard make them anxious. Both block this need. A growth mindset helps here, but the bigger levers are the challenge level and the feedback.

Relatedness and purpose. Marcus learned from people in online tutorials, and on Monday the first thing he wanted was to show someone. Feeling connected to others, and feeling that your work matters to someone, keeps motivation going. “Because it will be on the test” isn’t a purpose. Connecting learning to students’ own questions, lives, and problems they care about is.

So the problem with Marcus’s math class probably isn’t Marcus. The worksheet gave him no choice, no visible progress, and no one to show. It met none of his needs, and the points and detentions couldn’t make up for that.

SDT also explains when rewards backfire. Rewards that feel controlling (“Do this and you’ll get that”) undercut autonomy. Feedback that’s informational (“Here’s what’s working and what to try next”) supports competence without taking ownership away. The clearest example is the overjustification effect: a promised reward for something a person already enjoys can make them enjoy it less.

ResearchThe drawing study

In a classic 1973 study, Mark Lepper, David Greene, and Richard Nisbett found preschoolers who already liked drawing with markers. Some were told ahead of time they’d earn a “Good Player” certificate for drawing. Some got the same certificate as a surprise afterward. Some got nothing. A week or two later, during free play, the children who had expected the certificate drew about half as much as the other two groups. The surprise group didn’t lose interest. The reward itself wasn’t the problem. Drawing for the reward was.

Later research filled in the conditions. Chapter 19 (Concept 4) covers when rewards are safe: when interest is low, when the reward is a surprise, and when it’s praise or feedback rather than a prize. The risk is highest when a tangible reward is promised for something students already enjoy.

1Findings

The drawing study above is a clear example of a reward changing later free-choice interest in one already-enjoyed activity. It does not answer every question about performance, learning, or the effects of every kind of reward.

Meta-analyses interpret the wider research differently. Deci, Koestner, and Ryan reviewed 128 studies and found that some expected, tangible, or contingent rewards reduced free-choice motivation, while positive feedback tended to increase it. Cameron and Pierce’s analysis of 96 experiments concluded that rewards did not generally decrease intrinsic motivation, with a small negative effect under particular conditions. A later meta-analysis of 60 studies (69 samples) on creativity found that creativity-contingent rewards tended to support creative performance; completion- or performance-contingent rewards had a small negative effect.

2Evidence and limits

Evidence strength: moderate and context-dependent. The findings vary with the reward, task, choice, feedback, and outcome measured. Free-choice interest after a reward ends is different from immediate performance while a reward is available. The broader evidence does not support a simple rule that rewards always harm complex work.

3Classroom application

Ask what outcome a reward is meant to change: effort now, quality, creativity, or willingness to choose the task later. For activities students already enjoy, use informative feedback and preserve meaningful choice. When using rewards for routine tasks, notice what happens when the reward ends.

ResearchThe candle problem

In a classic puzzle first created by Karl Duncker, people get a candle, a box of thumbtacks, and matches, and are asked to attach the candle to a wall so the wax won’t drip on the table.

The trick is to see the tack box as a shelf: empty it, tack it to the wall, and set the candle in it.

In 1962, Sam Glucksberg offered some people cash for solving it quickly. When the tacks came inside the box, the paid group took longer. The reward seemed to narrow their thinking, and they had trouble seeing the box as anything but a container. But when the tacks were placed beside the empty box, making the solution obvious, the paid group tended to be faster. Daniel Pink made this study famous, and the full result is the useful part: rewards can help with simple, straightforward work, but they can get in the way of work that takes creativity and judgment. It is one small study from 1962, so treat it as an illustration, not proof.

Go deeperWhat the intrinsic motivation story misses►

Chapter 19 listed what behaviorism misses. Intrinsic motivation deserves the same honesty. The research in this chapter is strong, but it’s easy to oversell.

  • Interest often comes after competence, not before. Many students start to like a subject once they get good at it. In a study that followed 1,478 Canadian children from first through fourth grade, math achievement predicted later enjoyment of math, but enjoyment didn’t predict later achievement. Some practice, like math facts, spelling, or scales on the piano, has to happen before the enjoyment arrives. Waiting for students to feel motivated before asking them to practice can mean waiting forever.
  • “Make it engaging” can become an excuse. Teachers sometimes swap real content for fun activities, or blame themselves when a hard task isn’t exciting. Hard and worthwhile is a fine combination. Not everything has to be fun.
  • Choice helps less than people think. Research on student choice shows real but modest benefits, and the popular claim that too many options paralyze people is weaker than it sounds. Chapter 16 (Concept 6) covers what makes choice work.
  • Popular books simplify. Daniel Pink’s Drive is popular writing, not a research study, and the candle problem is one classic experiment, not proof that rewards always backfire.
  • Much of the evidence shows connection, not cause. Many SDT studies show that autonomy, competence, and relatedness go along with motivation. That’s not the same as proving that meeting them always causes it, though experiments do support the main ideas.

The point isn’t that intrinsic motivation is overrated. Teachers need both chapters: structure, practice, and sometimes rewards to get students going, and autonomy, competence, and connection to keep them going.

Retrieval practiceA student is working on a challenging creative writing project. Describe one teacher action that would support each of the three needs and one that would undermine each.►

Autonomy. Support: offer a real choice of format, audience, or topic within a clear framework. Undermine: dictate every detail and check in so often the student feels untrusted. (Models and templates aren’t the problem by themselves; Chapter 16, Concept 6, shows structure helps when it guides instead of controls.)

Competence. Support: give specific feedback on a draft about what’s working and one thing to develop next. Undermine: return the draft with only a grade, or assign a task so far beyond the student’s skill that effort can’t get them there.

Relatedness and purpose. Support: ask what the student wants the piece to say, and connect craft choices to that goal; share the work with a real audience. Undermine: frame the project only as “20% of your grade, scored on the rubric.”

Major concept 5 · What Teachers Can Actually Do

Supporting Student Motivation

Marcus’s teacher can’t hand him a Minecraft project every day, and she still has standards to teach. So what can she actually change? The research points to a clear order: first, stop doing harm. Then, build.

First, do no harm

Before asking “How do I motivate my students?” ask a harder question: “Am I demotivating them?” Sticker charts for reading, public leaderboards, grading curves, and reward systems that signal “this is the kind of thing you have to be paid to do” can quietly wear down motivation students already had. The drawing study in Concept 4 showed how promised rewards can backfire on things students already enjoy. That doesn’t make every reward a mistake. When interest is low, a reward tied to a step students can control can help. In one large set of trials, paying second graders for each book they read and passed a quiz on led to gains for some students (Fryer, 2011; see Chapter 21). The risk is greatest for students who already care. Remove the damage first.

Key aphorism

First, do no (motivational) harm.

Before adding anything to motivate students, find what in your classroom is getting in the way.

Medicine has a version of this rule because the body does much of its own healing, and a doctor’s first job is not to get in the way. Classrooms work the same way more often than we think. Most students arrive with some curiosity already running.

About the Aphorism

Where it comes from: “First, do no harm” is usually credited to the Hippocratic Oath, but those words aren’t in it. The Oath does promise to keep patients from harm. The closest wording is in another Hippocratic text, Epidemics, which tells physicians “to help, or at least to do no harm.” The Latin version, primum non nocere, shows up much later, in nineteenth-century medical writing.

What it means here: a doctor’s first duty is to avoid making a patient worse. A teacher’s first motivational duty is to avoid making students care less. That means looking hard at the things that quietly signal “this is the kind of thing you have to be paid to do”: promised prizes for reading when students already love to read, public rankings, constant surveillance, and work that offers no choice, no visible progress, and no reason.

Why it’s such a big win: it’s the cheapest thing in the chapter, and it helps every student at once. You don’t have to invent a new incentive or redesign a unit. You stop doing a few things. And the stakes are real. In a study of nearly 800 students, Mark Lepper, Jennifer Corpus, and Sheena Iyengar found that intrinsic motivation declined steadily from third grade through eighth. (The study compared different students at different ages, so it can’t show exactly what caused the drop.) Every classroom that stops wearing curiosity down protects something students arrived with and can’t easily get back.

Where it stops: “do no harm” isn’t “do nothing.” Doctors still treat patients, and teachers still need the structure, routines, and reinforcement from Chapter 19. The rule is about the order: check for harm first, then build.

Then, build

The practices below fall into four groups. Knowing why each one works will help you adapt it to your own students instead of following it like a recipe.

Protect the motivation students bring.

  • Trade controlling rewards for informational feedback. “Here’s how your thinking is growing” supports competence. “Good job, here’s a sticker” turns attention to the sticker. The difference isn’t whether the message is positive. It’s whether it’s about the student’s growth or your approval.
  • Use rewards and competition as scaffolding, and plan to take them down. Points and prizes can help early, before students have built their own skill or reasons to care. The mistake is leaving them up forever. If a class runs on the same reward system in May as in September, something was missed.
  • Give real choices. Even small, real choices help: the order of assignments, the format of a product, who to work with. Make the choices matter, not just cosmetic. Chapter 16 (Concept 6) shows what makes choice work: a few real options, with enough structure to choose well.

Help students find the why.

  • Give an honest reason for required work, and let students find their own. Even mandatory work can feel self-chosen when students understand and agree with the reason. “Because I said so” gets compliance at best. Better still, ask students to work out how the material connects to their own lives; the research below shows why that matters.
  • Connect content to students’ own questions and lives. Purpose is the feeling that this learning connects to something I care about. Learning what students are curious about and building bridges to the curriculum isn’t just relationship-building. It’s motivation work.
  • Explain the reasons behind class rules. Rules that feel arbitrary undermine autonomy. Rules that clearly serve values students share, like fairness, safety, and learning, can feel acceptable even when they aren’t up for debate.
ResearchLet students find the relevance

In a 2009 experiment, Chris Hulleman and Judith Harackiewicz had ninth-grade science students write short essays during the year. Some summarized what they were learning. Others wrote about how it connected to their own lives. Students who started the year doubting they could do well in science and wrote about its relevance ended up more interested in science and earned better grades than similar students who wrote summaries. A later study found an important twist: simply telling students why the material was useful backfired for students who lacked confidence, while having them work out the connection themselves helped. So the most useful version of “explain the why” is to ask students to find it.

Build relationships and belonging.

  • Learn students’ names, interests, and strengths early, and use them. Relatedness grows through many small signs that the teacher sees each student as a person. Students who feel seen are more likely to keep going when the work gets hard.
  • Design group work that builds real connection. Splitting a task into parts and stapling them together doesn’t build belonging. Structures where students share their thinking, respond to each other, and build understanding together do.

Move from outside control to inside values.

  • Appeal to identity and values, not just compliance. The deepest motivation comes when students see a behavior as part of who they are: curious learners, people who do good work, members of a learning community. This is slower than a reward system, and harder to see working, but it lasts.
  • Hand responsibility back as students grow. Detailed rubrics, frequent reminders, and step-by-step scaffolds are often needed early. But structure that helped in week two can become a ceiling by week twelve. Watch for signs that students are building their own reasons to engage, and loosen the scaffolding when they do.
Key aphorism

“The greatest sign of success for a teacher … is to be able to say, ‘The children are now working as if I did not exist.’”

Maria Montessori, The Absorbent Mind (1949), chapter 27.

A gospel lens: agents who act

Before he became an apostle, Elder David A. Bednar spent his career studying motivation at work. He earned a PhD in organizational behavior from Purdue, taught management at Texas Tech and the University of Arkansas, and then served as president of Ricks College as it became BYU–Idaho.

In a 2006 address to religious educators, “Seek Learning by Faith,” he drew on 2 Nephi 2 to describe learners as agents “to act and not to be acted upon.” He noted that the Spirit helped Nephi “act in the learning process and not simply be acted upon,” and asked teachers a pointed question: “Are the children, youth, and adults we serve acting and seeking to learn by faith, or are they waiting to be taught and acted upon?”

That is autonomy in the language of the gospel. A teacher can’t do a student’s learning for them, any more than a teacher can make them care. The work is to build a classroom where students act.

Picture what this could mean for Marcus. His teacher asks him where the logic inside his calculator shows up in the day’s lesson. She lets him choose between two problem sets or show his reasoning in his own way. She gives him quick, specific feedback so he can see himself improving, the way the game did. And she asks him to explain a solution to the class. None of it requires a Minecraft lesson. All of it meets the needs his weekend project met. And the Chapter 19 tools weren’t wrong, just limited. Points might still help Marcus push through math-fact practice he doesn’t enjoy. They were never going to produce a Saturday like that one.

Both, not either

Read together, Chapters 19 and 20 can sound like a debate: rewards versus real motivation, structure versus freedom, Skinner versus Deci and Ryan. In real classrooms, it isn’t a debate. The best teachers use both, and they learn to tell which one a moment calls for.

If anything, new teachers today need a push to take behaviorism seriously. You’ll hear in classes, online, and in faculty rooms that rewards are bribes, that consequences do harm, and that a good enough lesson makes classroom management unnecessary. The research doesn’t support that. Clear routines, consistent consequences, specific praise, and well-run reinforcement systems are some of the best-supported practices in education. The Good Behavior Game from Chapter 19 still showed effects when those first graders were adults. A classroom without that structure isn’t a place where intrinsic motivation blooms. It’s a place where no one can concentrate long enough to find out what they love.

Think of it as an order of operations. Structure comes first. Routines, clear expectations, and reinforcement give students the safety and predictability that every theory in this chapter assumes. Then, inside that structure, autonomy, competence, and connection have room to grow. As students build their own reasons to engage, the outside supports come down a little at a time. Behaviorism builds the house. Intrinsic motivation is what makes students want to live in it.

Marcus needs both. He needs a teacher who runs a calm, predictable room, notices and reinforces the day he finally turns something in, and doesn’t let him skip the practice. And he needs a teacher who helps him find a reason, gives him a choice, sets a challenge that fits, and gives him someone to show. Either one alone will probably lose him. Together, they give him his best chance at a Saturday like that one, in math class.

Know the limits

This approach assumes students’ basic needs are at least partly met. Motivation science doesn’t override poverty, trauma, or chronic instability. When those are present, the most important help may not be in the curriculum at all. But as Concept 3 showed, a supportive relationship with a teacher is one of the strongest protections a child can have. Maslow’s hierarchy shows what to invest in. It doesn’t set a ceiling on what teachers can do.

Retrieval practiceA PE teacher starts giving prizes for miles run to students who already liked running. When the prizes end, those students run less than they did before the program. What effect explains this? What could the teacher have done instead?►

The overjustification effect. The students’ reason for running shifted from “I like it” to “I’m doing it for prizes,” so when the prizes ended, so did the reason.

Better options: skip prizes for something students already enjoy; if using rewards, make them surprises rather than promised in advance; or use specific feedback about progress (“You took fifteen seconds off your mile this week”), which builds a sense of competence instead of replacing their own reasons.

Retrieval practiceA teacher posts a weekly leaderboard showing which students have the most “participation points.” Analyze this using the “first, do no harm” principle, and suggest one alternative that targets the same goal with less risk.►

The leaderboard is a controlling reward that threatens several needs at once. Students at the top may keep participating, but now for the leaderboard instead of because they have something to say. Students in the middle and bottom get a weekly public message about where they rank, which tends to push them toward withdrawal, not participation. It hurts most the students it was meant to help.

A better option: give each student brief, private feedback on their own participation (“You asked two really thoughtful questions this week”). That supports competence without ranking anyone, and keeps participation about contributing to the class instead of performing for the teacher.

In the classroomWhat this chapter means for your practice

Intrinsic motivation can’t be installed, but it can be protected and grown, and it grows best inside a well-run classroom. Start with the structure from Chapter 19: routines, clear expectations, and consistent reinforcement. Then use these practices to build students’ own reasons to engage.

  • Check for demotivators first. Before adding new incentives, look at your current reward systems. Sticker charts for reading, public leaderboards, and grading curves all carry risk, especially for students who already care.
  • Help students find the why. Give a real reason for required work, and better yet, ask students to work out how it connects to their own lives.
  • Use informational feedback instead of controlling rewards. “Here’s how your thinking is growing, and one thing that would make it stronger” builds competence. “Good job, 4 out of 5 stars” mostly signals your approval.
  • Offer real choices. Task, time, technique, or team. Small real choices matter; cosmetic ones don’t.
  • Aim for the right level of challenge. Too easy bores students; too hard makes them anxious. Quick, honest feedback helps students see their own progress.
  • Don’t wait for motivation before practice. Interest often follows competence. Structured practice, with support, is how many students start to like a subject.
  • When a student seems unmotivated, check needs before blaming attitude. And remember that hardship at home doesn’t make you powerless. It makes the relationship more important.
  • Plan to take the scaffolding down. Structure that helped in week two can become a ceiling by week twelve.
Chapter synthesis
Marcus was never unmotivated. He was motivated by things his math class didn’t offer.
Intrinsic motivation is ownership, not just fun. It can’t be installed, only supported. Motivation runs along a continuum, and helping students reach “I see why this matters” is a realistic goal.
Drive reduction explains survival behavior but not curiosity, play, or meaning. Meeting basic needs is the floor, not the ceiling.
Maslow’s hierarchy works best as interacting needs, not a strict ladder. His late addition, self-transcendence, suggests the highest motivation is serving something bigger than yourself. A caring teacher can matter enormously even when needs aren’t met at home.
SDT’s three needs, autonomy, competence, and relatedness, predict when motivation grows or withers. Promised rewards for things students already enjoy can backfire; informational feedback supports motivation. Rewards can help with simple tasks but get in the way of creative ones.
The practical order is: first, stop demotivating; then build. Support autonomy through reasons and choices, competence through feedback and the right challenge, and relatedness through real connection. Plan for the scaffolding to come down.
Behaviorism and intrinsic motivation work together. Structure comes first and makes the room safe enough to learn in. Autonomy, competence, and connection grow inside it. Don’t let behaviorism’s bad reputation talk you out of the tools that make the rest possible.

Psychology’s century-long search for what moves people helps explain Marcus. Drive reduction explained hunger but not curiosity. Maslow showed that needs build on each other, but not why one task lights a student up and another doesn’t. Self-Determination Theory finally explained him: he had choice, a challenge that fit, visible progress, and someone to show.

This chapter doesn’t replace Chapter 19. It finishes it. Structure, routines, and reinforcement build the calm, predictable room that every theory here depends on, and intrinsic motivation grows best inside it. Good teachers don’t pick a side. They use both.

In the April 2022 general conference, Elder D. Todd Christofferson warned against a transactional view of faith: “We ought not to think of God’s plan as a cosmic vending machine where we (1) select a desired blessing, (2) insert the required sum of good works, and (3) the order is promptly delivered.” His point was that discipleship is a relationship of trust, not a transaction (“Our Relationship with God,” April 2022).

School can become a vending machine too: put in work, get out points. A student who sees it that way stops when the points stop. The fire this chapter is about belongs to students who see learning as part of who they are becoming, and that is the kind of student a good teacher, using everything in these two chapters, is trying to raise.
