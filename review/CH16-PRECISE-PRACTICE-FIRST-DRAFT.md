# EDCOR 310 · Chapter 16 Precise Practice pilot — first instructional draft

**Status:** For instructor/Taylor review; not a live textbook change. Based on the current `etb-live/chapter16.html`, **Agency and Self-Belief** (six concepts). No practice bank has yet been implemented. All sample questions below are **original draft items**. Study summaries derive from the chapter and need source-level verification before release.

## What we're building

**Student goal:** Make sound, humane, research-informed decisions about agency, attribution, learned control, self-efficacy, and autonomy support. Students should distinguish motivational slogans from evidence, diagnose realistic classroom cases, and justify a feasible intervention while acknowledging uncertainty and context.

**Four layers:**
1. **Foundations:** retrieval, A/A−/F definition discrimination, rapid sorts, identify the missing idea, and specific misconception correction.
2. **Application:** classroom cases, choose the best next move, predict what changes, evaluate a flawed teacher explanation.
3. **Exam-style check:** short, unfamiliar, multi-concept applied decisions; eventual target is a lightweight optional five-correct-in-a-row readiness *indicator*, not proof of mastery.
4. **Short advanced reasoning:** AAQ-inspired **Research Detective** (one study) and EBQ-inspired **Evidence-Based Decision** (two compact sources), with a classroom-decision variant. One prompt visible at a time. Student writes 2–4 sentences, reveals a **five-out-of-five exemplar**, and compares it against a five-point analytic checklist. No automatic essay grading.

**Time targets to test:** foundational moves often 10–30 seconds; applied cases ~30–90 seconds; each advanced response ~3–6 minutes, not AP's full-length timed essays. Avoid fixed completion gates and long text-entry requirements.

## Sample Tier 1 · Foundation: A/A−/F discrimination

**Concept 5: Self-efficacy**

Sort these three statements:

- **A:** “Self-efficacy is a person's belief that they can succeed at a particular task; genuine successful performance is an important source of that belief.”
- **A−:** “Self-efficacy is confidence in yourself.” *(Partly true but misses task specificity and the difference from global self-worth.)*
- **F:** “A teacher can reliably create self-efficacy simply by telling students they are talented, even when they never succeed.” *(Confuses verbal encouragement with evidence of competence.)*

**Feedback:** The central distinction is **specific, earned competence**, not global positivity. The interface should allow students to classify several statements at once and then explain the missing idea.

## Sample Tier 2 · Classroom application

**Concept 6: Autonomy support**

A teacher announces, “For your final project, choose any topic, any format, and any deadline within the next month.” Many students stall and ask what good work looks like. Which revision best preserves agency *and* makes success more likely?

A. Remove all choices and assign the same detailed project to everyone.  
B. Keep every choice but add a motivational speech about responsibility.  
C. Offer a few meaningful topic or format options, show quality criteria and examples, and add checkpoints with feedback. **[Best]**  
D. Let students vote on the poster color while keeping all substantive decisions fixed.

**Feedback:** C adds structure without eliminating meaningful choice. A may create clarity but unnecessarily removes autonomy; B adds reassurance without the needed support; D offers cosmetic rather than substantive choice.

## Sample Tier 3A · Research Detective (AAQ-inspired)

**Time target:** ~4 minutes. **One short research summary.** Drafted from the chapter's description of Kidd, Palmeri, & Aslin (2013); confirm against the original study before publication.

**Study card:** Researchers first gave 28 preschoolers experience with an adult who either fulfilled or broke a promise about better art supplies. The children were then offered one treat immediately or two after waiting. In the chapter's account, children in the reliable-adult condition waited about 12 minutes on average, compared with about 3 minutes in the unreliable-adult condition.

**Prompt (2–4 sentences):** What did the researchers vary? What does the difference in waiting suggest about interpreting children's behavior? Give one reason a teacher should be cautious about applying this result to every student.

**Five-point self-review criteria:**
1. Identifies prior adult reliability as the manipulated condition.
2. Accurately states the direction of the waiting-time difference.
3. Explains that expectations about whether a promise will be kept can affect apparently impulsive behavior.
4. Avoids claiming that the experiment proves willpower is unimportant or that all delay behavior is caused by trust.
5. Names a credible limitation (small sample, specific age/task, or generalization to other contexts).

**Illustrative five-out-of-five answer:**  
“The researchers varied whether the adult had previously kept a promise. Children who experienced a reliable adult waited much longer on average (about 12 versus 3 minutes), suggesting that waiting partly reflects whether children expect the delayed reward to arrive—not just an unchanging trait like willpower. Because the study involved only 28 preschoolers in one task, I wouldn't assume the same effect size applies to every classroom or age group.”

**Common almost-right answer:** “The children with good self-control waited longer.” This describes behavior but ignores the experimentally varied reliability cue and overattributes the result to a trait.

## Sample Tier 3B · Evidence-Based Decision (EBQ-inspired)

**Time target:** ~5–6 minutes. **Two short source cards.** Drafted from chapter summaries; verify original sources and quantitative details before publication.

**Source A — Forsyth et al. (2007):** After a poor first psychology exam, college students received review questions; some also received messages encouraging high self-esteem. In the small subgroup initially earning D/F grades, the chapter reports a decline in final-exam averages from roughly 57% to 38% among those receiving self-esteem messages. The subgroup was small, so the result should not be treated as a universal effect.

**Source B — Finn, Miele, & Wigfield (2024):** In a study involving 570 third- and sixth-graders, students worked through difficult math items, with some receiving additional easier items. The group with some easier items preferred the experience and was more willing to choose it again. This concerns willingness to reengage, not proof of long-term achievement gains.

**Prompt (3–4 sentences):** A teacher wants to help discouraged students and proposes replacing difficult practice with daily “You are brilliant!” messages. Recommend a better approach using evidence from **both** sources. Explain one limit of the evidence.

**Five-point self-review criteria:**
1. Makes a specific, defensible instructional recommendation.
2. Accurately uses Source A and avoids claiming that positive messages are always harmful.
3. Accurately uses Source B and distinguishes willingness to reengage from demonstrated learning.
4. Explains a psychological mechanism: genuine achievable success, feedback, or task-specific self-efficacy.
5. Acknowledges a meaningful limitation or context difference between studies and the proposed classroom.

**Illustrative five-out-of-five answer:**  
“I would keep challenging work but add supported on-ramps, some winnable problems, and feedback so students can see real progress. Forsyth's small study cautions against assuming that generic self-esteem messages improve grades; in one subgroup, outcomes were worse. Finn and colleagues found that adding easier items to difficult work made students more willing to return, which supports designing achievable success rather than simply telling students they are brilliant. Neither study alone proves that this exact routine will raise long-term achievement in my classroom, so I'd monitor both learning and engagement.”

**Common almost-right answer:** “Research proves praise is bad and easy questions are good.” This overstates both sources and misses the role of appropriate challenge.

## Sample Tier 3C · Classroom Judgment (same response engine, optional)

**Prompt (3–4 sentences):** A student says, “I'm terrible at math; I'll always fail.” Their teacher knows the student has experienced repeated failure and has also missed lessons because of family instability. Identify an unhelpful attribution, propose one instructional change that creates credible evidence of progress, and explain how to avoid blaming the student for circumstances outside their control.

**Five-point self-review criteria:** identifies a global/stable attribution; does not dismiss genuine constraints; offers a feasible scaffold or attainable milestone; connects progress/feedback to task-specific efficacy or perceived control; includes a humane, realistic next step.

**Illustrative five-out-of-five answer:**  
“‘I'll always fail’ treats a specific setback as permanent and global, but the student's missed instruction also reflects circumstances they didn't choose. I'd reteach the missing prerequisite, offer a few problems the student can solve with fading support, and show progress against a clear goal. That gives the student real evidence that their actions can work without pretending family instability is their fault. I'd coordinate additional support rather than relying on reassurance alone.”

## End-of-chapter UX proposal

- Keep the chapter's existing prose, retrieval prompts, and any authored end-of-chapter FRQ untouched for now.
- Put **one compact readiness panel** immediately before the existing chapter-end written practice: optional five-correct-in-a-row from reviewed advanced application questions, followed by a choice of **Research Detective** or **Evidence-Based Decision** (one visible at a time).
- Students write short responses and reveal five-point exemplars and common almost-right answers. No AI score, automated grade, or “mastered” badge.
- Later, after student testing, consider an optional “compare my answer” checklist with each criterion separately; do not require a lengthy essay or unverified automatic scoring.
- **Do not yet implement** cross-chapter banks, adaptive scheduling, persistent mastery tracking, or a student item-quality dashboard.
